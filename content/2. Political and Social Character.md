## Civilizational Nationalism

Priority is given to cultural cohesion, high-trust social conditions, national sovereignty, and strong borders. Immigration policy should be selective and oriented toward cultural compatibility. The state’s primary duties are to its own citizens and the long-term continuity of the political community.

## Economic Orientation 

Free-market leaning with a preference for low, simple taxation (including flat tax structures). Mandatory welfare and large-scale redistribution are viewed with skepticism (see [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance|Domestic Policy & Governance]] for the specific position). Subsidies require taxpayer opt-in, and industrial policy should be minimal and tightly justified.

## Cultural Stance

Preference for the nuclear family and traditional social patterns as the preferred default, without requiring legal mandates for private life. Strong opposition to identity politics and DEI frameworks. Free speech is treated as a near-absolutist value, with narrow exceptions for direct threats and incitement to imminent violence. Islamism and communism are condemned in the government's official voice, with no official platforms, public funding, or public honors, while the law restricts them only as it restricts any other ideology.

## Order and Security 

Hardline orientation on crime: expanded use of imprisonment, support for the death penalty in serious cases, and decisive action against cartels and chronic rights violators. Substantial state coercive power is accepted as necessary to suppress recurrent tendencies toward predation and disorder. Islamist and communist ideologies are treated as civilizational and security threats due to their systematic hostility to the Western rights-based framework; they are monitored only through public data and lawful process, with no legal penalty for belief.

## The Future and Technology 

Techno-optimist within the bounds of the prescribed sentientist constraints. Support for AI, genetic technologies, and related advances. Any emergent AI sentience is recognized under the ethical framework but remains subject to decisive termination if it violates or imminently threatens rights, posing a systemic threat to the rights-respecting order.

## Western Values and Compatibility

"Western" names a value structure rather than an ethnicity or a geography: individual negative rights, liberty, religious freedom with separation of church and state, the rule of law, civic responsibility, and economic opportunity. Compatibility means holding those values. It is judged at the country level through a presumption against admitting migrants from places where Islamist movements govern or where survey data show majority support for state-enforced religious law, and at the individual level through values vetting of every immigrant (see [[4. Religion & Secularism#Islam and Extremism|Religion & Secularism]] and [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Foreign Policy & International Relations]]). Compatibility judgments govern admission to the political community; they impose no legal penalty for belief on anyone already within it.

## Relation to Other Political Traditions

#### Position
This comparison is an interpretation of the framework's positions; the framework does not classify itself under any existing label. It agrees with classical liberalism and libertarianism on negative rights, private property, free markets, and a state limited to protecting individuals from force and fraud ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]; [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance|Domestic Policy & Governance]]). It departs from libertarianism in accepting substantial coercive power against chronic rights violators, including the mandatory death penalty ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Criminal Justice & Security]]), strict border control with lethal force against violent or armed crossings ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Foreign Policy & International Relations]]), and decisive force against organized threats. It agrees with national conservatism on assimilation, patriotism, national interest in trade, and skepticism of transnational institutions ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Patriotism and National Identity|Cultural & Social Issues]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Global Institutions|Foreign Policy & International Relations]]), but grounds the shared culture in a value structure open to people of any ethnicity rather than in religious or ancestral heritage, keeps church and state separate and taxes churches ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Religious Exemptions|Religion & Secularism]]), and recognizes same-sex marriage ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#LGBTQ+ and Identity Issues|Cultural & Social Issues]]). Its distinctive element, shared by none of the three, is sentientism: rights rest on sentience rather than species, extending protection to animals and to any truly sentient artificial being ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sentience Determination|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]).

> [!question]- "Isn't this just national conservatism with a libertarian economic policy?"
> No. National conservatism, in its own statement of principles, calls for public life rooted in Christianity where a Christian majority exists and for public support of the traditional family. The framework instead keeps church and state separate, taxes churches, and recognizes same-sex marriage. Its sentientist grounding of rights has no counterpart in any of the three traditions.

#### Key Elements
- Stated as an interpretation, not a self-classification.
- Agreement with classical liberalism and libertarianism: negative rights, property, markets, limited state.
- Departure from libertarianism: mandatory death penalty, strict borders, decisive force against organized threats.
- Agreement with national conservatism: assimilation, patriotism, national-interest trade, skepticism of transnational bodies.
- Departure from national conservatism: culture grounded in values rather than heritage, church-state separation, same-sex marriage.
- Distinctive element: rights grounded in sentience.

#### Rationale
The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes liberalism as a tradition in which liberals accord liberty primacy as a political value, with a presumption in favor of liberty that places the burden of justification on those who would restrict it; the framework's burden-of-proof rule for regulation follows the same pattern ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Infrastructure and Regulation|Domestic Policy & Governance]]). The Encyclopedia describes libertarianism as a family of views in political philosophy built on strong rights of self-ownership and property and a correspondingly minimal state. The National Conservatism Statement of Principles (2022) affirms national independence, free enterprise that serves the national interest, restrictive and assimilationist immigration, a strong but limited state, and public life rooted in Christianity where a Christian majority exists. Measured against these definitions, the framework's economic and rights positions sit closest to classical liberalism, its security and immigration positions closest to national conservatism, and its religious and sentientist positions apart from all three.

> This is an interpretation. The framework shares the liberal and libertarian commitment to rights, property, and markets, but accepts far more force against criminals and at the border. Grounding rights in sentience sets it apart from all three.
