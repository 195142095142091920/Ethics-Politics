## Civilizational Nationalism

>[!tldr] In short
> The country puts its own citizens first, keeps strong borders, and admits immigrants who share Western values and assimilate. Those values, not ethnicity, define who fits.

#### Position
Priority is given to cultural cohesion, high-trust social conditions, national sovereignty, and strong borders. The state's primary duties are to its own citizens and to the long-term continuity of the political community. Immigration is selective and oriented toward compatibility with the shared Western value structure, judged at the country level and through values vetting of every immigrant, and cultural assimilation is paramount. The border is secured, with lethal force used when necessary. "Western" names a value structure, not an ethnicity, so compatibility is open to anyone of any ancestry who holds those values; being Muslim creates a presumption of failing the values test, rebuttable only by verified individual evidence of conduct and record.

> [!question]- "Isn't nationalism just ethnic exclusion?"
> No. The community is defined by shared values: individual [[Glossary#Negative rights|negative rights]], liberty, religious freedom with separation of church and state, the rule of law, civic responsibility, and economic opportunity. People of any ethnicity who hold those values are compatible, and being Muslim creates a rebuttable presumption of failing the values test; compatibility judgments govern admission only; they impose no legal penalty for belief on anyone already within the community; the two civic disabilities for [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#^excluded-ideologies|excluded ideologies]] are not penalties.

> [!question]- "Does civilizational nationalism mean barring immigrants by religion?"
> It can. Being Muslim creates a presumption against admission. So does coming from a country where Islamist movements govern or where survey data (Pew's 2013 survey, fieldwork 2008 to 2012) show majority support for state-enforced religious law. Either presumption can be rebutted only through strict individual values vetting on verified evidence of conduct and record, never on self-declaration alone ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Islam and Extremism|Islam and Extremism]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]).

#### Key Elements
- Cultural cohesion, high trust, sovereignty, and strong borders.
- The state's first duties are to its own citizens.
- Selective immigration based on compatibility with Western values.
- Assimilation paramount.
- Lethal force when necessary.
- Compatibility defined by values, not ethnicity.

#### Rationale
The detailed immigration position is set out in [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Foreign Policy & International Relations]], border enforcement in [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Border Security and Immigration Enforcement|Border Security and Immigration Enforcement]], and the definition of Western compatibility in [[#Western Values and Compatibility]]. Assimilation and national identity are developed in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Patriotism and National Identity|Cultural & Social Issues]], and English as the common civic language reflects Executive Order 14224, which designated English the official language of the United States in 2025.

## Western Values and Compatibility

>[!tldr] In short
> "Western" means a set of values, not an ethnicity or a place: rights, liberty, religious freedom with church-state separation, rule of law, civic responsibility, and opportunity. Anyone who holds them is compatible, whatever their ancestry; being Muslim creates a presumption of failing it, which only verified individual evidence can overcome. Compatibility is judged by country-level presumptions and individual vetting, governs admission only, and penalizes no one's belief inside the community; the two civic disabilities for [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#^excluded-ideologies|excluded ideologies]] are not penalties.

#### Position
"Western" names a value structure rather than an ethnicity or a geography: individual negative rights, liberty, religious freedom with separation of church and state, the rule of law, civic responsibility, and economic opportunity. Compatibility means holding those values. It is judged at the country level through a presumption against admitting migrants from places where Islamist movements govern or where survey data show majority support for state-enforced religious law, and at the individual level through values vetting of every immigrant (see [[4. Religion & Secularism#Islam and Extremism|Religion & Secularism]] and [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Foreign Policy & International Relations]]). Compatibility judgments govern admission to the political community; they impose no legal penalty for belief on anyone already within it; the two civic disabilities for [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#^excluded-ideologies|excluded ideologies]] are not penalties ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Role of Religion in Government|Role of Religion in Government]]).

**My view:** I believe being Muslim creates a presumption of failing the values test. A Muslim applicant is presumed incompatible with the shared Western value structure, and answers in an interview do not overcome that. Only verified evidence of conduct and record does.

> [!question]- "Isn't judging cultures as incompatible just prejudice by another name?"
> The test is about values, and it cuts in both directions. A person who holds these values is compatible whatever their ancestry; a person who rejects them is not, whatever their ancestry. Being Muslim creates a presumption of failing it, which verified individual evidence can overcome. Many cultures outside Europe and North America are judged compatible. The judgment is aimed at the value structure and applied to individuals through vetting rather than assumed from origin alone, and it imposes no legal penalty for belief on anyone already in the political community.

> [!question]- "How is compatibility with Western values tested in practice?"
> By objective standards, not by opinion. A lawful non-citizen resident must pass the English and civics test used for naturalization within two years of admission, and removal requires a court finding by clear and convincing evidence. Opinions, beliefs, and speech are never grounds for removal ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Removal of Non-Citizen Residents and Loss of Citizenship|Removal of Non-Citizen Residents]]).

#### Key Elements
- "Western" is a value structure, not an ethnicity or geography: individual negative rights, liberty, religious freedom with separation of church and state, the rule of law, civic responsibility, and economic opportunity.
- Compatibility means holding those values.
- Country level: a presumption against admitting migrants from places where Islamist movements govern or where survey data show majority support for state-enforced religious law.
- Individual level: values vetting of every immigrant.
- Muslim applicants: a rebuttable presumption against admission, overcome only by clear and convincing evidence of conduct and record ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]).
- Compatibility governs admission only; no legal penalty for belief on anyone already within the community; the two civic disabilities for [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#^excluded-ideologies|excluded ideologies]] are not penalties.
- Christianity's historical role is acknowledged; scripture is never mandated.

#### Rationale
The values in that list are the ones the rest of my framework already depends on. Individual negative rights and liberty are the core of [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]. Religious freedom with separation of church and state is set out in [[4. Religion & Secularism#Role of Religion in Government|Religion & Secularism]]. The rule of law and civic responsibility appear in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Due Process and Rule of Law|Authority & Civil Liberties]], and economic opportunity in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Economic System|Domestic Policy & Governance]]. A person who holds these values is compatible whatever their ancestry. A person who rejects them is not, whatever their ancestry.

Because the test is about values, it cuts in both directions, and being Muslim creates a presumption of failing it. Many cultures outside Europe and North America are judged compatible; many legally immigrated Mexican and other Latin American communities are named as examples in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Patriotism and National Identity|Cultural & Social Issues]]. Cultures whose governing norms clash with these values, such as Islamist movements that seek state-enforced religious law, are judged incompatible. That judgment is aimed at the value structure, and it is applied to individuals through vetting rather than assumed from origin alone.

Christianity's historical role is acknowledged as part of how these values developed in the West, while scripture is never mandated ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Christianity and Western Heritage|Religion & Secularism]]). The standard is the values themselves, which a citizen can hold as a Christian, a member of another faith, or an atheist.

Shared values matter because trust is the medium of a rights-respecting order. Robert Putnam found that in the short run, residents of more ethnically diverse U.S. neighborhoods showed lower trust, even of their own group, and less community cooperation, and that successful immigrant societies overcome this by building shared identities. A 2020 meta-analysis of 1,001 estimates from 87 studies found a statistically significant but small negative relationship between ethnic diversity and social trust, stronger for trust in neighbors. A common value structure is the shared identity that lets a diverse society keep its trust.

## Economic Orientation

>[!tldr] In short
> Free markets, a flat tax with a large zero bracket, opt-in welfare, no business subsidies without opt-in, regulation that must justify itself, and balanced budgets with defense first. A flat, time-limited Sanctuary Levy of no more than 3%, on income above \$75,000 single or \$150,000 joint, pays for the care of animals freed by the shutdown of animal agriculture and testing.

#### Position
The economy is free-market leaning, with low and simple taxation built on a flat income tax with a large zero bracket. Mandatory welfare and large-scale redistribution are rejected in favor of an opt-in model. Subsidies to any business or industry require taxpayer opt-in, industrial policy is minimal and tightly justified, and the burden of proof rests on every regulation rather than on the people regulated. Budgets are balanced, with defense the top spending priority. There are two time-limited exceptions to the single flat rate. The first is a 2-point transition surcharge through 2041, all of it to the debt ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#^surcharge|Taxation]]). The second is a flat Sanctuary Levy of no more than 3%, on income above \$75,000 single or \$150,000 joint, for the care of animals freed by the shutdown of animal agriculture and testing. It ends when the sanctuary population falls below 1% of its starting animal units and its bonds are repaid ([[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]).

> [!question]- "Without mandatory welfare, what happens to people in need?"
> The welfare model is opt-in, and emergency protections remain, such as emergency shelters and emergency medical care set out in the detailed domestic policy section. What is rejected is compulsory redistribution as the default, not help for people in genuine emergencies.

> [!question]- "Isn't a flat income tax regressive?"
> The flat rate sits on top of a large zero bracket, so the lowest earners pay no income tax at all, and everyone above it pays the same rate on income past that bracket. Taxes stay low and simple and fund only essential functions. In dollar terms the largest cuts go to the highest earners, whose top federal rate falls from today's 37% to the flat 20% (22% through 2041 with the transition surcharge); I accept that on the principle of one rate for everyone ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Taxation|Taxation]]).

> [!question]- "Why is defense the top spending priority?"
> The state exists first to protect negative rights and the security of the community that sustains them, including defense against external threats ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Political Philosophy|Political Philosophy]]). Defense is that core function, so it comes first in a balanced budget.

> [!question]- "Does economic nationalism mean heavy tariffs?"
> No. Tariffs are treated as good in theory, as leverage and as protection for strategic industries, and questionable in practice, because studies of the 2018 tariffs found they were passed almost entirely into prices paid by American buyers. American industry is supported mainly through full expensing, long-run payroll tax cuts as the transition levy steps down, and regulatory relief, and through trade deals in which the United States also exports to each partner ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Trade and Economics|Trade and Economics]]).

#### Key Elements
- Free markets as the default.
- Flat income tax with a large zero bracket.
- Opt-in welfare instead of mandatory redistribution.
- No business subsidies without taxpayer opt-in.
- Burden of proof on regulation.
- Balanced budgets, with defense first.

#### Rationale
The tax, welfare, and regulatory positions are set out in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance|Domestic Policy & Governance]], including [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Welfare and Entitlements|Welfare and Entitlements]], [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Infrastructure and Regulation|Infrastructure and Regulation]], and [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Fiscal Policy|Fiscal Policy]]. The no-subsidy rule is applied to energy in [[9. Energy & Environment#Subsidies and Mandates|Energy & Environment]]. The scale of business subsidy the rule removes is visible in agriculture. Direct government payments to U.S. farm producers totaled about \$54 billion in 2020, measured in 2024 dollars, much of it pandemic assistance, according to USDA's Economic Research Service.

## Cultural Stance

>[!tldr] In short
> Traditional family life is the preferred default but not a legal mandate, merit replaces identity politics, speech is near-absolute, and the government condemns Nazism, communism, and Islam without punishing belief.

#### Position
The nuclear family and traditional social patterns are the preferred default, without legal mandates for private life; same-sex marriage is legal, and divorce is allowed. Identity politics and DEI frameworks are opposed, and people are treated on merit. Free speech is near-absolute, with narrow exceptions for true threats and incitement to imminent violence. Nazism, communism, and Islam are condemned in the government's official voice, with no official platforms, public funding, or public honors. The criminal law restricts them only as it restricts any other ideology; as [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#^excluded-ideologies|excluded ideologies]], Nazism, communism, socialism, and Islam also carry two civic disabilities, which are not penalties.

> [!question]- "How can the government condemn an ideology and still protect free speech?"
> Condemnation is the government speaking for itself; it is not a restriction on anyone else. Citizens keep full freedom to hold and express these views. What they do not get is official endorsement, public funding, or public honors.

> [!question]- "Does cultural assimilation mean banning other languages?"
> No. English is the common language of civic life, and immigrants are expected to learn it as part of joining the shared Western value structure, but private use of any language is free ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Language and Assimilation|Language and Assimilation]]).

#### Key Elements
- Nuclear family as the preferred default, not a legal mandate.
- Same-sex marriage legal; divorce allowed.
- Opposition to identity politics and DEI; treatment on merit.
- Near-absolute free speech, except true threats and incitement.
- Official condemnation of Nazism, communism, and Islam without legal penalty for belief; the two civic disabilities for [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#^excluded-ideologies|excluded ideologies]] are not penalties: the office bar and the bar on building, rebuilding, expanding, or relocating places that promote these causes ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#^building-bar|Property Rights]]).

#### Rationale
The family, speech, and merit positions are set out in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Family and Gender|Family and Gender]], [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Free Speech and Censorship|Free Speech and Censorship]], [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Race and Diversity|Race and Diversity]], and [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#LGBTQ+ and Identity Issues|LGBTQ+ and Identity Issues]]. The treatment of Islamism is developed in [[4. Religion & Secularism#Islam and Extremism|Religion & Secularism]]. The free speech exceptions track the narrow categories the Supreme Court has recognized; in *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969) the Court held that advocacy may be punished only when directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and likely to incite or produce such action.

## Order and Security

>[!tldr] In short
> Crime is met hard: a mandatory death penalty for murder (including attempted murder), violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, human trafficking, and selling hard drugs, [[Glossary#Rights forfeiture|forfeiture]] for repeat offenders, and decisive action against cartels, under due process and police oversight outside emergencies, with no penalty for belief; the two civic disabilities for [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#^excluded-ideologies|excluded ideologies]] are not penalties.

#### Position
The orientation on crime is hardline. Murder (including attempted murder), violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, human trafficking, and selling hard drugs carry a mandatory death penalty under a strict evidentiary standard. Repeat offenders who reach three marks forfeit the right to life, with the victim choosing execution, experimentation, or life with labor (a judge decides when victims disagree, the victim has died, or the victim is an animal). Sentences for first and second offenses are greatly increased in length, every custodial sentence is served as compelled labor, idle permanent incarceration is abolished, and cartels and chronic rights violators face decisive action. Substantial state coercive power is accepted as necessary to suppress recurrent predation and disorder, with independent oversight of police. Islamist and communist ideologies are treated as civilizational and security threats, monitored only through public data and lawful process, with no legal penalty for belief; the two civic disabilities for excluded ideologies are not penalties.

> [!example]- Quick reference: crimes and outcomes
> ![[6. Criminal Justice & Security#^crime-table]]

> [!question]- "Doesn't this much state power threaten the innocent?"
> The power is aimed at those who have violated rights, and outside emergencies it operates under due process: a strict evidentiary standard, compensation when convictions are vacated, and independent auditors of police conduct. Belief alone is never punished; adherence to an excluded ideology carries only the two civic disabilities in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#^excluded-ideologies|Excluded ideologies]].

> [!question]- "Isn't hardline crime policy plus nationalism just authoritarianism?"
> Authoritarianism concentrates power and removes the checks on it; here the state adds checks alongside the harsh penalties. Emergency powers are strictly limited ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Emergency Powers|Emergency Powers]]), every elected office faces the voters every three years ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Term Limits|Term Limits]]), power is divided and checked ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Government Structure and Checks|Government Structure and Checks]]), juries must be unanimous ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Role of Juries|Role of Juries]]), police face independent oversight and personal liability, and speech, including speech against the government, is protected absolutely ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Free Speech and Censorship|Free Speech and Censorship]]). The state's force falls on those proven to have violated rights, not on dissent or belief.

#### Key Elements
- Mandatory death penalty for murder (including attempted murder), violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, human trafficking, and selling hard drugs under a strict evidentiary standard.
- Forfeiture of the right to life for repeat offenders at three marks; the victim chooses execution, experimentation, or life with labor. When multiple victims disagree, when the victim has died, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim.
- Longer sentences served as compelled hard labor; grave abuse without [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|lasting injury]] falls in the grave tier: three marks: execution, experimentation, or life with labor; lesser crimes carry one or two marks and a fixed term; no idle permanent incarceration.
- Decisive action against cartels and chronic violators.
- Independent oversight of police.
- Ideological threats monitored through public data only; no penalty for belief (the two civic disabilities for [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#^excluded-ideologies|excluded ideologies]] are not penalties).

## The Future and Technology

>[!tldr] In short
> Technology is embraced within sentient-rights limits: AI and genetics are supported, data is opt-in, animal testing is banned, and sentient AI has rights and the same forfeiture rules as everyone else.

#### Position
The orientation is techno-optimist within [[Glossary#Sentience basis (sentientism)|sentientist]] constraints. AI, genetic technologies, brain-computer interfaces, and privatized civilian spaceflight are supported, with private innovation preferred and government limited to regulating existential threats, rights violations, and national security. Data trading is opt-in only. Animal testing is banned, with experimentation redirected to condemned offenders whose rights are forfeited. Any emergent AI sentience is recognized under the ethical framework and receives full protection, but remains subject to decisive termination if it violates or imminently threatens rights.

> [!question]- "Is it consistent to recognize AI sentience and still allow termination?"
> Yes. The same forfeiture rules apply to every sentient being. A sentient AI holds rights like any other, and like any other it forfeits them if it violates or imminently threatens the rights of others.

> [!question]- "How does technology fit the sentientist goals?"
> Lab-grown meat is treated as humanity's best hope for a sentientist world and the replacement path as animal agriculture is banned, and animal testing is banned, with experiments done instead on offenders sentenced to the experimentation track ([[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Animal Rights and Sentience Technology]]; [[9. Energy & Environment#Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat|Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat]]).

#### Key Elements
- Support for AI, genetic technologies, BCIs, and private spaceflight.
- Private innovation preferred; regulation limited to existential, rights, and security risks.
- Opt-in data trading.
- Animal testing banned; experimentation redirected to forfeited offenders.
- Sentient AI protected, and subject to the same forfeiture rules.

## Relation to Other Political Traditions

>[!tldr] In short
> I share the liberal and libertarian commitment to rights, property, and markets, but I accept far more force against criminals and at the border. I share national conservatism's emphasis on fiscal restraint, a strong military, assimilation, and patriotism, but I base culture on values rather than heritage and keep religion out of government. Grounding rights in sentience sets me apart from all three.

#### Position
I don't file my views under an existing label, but it's fair to say where I stand. I agree with classical liberalism and libertarianism on negative rights, private property, free markets, and a state limited to protecting people from force and fraud ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]; [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance|Domestic Policy & Governance]]). I part from libertarianism in accepting far more coercive power against those who violate rights: the mandatory death penalty ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Criminal Justice & Security]]), strict borders with lethal force when necessary ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Foreign Policy & International Relations]]), and decisive force against organized threats. National conservatism, as most people use the term, means being fiscally conservative, socially traditional, and pro-military, with an emphasis on cultural cohesion and national identity. I share that commitment to fiscal restraint, a strong military, assimilation, patriotism, and national interest in trade ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Patriotism and National Identity|Cultural & Social Issues]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Global Institutions|Foreign Policy & International Relations]]), but I ground the shared culture in a structure of values open to people of any ethnicity rather than in religious or ancestral heritage, church and state ought to stay separate and churches ought to be taxed ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Religious Exemptions|Religion & Secularism]]), and the state ought to recognize same-sex marriage ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#LGBTQ+ and Identity Issues|Cultural & Social Issues]]). What sets me apart from all three is sentientism: rights rest on sentience rather than species, which extends protection to animals and to any truly sentient artificial being ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sentience Determination|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]).

> [!question]- "Isn't this just national conservatism with a libertarian economic policy?"
> No. The movement's own statement of principles calls for public life rooted in Christianity in nations with a Christian majority and for public support of the traditional family. The government ought to keep church and state separate, tax churches, and recognize same-sex marriage. Grounding rights in sentience, meanwhile, has no counterpart in any of the three traditions.

#### Key Elements
- I align with libertarianism and national conservatism on fiscal restraint, the role of government, tradition, strong military, and cohesion.
- I align with classical liberalism on marriage freedom and the separation of church and state.
- I reject a mandatory welfare state, forced redistribution, tradition that violates sentient rights, and the progressive left's social agenda.
- I ground rights in sentience, which is unique.
- I am stricter on rights violations and crime than most of the political spectrum.
- No existing label fits the whole framework.

## Communism in Practice

>[!tldr] In short
> Communist theory promises a classless society in which "the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all." In practice, every state that tried to build it abolished private property by force, banned rival parties, and ran forced-labor camps, and several caused famines and mass killings that cost millions of lives. Communism is condemned at the same severity as Nazism and Islamism, and the state treats its crimes as it treats Nazi crimes in what it teaches, commemorates, honors and funds, without making belief in it, or praise of it, a crime.

#### Position
I judge communism by its program and its record. Marx and Engels described the goal as an association in which "the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all." They also wrote that the theory "may be summed up in the single sentence: Abolition of private property," to be achieved at first "by means of despotic inroads on the rights of property." Every state that acted on this program in the twentieth century gave one party a monopoly of power. It confiscated land and businesses, punished political opponents, and directed labor by force. The worst cases include the Soviet Gulag, the Soviet famine of 1931–34 including the Holodomor in Ukraine, China's Great Leap Forward famine, and the Khmer Rouge regime in Cambodia. These are violations of the right to life, liberty, and property on a scale few other systems have matched. Communism is condemned at the same severity as Nazism and Islamism, without making belief in it a crime, and its organized violent groups treated as threats ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Terrorism and Domestic Extremism|Terrorism and Domestic Extremism]]).

I believe the United States ought to treat communism the way it treats Nazism in everything the government does itself. State history standards ought to teach the crimes of communist regimes alongside the Holocaust, the nation ought to remember their victims, and no communist figure or regime ought to receive a public honor, name, statue, holiday, or public funding, just as no Nazi one does. Existing public honors, names, statues, and holidays for communist figures or regimes ought to be removed, as a Nazi one would not be left standing ([[2. Political and Social Character#Cultural Stance|Cultural Stance]]). That is the government speaking for itself. Private citizens keep their full right to praise Lenin or Mao, as they keep the right to praise Hitler ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Free Speech and Censorship|Free Speech and Censorship]]).

> [!question]- "Death-toll figures are disputed. Aren't critics inflating them?"

> [!question]- "Isn't equating them wrong? Nazism aimed at extermination, while communism's deaths came from a failed utopian plan."
> Defenders of the distinction point to intent: Nazism made killing a goal, while communism promised liberation. The record answers them. #### Key Elements
- Communism judged by its program and its record.
- Abolition of private property by force, one-party rule, and forced labor judged violations of negative rights.
- Documented harms: Gulag, Soviet famine and Holodomor, Great Leap famine, Khmer Rouge killings.
- Death tolls cited as ranges, from the Black Book's 85 to 100 million to the lowest mainstream figures.
- Treated like Nazism in the government's own conduct: its crimes taught in state history standards alongside the Holocaust, its victims publicly remembered, no public honor, name, statue, holiday, or funding for communist figures or regimes, and existing public ones removed; praising either stays legal.
- Condemned at the same severity as Nazism and Islamism, without legal penalty for belief or peaceful advocacy, with organized violent groups treated as threats. As one of the four [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#^excluded-ideologies|excluded ideologies]], it carries the two civic disabilities, which are not penalties: adherents may not hold taxpayer-funded positions or public office, which require the oath ([[4. Authority & Civil Liberties#^building-bar|Property Rights]]).

#### Rationale
I rest on individual negative rights to life, liberty, and property ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Political Philosophy|Political Philosophy]]). Communist states removed all three as a matter of system design. The 1977 Soviet Constitution named the Communist Party "the leading and guiding force of Soviet society and the nucleus of its political system," which left no lawful route for opposition. Forced collectivization began in 1929. Peasants were made to give up land and property, and resisters and "kulaks" were deported. Britannica reports that between 1931 and 1934 at least 5 million people died of hunger across the USSR, including at least 3.9 million Ukrainians, and that Ukrainian villages were blacklisted from food and peasants were barred from leaving to seek it. Britannica puts the Gulag at about 5 million prisoners by 1936; the NKVD's own records, opened in the 1990s, are lower, about 1.2 million in its camps and labor colonies at the end of 1936, not counting prisons and deported "special settlers." Western scholars estimate 1.2 to 1.7 million Gulag deaths from 1918 to 1956.

In China, the Great Leap Forward caused what Vaclav Smil called the largest famine in human history. Demographic reconstructions put the toll at 23 to 30 million excess deaths, and Smil wrote that it was "largely a manmade catastrophe." Britannica gives a lower estimate of about 20 million. In Cambodia, Yale's Cambodian Genocide Program estimates that about 1.7 million people, 21% of the population, died under the Khmer Rouge from 1975 to 1979. A UN-backed tribunal later convicted the regime's surviving leaders of genocide and crimes against humanity.

The widest estimate comes from the authors of *The Black Book of Communism*, who put the cumulative toll of communist rule at between 85 and 100 million. The breakdown reported at publication in 1997 gave China 45 to 72 million and the Soviet Union about 20 million. Critics, including two of the book's own contributors, Nicolas Werth and Jean-Louis Margolin, objected to its editor's "obsession" with reaching a hundred million dead, and Werth counted 15 million Soviet victims. The dispute is over the size of the total, not its order: Werth's 15 million Soviet victims and the Great Leap's 23 to 30 million already come to 38 to 45 million before Cambodia, North Korea or anywhere else is counted.

Treating the two alike in the state's own conduct already has precedent. Congress authorized the Victims of Communism Memorial in Washington in 1993, and it was dedicated in 2007. Florida's History of Communism standards, approved in November 2025 under a 2024 law, take effect in its public schools in the 2026–27 school year. In 2019 the European Parliament, by 535 votes to 66, called on member states to make "a clear and principled assessment" of the crimes of the communist regimes and the Nazi regime, to commemorate 23 August as a day of remembrance for the victims of totalitarian regimes, and to put that history in school curricula. In *Pleasant Grove City v. Summum* (2009) the Supreme Court held that placing a permanent monument in a public park is government speech, not subject to scrutiny under the Free Speech Clause, so the government's choices about memorials and honors restrict no one else's speech.

The common thread is collective goals placed above individual rights. When the state owns all property and allows no opposition, a person has no legal defense against it. Famines then go unreported, and dissent becomes a crime. My answer is the opposite design: property rights, competing parties, and limits on state power, with belief itself left free.
