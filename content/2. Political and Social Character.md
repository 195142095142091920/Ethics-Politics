## Civilizational Nationalism

>[!tldr] In short
> The country puts its own citizens first, keeps strong borders, and admits immigrants who share Western values and assimilate. Those values, not ethnicity, define who fits.

#### Position
Priority is given to cultural cohesion, high-trust social conditions, national sovereignty, and strong borders. The state's primary duties are to its own citizens and to the long-term continuity of the political community. Immigration is selective and oriented toward compatibility with the shared Western value structure, judged at the country level and through values vetting of every immigrant, and cultural assimilation is paramount. The border is secured, with lethal force used when necessary against violent or armed crossings. "Western" names a value structure, not an ethnicity, so compatibility is open to anyone of any ancestry who holds those values; being Muslim fails the values test.

> [!question]- "Isn't nationalism just ethnic exclusion?"
> No. The community is defined by shared values: individual [[Glossary#Negative rights|negative rights]], liberty, religious freedom with separation of church and state, the rule of law, civic responsibility, and economic opportunity. People of any ethnicity who hold those values are compatible, and being Muslim fails the values test; compatibility judgments govern admission only; they impose no legal penalty for belief on anyone already within the community.

> [!question]- "Does civilizational nationalism mean barring immigrants by religion?"
> It can. Being Muslim fails the values test, and I am fine with an outright ban on Muslims immigrating ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Islam and Extremism|Islam and Extremism]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]). For non-Muslim migrants from countries where Islamist movements govern or where survey data show majority support for state-enforced religious law, there is a presumption against admission that can be rebutted through strict individual values vetting.

#### Key Elements
- Cultural cohesion, high trust, sovereignty, and strong borders.
- The state's first duties are to its own citizens.
- Selective immigration based on compatibility with Western values.
- Assimilation paramount.
- Lethal force when necessary against violent or armed crossings.
- Compatibility defined by values, not ethnicity.

#### Rationale
The detailed immigration position is set out in [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Foreign Policy & International Relations]], border enforcement in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Border Security and Immigration Enforcement|Criminal Justice & Security]], and the definition of Western compatibility in [[#Western Values and Compatibility]]. Assimilation and national identity are developed in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Patriotism and National Identity|Cultural & Social Issues]], and English as the common civic language reflects Executive Order 14224, which designated English the official language of the United States in 2025.

## Western Values and Compatibility

>[!tldr] In short
> "Western" means a set of values, not an ethnicity or a place: rights, liberty, religious freedom with church-state separation, rule of law, civic responsibility, and opportunity. Anyone who holds them is compatible, whatever their ancestry; being Muslim fails the test. Compatibility is judged by country-level presumptions and individual vetting, governs admission only, and penalizes no one's belief inside the community.

#### Position
"Western" names a value structure rather than an ethnicity or a geography: individual negative rights, liberty, religious freedom with separation of church and state, the rule of law, civic responsibility, and economic opportunity. Compatibility means holding those values. It is judged at the country level through a presumption against admitting migrants from places where Islamist movements govern or where survey data show majority support for state-enforced religious law, and at the individual level through values vetting of every immigrant (see [[4. Religion & Secularism#Islam and Extremism|Religion & Secularism]] and [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Foreign Policy & International Relations]]). Compatibility judgments govern admission to the political community; they impose no legal penalty for belief on anyone already within it.

**My view:** I believe being Muslim fails the values test. A Muslim applicant is not compatible with the shared Western value structure, whatever their individual answers in vetting.

> [!question]- "Isn't judging cultures as incompatible just prejudice by another name?"
> The test is about values, and it cuts in both directions. A person who holds these values is compatible whatever their ancestry; a person who rejects them is not, whatever their ancestry. Being Muslim fails the test. Many cultures outside Europe and North America are judged compatible. The judgment is aimed at the value structure and applied to individuals through vetting rather than assumed from origin alone, and it imposes no legal penalty for belief on anyone already in the political community.

> [!question]- "How is compatibility with Western values tested in practice?"
> By objective standards, not by opinion. A lawful non-citizen resident must pass the English and civics test used for naturalization within two years of admission, and removal requires a court finding by clear and convincing evidence. Opinions, beliefs, and speech are never grounds for removal ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Removal of Non-Citizen Residents and Loss of Citizenship|Removal of Non-Citizen Residents]]).

#### Key Elements
- "Western" is a value structure, not an ethnicity or geography: individual negative rights, liberty, religious freedom with separation of church and state, the rule of law, civic responsibility, and economic opportunity.
- Compatibility means holding those values.
- Country level: a presumption against admitting migrants from places where Islamist movements govern or where survey data show majority support for state-enforced religious law.
- Individual level: values vetting of every immigrant.
- Compatibility governs admission only; no legal penalty for belief on anyone already within the community.
- Christianity's historical role is acknowledged; scripture is never mandated.

#### Rationale
The values in that list are the ones the rest of the framework already depends on. Individual negative rights and liberty are the core of [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]. Religious freedom with separation of church and state is set out in [[4. Religion & Secularism#Role of Religion in Government|Religion & Secularism]]. The rule of law and civic responsibility appear in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Due Process and Rule of Law|Authority & Civil Liberties]], and economic opportunity in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Economic System|Domestic Policy & Governance]]. A person who holds these values is compatible whatever their ancestry. A person who rejects them is not, whatever their ancestry.

Because the test is about values, it cuts in both directions, and being Muslim fails it. Many cultures outside Europe and North America are judged compatible; many legally immigrated Mexican and other Latin American communities are named as examples in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Patriotism and National Identity|Cultural & Social Issues]]. Cultures whose governing norms clash with these values, such as Islamist movements that seek state-enforced religious law, are judged incompatible. That judgment is aimed at the value structure, and it is applied to individuals through vetting rather than assumed from origin alone.

Christianity's historical role is acknowledged as part of how these values developed in the West, while scripture is never mandated ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Christianity and Western Heritage|Religion & Secularism]]). The standard is the values themselves, which a citizen can hold as a Christian, a member of another faith, or an atheist.

Shared values matter because trust is the medium of a rights-respecting order. Robert Putnam found that in the short run, residents of more ethnically diverse U.S. neighborhoods showed lower trust, even of their own group, and less community cooperation, and that successful immigrant societies overcome this by building shared identities. A 2020 meta-analysis of 1,001 estimates from 87 studies found a statistically significant but small negative relationship between ethnic diversity and social trust, stronger for trust in neighbors. A common value structure is the shared identity that lets a diverse society keep its trust.

## Economic Orientation

>[!tldr] In short
> Free markets, a flat tax with a large zero bracket, opt-in welfare, no business subsidies without opt-in, regulation that must justify itself, and balanced budgets with defense first.

#### Position
The economy is free-market leaning, with low and simple taxation built on a flat income tax with a large zero bracket. Mandatory welfare and large-scale redistribution are rejected in favor of an opt-in model. Subsidies to any business or industry require taxpayer opt-in, industrial policy is minimal and tightly justified, and the burden of proof rests on every regulation rather than on the people regulated. Budgets are balanced, with defense the top spending priority.

> [!question]- "Without mandatory welfare, what happens to people in need?"
> The welfare model is opt-in, and emergency protections remain, such as emergency shelters and emergency medical care set out in the detailed domestic policy section. What is rejected is compulsory redistribution as the default, not help for people in genuine emergencies.

> [!question]- "Isn't a flat income tax regressive?"
> The flat rate sits on top of a large zero bracket, so the lowest earners pay no income tax at all, and everyone above it pays the same rate on income past that bracket. Taxes stay low and simple and fund only essential functions. In dollar terms the largest cuts go to the highest earners, whose top federal rate falls from today's 37% to the flat 20%; the framework accepts that on the principle of one rate for everyone ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Taxation|Taxation]]).

> [!question]- "Why is defense the top spending priority?"
> The state exists first to protect negative rights and the security of the community that sustains them, including defense against external threats ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Political Philosophy|Political Philosophy]]). Defense is that core function, so it comes first in a balanced budget.

> [!question]- "Does economic nationalism mean heavy tariffs?"
> No. Tariffs are treated as good in theory, as leverage and as protection for strategic industries, and questionable in practice, because studies of the 2018 tariffs found they were passed almost entirely into prices paid by American buyers. American industry is supported mainly through payroll tax cuts, full expensing, and regulatory relief, and through trade deals in which the United States also exports to each partner ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Trade and Economics|Trade and Economics]]).

#### Key Elements
- Free markets as the default.
- Flat income tax with a large zero bracket.
- Opt-in welfare instead of mandatory redistribution.
- No business subsidies without taxpayer opt-in.
- Burden of proof on regulation.
- Balanced budgets, with defense first.

#### Rationale
The tax, welfare, and regulatory positions are set out in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance|Domestic Policy & Governance]], including [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Welfare and Entitlements|Welfare and Entitlements]], [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Infrastructure and Regulation|Infrastructure and Regulation]], and [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Fiscal Policy|Fiscal Policy]]. The no-subsidy rule is applied to energy in [[9. Energy & Environment#Subsidies and Mandates|Energy & Environment]]. The scale of business subsidy the rule removes is visible in agriculture. Direct government payments to U.S. farm producers totaled about \$54 billion in 2020, measured in 2024 dollars, much of it pandemic assistance, according to USDA's Economic Research Service.

## Cultural Stance

>[!tldr] In short
> Traditional family life is the preferred default but not a legal mandate, merit replaces identity politics, speech is near-absolute, and the government condemns Islamism and communism without punishing belief.

#### Position
The nuclear family and traditional social patterns are the preferred default, without legal mandates for private life; same-sex marriage is legal, and divorce is allowed. Identity politics and DEI frameworks are opposed, and people are treated on merit. Free speech is near-absolute, with narrow exceptions for true threats and incitement to imminent violence. Islamism and communism are condemned in the government's official voice, with no official platforms, public funding, or public honors, while the law restricts them only as it restricts any other ideology.

> [!question]- "How can the government condemn an ideology and still protect free speech?"
> Condemnation is the government speaking for itself; it is not a restriction on anyone else. Citizens keep full freedom to hold and express these views. What they do not get is official endorsement, public funding, or public honors.

> [!question]- "Does cultural assimilation mean banning other languages?"
> No. English is the common language of civic life, and immigrants are expected to learn it as part of joining the shared Western value structure, but private use of any language is free ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Language and Assimilation|Language and Assimilation]]).

#### Key Elements
- Nuclear family as the preferred default, not a legal mandate.
- Same-sex marriage legal; divorce allowed.
- Opposition to identity politics and DEI; treatment on merit.
- Near-absolute free speech, except true threats and incitement.
- Official condemnation of Nazism, communism, and Islamism without legal penalty for belief.

#### Rationale

## Order and Security

>[!tldr] In short
> Crime is met hard: a mandatory death penalty for murder (including attempted murder), violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, and human trafficking, [[Glossary#Rights forfeiture|forfeiture]] for repeat offenders, and decisive action against cartels, under due process and police oversight outside emergencies, with no penalty for belief.

#### Position
The orientation on crime is hardline. Murder (including attempted murder), violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, and human trafficking carry a mandatory death penalty under a strict evidentiary standard. Repeat offenders who reach three marks forfeit the right to life, with the victim choosing execution, experimentation, or life with labor (a judge decides when victims disagree, the victim has died, or the victim is an animal). Sentences for first and second offenses are greatly increased in length, every custodial sentence is served as compelled labor, idle permanent incarceration is abolished, and cartels and chronic rights violators face decisive action. Substantial state coercive power is accepted as necessary to suppress recurrent predation and disorder, with independent oversight of police. Islamist and communist ideologies are treated as civilizational and security threats, monitored only through public data and lawful process, with no legal penalty for belief.

> [!question]- "Doesn't this much state power threaten the innocent?"
> The power is aimed at those who have violated rights, and outside emergencies it operates under due process: a strict evidentiary standard, compensation when convictions are vacated, and independent auditors of police conduct. Belief alone is never punished.

> [!question]- "Isn't hardline crime policy plus nationalism just authoritarianism?"
> Authoritarianism concentrates power and removes the checks on it; the framework adds checks alongside the harsh penalties. Emergency powers are strictly limited ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Emergency Powers|Emergency Powers]]), elected offices have term limits ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Term Limits|Term Limits]]), power is divided and checked ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Government Structure and Checks|Government Structure and Checks]]), juries must be unanimous ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Role of Juries|Role of Juries]]), police face independent oversight and personal liability, and speech, including speech against the government, is protected absolutely ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Free Speech and Censorship|Free Speech and Censorship]]). The state's force falls on those proven to have violated rights, not on dissent or belief.

#### Key Elements
- Mandatory death penalty for murder (including attempted murder), violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, and human trafficking under a strict evidentiary standard.
- Forfeiture of the right to life for repeat offenders at three marks; the victim chooses execution, experimentation, or life with labor. When multiple victims disagree, when the victim has died, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim.
- Longer sentences served as compelled hard labor; grave abuse without [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|lasting injury]] is egregious: three marks: execution, experimentation, or life with labor; lesser crimes carry one or two marks and a fixed term; no idle permanent incarceration.
- Decisive action against cartels and chronic violators.
- Independent oversight of police.
- Ideological threats monitored through public data only; no penalty for belief.

## The Future and Technology

>[!tldr] In short
> Technology is embraced within sentient-rights limits: AI and genetics are supported, data is opt-in, animal testing is banned, and sentient AI has rights and the same forfeiture rules as everyone else.

#### Position
The orientation is techno-optimist within [[Glossary#Sentience basis (sentientism)|sentientist]] constraints. AI, genetic technologies, brain-computer interfaces, and privatized civilian spaceflight are supported, with private innovation preferred and government limited to regulating existential threats, rights violations, and national security. Data trading is opt-in only. Animal testing is banned, with experimentation redirected to condemned offenders whose rights are forfeited. Any emergent AI sentience is recognized under the ethical framework and receives full protection, but remains subject to decisive termination if it violates or imminently threatens rights.

> [!question]- "Is it consistent to recognize AI sentience and still allow termination?"
> Yes. The same forfeiture rules apply to every sentient being. A sentient AI holds rights like any other, and like any other it forfeits them if it violates or imminently threatens the rights of others.

> [!question]- "How does technology fit the sentientist goals?"
> Lab-grown meat is treated as humanity's best hope for a sentientist world and the replacement path as animal agriculture is banned, and animal testing is banned, with experiments done instead on offenders sentenced to the experimentation track ([[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Animal Rights and Sentience Technology]]; [[9. Energy & Environment#Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat|Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat]]).

#### Key Elements
- Support for AI, genetic technologies, BCIs, and private spaceflight.
- Private innovation preferred; regulation limited to existential, rights, and security risks.
- Opt-in data trading.
- Animal testing banned; experimentation redirected to forfeited offenders.
- Sentient AI protected, and subject to the same forfeiture rules.

## Relation to Other Political Traditions

>[!tldr] In short
> I share the liberal and libertarian commitment to rights, property, and markets, but I accept far more force against criminals and at the border. I share national conservatism's emphasis on fiscal restraint, a strong military, assimilation, and patriotism, but I base culture on values rather than heritage and keep religion out of government. Grounding rights in sentience sets this framework apart from all three.

#### Position
I do not file this framework under an existing label, but it is fair to say where it stands. I agree with classical liberalism and libertarianism on negative rights, private property, free markets, and a state limited to protecting people from force and fraud ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]; [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance|Domestic Policy & Governance]]). I part from libertarianism in accepting far more coercive power against those who violate rights: the mandatory death penalty ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Criminal Justice & Security]]), strict borders with lethal force against violent or armed crossings ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Foreign Policy & International Relations]]), and decisive force against organized threats. National conservatism, as most people use the term, means being fiscally conservative, socially traditional, and pro-military, with an emphasis on cultural cohesion and national identity. I share that commitment to fiscal restraint, a strong military, assimilation, patriotism, and national interest in trade ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Patriotism and National Identity|Cultural & Social Issues]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Global Institutions|Foreign Policy & International Relations]]), but I ground the shared culture in a structure of values open to people of any ethnicity rather than in religious or ancestral heritage, I keep church and state separate and tax churches ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Religious Exemptions|Religion & Secularism]]), and I recognize same-sex marriage ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#LGBTQ+ and Identity Issues|Cultural & Social Issues]]). What sets this framework apart from all three is sentientism: rights rest on sentience rather than species, which extends protection to animals and to any truly sentient artificial being ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sentience Determination|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]).

> [!question]- "Isn't this just national conservatism with a libertarian economic policy?"
> No. The movement's own statement of principles calls for public life rooted in Christianity in nations with a Christian majority and for public support of the traditional family. I keep church and state separate, tax churches, and recognize same-sex marriage. Grounding rights in sentience, meanwhile, has no counterpart in any of the three traditions.

#### Key Elements
- I align with libertarianism and national conservatism on fiscal restraint, the role of government, tradition, strong military, and cohesion.
- I align with classical liberalism on marriage freedom and the separation of church and state.
- I reject a mandatory welfare state, forced redistribution, tradition that violates sentient rights, and the progressive left's social agenda.
- I ground rights in sentience, which is unique.
- I am stricter on rights violations and crime than most of the political spectrum.
- No existing label fits the whole framework.

#### Rationale
The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes liberalism as a tradition in which liberals accord liberty primacy as a political value, with a presumption in favor of liberty that puts the burden of justification on anyone who would restrict it. That is the same burden I place on regulation ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Infrastructure and Regulation|Domestic Policy & Governance]]). The Encyclopedia describes libertarianism as a family of views built on strong rights of self-ownership and property and a correspondingly minimal state. In everyday usage, national conservatism names a fiscally conservative, socially traditional, pro-military outlook centered on cultural cohesion and national identity, and that everyday sense is the one I mostly share. The national conservative movement's formal Statement of Principles, published in 2022, goes further. It calls for public life rooted in Christianity in nations with a Christian majority and for public support of the traditional family, which is where I part ways. Measured against those definitions, my economic and rights positions sit closest to classical liberalism, my security and immigration positions closest to national conservatism, and my religious and sentientist positions apart from all three.

## Communism in Practice

>[!tldr] In short
> Communist theory promises a classless society in which "the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all." In practice, every state that tried to build it abolished private property by force, banned rival parties, and ran forced-labor camps, and several caused famines and mass killings that cost millions of lives. Communism ought to be condemned at the same severity as Nazism and Islamism, without making belief in it a crime.

#### Position
The framework separates what communist theory claims from what communist states did. Marx and Engels described the goal as an association in which "the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all." They also wrote that the theory "may be summed up in the single sentence: Abolition of private property," to be achieved at first "by means of despotic inroads on the rights of property." Every state that acted on this program in the twentieth century gave one party a monopoly of power. It confiscated land and businesses, punished political opponents, and directed labor by force. The worst cases include the Soviet Gulag, the Soviet famine of 1931–34 including the Holodomor in Ukraine, China's Great Leap Forward famine, and the Khmer Rouge regime in Cambodia. These are violations of the right to life, liberty, and property on a scale few other systems have matched. Communism ought to be condemned at the same severity as Nazism and Islamism, without making belief in it a crime, and its organized violent groups treated as threats ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Terrorism and Domestic Extremism|Terrorism and Domestic Extremism]]).

> [!question]- "Wasn't that not real communism, only distortions by Stalin, Mao, and Pol Pot?"
> The framework judges the program and its results. Theorists can fairly say no state reached the classless society Marx described. But the program itself called for abolishing private property and for "despotic inroads" to do it. The abuses followed from giving a state unchecked power over all property and labor, in countries with very different cultures and leaders. A plan that has failed the same way in every state that tried it is judged by that record.

> [!question]- "Didn't communist states also reduce illiteracy, expand health care, and industrialize?"
> Supporters point to gains in literacy, health care, and heavy industry, and the framework does not dispute that some gains occurred. But the framework does not accept trading some people's lives and liberty for others' gains, and market economies achieved these gains without camps or famine ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Economic System|Economic System]]). China's own reversal after the Great Leap, returning private plots and tools to peasants, is a concession from inside the system.

> [!question]- "Death-toll figures are disputed. Aren't critics inflating them?"
> Some totals are disputed, so the framework cites ranges from mainstream scholarship and not the highest claims. For the Great Leap famine, early demographic estimates were 16.5 to 23 million excess deaths, and later studies found 23 to 30 million. Western scholars put Gulag deaths from 1918 to 1956 at 1.2 to 1.7 million. Even the lowest figures describe deaths caused by state policy on a scale that settles the moral question.

> [!question]- "If communism is condemned, why not ban the party?"
> Because the framework punishes harm, not belief. Advocacy is protected up to true threats and incitement to imminent violence, the same rule that applies to every ideology ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Free Speech and Censorship|Free Speech and Censorship]]). Banning a belief would copy the method being condemned.

#### Key Elements
- Communist theory's stated aims distinguished from communist state practice.
- Abolition of private property by force, one-party rule, and forced labor judged violations of negative rights.
- Documented harms: Gulag, Soviet famine and Holodomor, Great Leap famine, Khmer Rouge killings.
- Death tolls cited as scholarly ranges.
- Condemned at the same severity as Nazism and Islamism, without legal penalty for belief or peaceful advocacy, with organized violent groups treated as threats.

#### Rationale
The framework rests on individual negative rights to life, liberty, and property ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Political Philosophy|Political Philosophy]]). Communist states removed all three as a matter of system design. The 1977 Soviet Constitution named the Communist Party "the leading and guiding force of Soviet society and the nucleus of its political system," which left no lawful route for opposition. Forced collectivization began in 1929. Peasants were made to give up land and property, and resisters and "kulaks" were deported. Britannica reports that between 1931 and 1934 at least 5 million people died of hunger across the USSR, including at least 3.9 million Ukrainians, and that Ukrainian villages were blacklisted from food and peasants were barred from leaving to seek it. The Gulag held about 5 million prisoners by 1936, and Western scholars estimate 1.2 to 1.7 million deaths from 1918 to 1956.

In China, the Great Leap Forward caused what Vaclav Smil called the largest famine in human history. Demographic reconstructions put the toll at 23 to 30 million excess deaths, and Smil wrote that it was "largely a manmade catastrophe." Britannica gives a lower estimate of about 20 million. In Cambodia, Yale's Cambodian Genocide Program estimates that about 1.7 million people, 21% of the population, died under the Khmer Rouge from 1975 to 1979. A UN-backed tribunal later convicted the regime's surviving leaders of genocide and crimes against humanity.

The common thread is collective goals placed above individual rights. When the state owns all property and allows no opposition, a person has no legal defense against it. Famines then go unreported, and dissent becomes a crime. The framework's answer is the opposite design: property rights, competing parties, and limits on state power, with belief itself left free.

## Nazism

>[!tldr] In short
> Nazism ranked human beings by supposed race and used the state to strip, expel, and kill those it ranked lowest. It murdered six million Jews and millions of others, including disabled people, Roma, Poles, and Soviet prisoners of war. It is the clearest case of what the framework rejects: rights assigned by group identity instead of held by every sentient individual. Nazism ought to be condemned at the same severity as communism and Islamism, without making belief in it a crime.

#### Position
Nazism is incompatible with the framework at its root. Its core claim was a racial hierarchy in which "Aryan" Germans were superior and Jews and others were inferior. It turned that claim into law, took total control of the state, and then pursued conquest and extermination. The framework grounds rights in sentience, which every person has equally, so any doctrine that assigns rights by race, ancestry, or disability contradicts it directly ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Animal Rights and Sentientism|Animal Rights and Sentientism]]). Nazism ought to be condemned at the same severity as communism and Islamism, without making belief in it a crime, and its organized violent groups treated as threats ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Terrorism and Domestic Extremism|Terrorism and Domestic Extremism]]).

> [!question]- "Doesn't the framework's own nationalism and civilizational language resemble it?"
> No, and the difference is the test itself. Nazism assigned worth by blood. The framework judges compatibility by values that anyone of any ancestry can hold, and it protects every sentient individual's rights, including non-citizens' ([[2. Political and Social Character#Western Values and Compatibility|Western Values and Compatibility]]). It does not penalize belief and leaves removal to courts with due process, which is the opposite of the Nazi method.

> [!question]- "Why give Nazism its own topic when no serious movement defends it?"
> Because it is the clearest case for testing a principle. Its racial laws, its "euthanasia" killings, and its genocide show what happens when a state decides some sentient beings have no rights. Naming the specific rules that made this possible shows why the framework puts rights before the state, the majority, and the group.

#### Key Elements
- Racial hierarchy and rights assigned by blood rejected as contrary to sentience-based equal rights.
- Documented harms: Nuremberg Race Laws, Aktion T4 and the wider "euthanasia" program, the Holocaust, Lebensraum conquest, and suspension of civil liberties.
- Victims cited from the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum.
- Condemned at the same severity as communism and Islamism, without legal penalty for belief or peaceful advocacy, with organized violent groups treated as threats.

#### Rationale
Each stage of Nazi rule removed rights the framework protects. The Reichstag Fire Decree of February 1933 suspended constitutional protections for personal liberty, free expression, the press, assembly, and the privacy of mail and telephone, and let the regime jail opponents without charge. The Nuremberg Race Laws of 1935 limited citizenship to those "of German or related blood." They banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and other Germans and were extended to Roma and Black people. These laws punished identity, not conduct.

The "euthanasia" program began in 1939 and targeted institutionalized disabled people as "life unworthy of life." By T4's own count, 70,273 people were killed at six gassing centers from January 1940 to August 1941, and historians estimate about 250,000 deaths across all phases. The U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum notes that T4's gas chambers and staff were later used for the killing centers of the Holocaust. The museum counts six million Jews murdered, about 2.7 million of them at killing centers and about 2 million in mass shootings. It also counts around 3.3 million Soviet prisoners of war, around 1.8 million non-Jewish Poles, 250,000 to 500,000 Roma, and 250,000 to 300,000 disabled people. Lebensraum ("living space") policy planned the removal of Slavs and Jews from eastern Europe. Pre-invasion guidelines stated that "many tens of millions of people in this territory will become superfluous and will have to die or migrate to Siberia."

The lesson the framework draws is that rights must belong to every sentient individual and must not depend on the state, a majority, or a group's ranking. Nazism reversed every part of that rule.

## Islamism

>[!tldr] In short
> Islamism is the political project of ruling by Islamic law. Where it holds power, it punishes apostasy and blasphemy, sometimes with death, and gives women and non-Muslims lesser legal standing. Its jihadist wing is the deadliest source of terrorism in the world today. It ought to be ranked as dangerous as Nazism and as the most dangerous ideology in the world today, and condemned at the same severity as Nazism and communism, without making belief in it a crime.

#### Position
Islamism seeks to make Islamic law the law of the state ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Sharia as State Law|Sharia as State Law]]). Where Islamist law is enforced, it punishes belief and speech through apostasy and blasphemy laws, imposes fixed (hudud) punishments such as stoning, flogging, and amputation, criminalizes consensual same-sex relations, and makes legal standing depend on sex and religion. Its jihadist wing pursues the same goal by terror. These are violations of the right to life, liberty, and equal standing that every sentient individual holds ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Animal Rights and Sentientism|Animal Rights and Sentientism]]). Islamism ought to be condemned at the same severity as Nazism and communism, without making belief in it a crime, and its organized violent groups treated as threats ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Terrorism and Domestic Extremism|Terrorism and Domestic Extremism]]). My separate critique of the faith's classical teachings is set out in [[4. Religion & Secularism#The Islamic Faith in Practice|The Islamic Faith in Practice]].

> [!question]- "How can Islamism rank with Nazism when Nazism killed far more people?"
> Nazism's toll is far larger, and I do not claim otherwise. It murdered six million Jews and millions of others in a few years ([[2. Political and Social Character#Nazism|Nazism]]). My ranking is about the present: which ideology is killing the most people now, in the most places, and holding state power to punish belief. On that test Islamism leads. The data and the points that cut against the ranking are set out in [[4. Religion & Secularism#Islam and Extremism|Islam and Extremism]].

> [!question]- "Isn't most terrorism in the West political rather than religious?"
> Yes. In North America since 2007, politically motivated attacks outnumbered religiously motivated ones, 84 to 21, and 58 were linked to the far right. Most terrorism deaths, 98% in 2024, occur in countries at war. But in the West, every death from religiously motivated terrorism from 2007 to 2024, 494 in all, was caused by jihadists, and worldwide the four deadliest groups were all jihadist.

> [!question]- "If Islamism is condemned, why not ban it?"
> Because I punish harm, not belief. Advocacy is protected up to true threats and incitement to imminent violence, the same rule that applies to every ideology ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Free Speech and Censorship|Free Speech and Censorship]]). Punishing belief would copy the method being condemned, which is exactly what apostasy and blasphemy laws do.

#### Key Elements
- Islamism defined as the political project of ruling by Islamic law.
- Apostasy and blasphemy laws, hudud punishments, criminalization of consensual same-sex relations, and unequal legal standing for women and non-Muslims judged violations of negative rights.
- Documented harms: jihadist terrorism led by Islamic State, JNIM, Tehrik-e-Taliban Pakistan, and al-Shabaab, and state penalties of death for blasphemy or apostasy.
- Ranked as dangerous as Nazism and as the most dangerous ideology in the world today, with Nazism's far larger historical toll acknowledged.
- Condemned at the same severity as Nazism and communism, without legal penalty for belief or peaceful advocacy, with organized violent groups treated as threats.

#### Rationale
Islamism removes rights my framework protects, both through state law and through terror. In 2019, 79 of 198 countries had laws against blasphemy and 22 had laws against apostasy, 13 of them in the Middle East and North Africa. In Afghanistan, Brunei, Iran, Mauritania, Nigeria, Pakistan, and Saudi Arabia, blasphemy can carry the death penalty. The Library of Congress found in 2014 that Afghanistan, Brunei, Mauritania, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Sudan, the United Arab Emirates, and Yemen expressly make apostasy a capital offense. Brunei's 2019 revisions provided death for adultery, sodomy, and insulting the Prophet, and amputation for theft, according to the UN human rights office. These laws punish belief, identity, and speech, not harm.

The jihadist wing kills on the largest scale of any terrorist movement today. In 2024, Islamic State and its affiliates were the deadliest group, killing 1,805 people in 22 countries. Together with Jamaat Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimeen, Tehrik-e-Taliban Pakistan, and al-Shabaab, they caused 4,204 deaths, 80% of the terrorism deaths attributed to a specific group, and were active in 30 countries. The Sahel alone accounted for 51% of all terrorism deaths. Total terrorism deaths fell 13% in 2024 to 7,555.

The common thread with Nazism and communism is a doctrine placed above individual rights, enforced by the state or by terror against those who do not submit. My answer is the same in each case: rights held equally by every sentient individual, a state barred from punishing belief, and force reserved for those who violate others' rights.
