These critiques focus on track records, incentives, stated platforms, and observable outcomes rather than vibes or any partisan lens.

### 1. Economic program ignores incentives and historical results

DSA’s platform pushes large-scale public ownership, Medicare for All (eliminating private insurance), Green New Deal with federal jobs guarantee, expansive rent control, debt cancellation, and heavy taxation/redistribution.

Evidence problem: Systems that heavily suppress private property, price signals, and profit motives (Venezuela under Chávez/Maduro, historical Soviet bloc, Maoist experiments) produced shortages, capital flight, hyperinflation, and collapse in living standards. Nordic success is frequently misattributed to “democratic socialism”; those countries maintain strong private property, flexible labor markets, and high economic freedom scores while running large welfare states. They are high-trust, market economies with redistribution layered on top — not models of public ownership of the means of production. Expanding the regulatory and ownership state in a lower-trust, more diverse society like the U.S. carries higher risks of stagnation and capture.

### 2. Soft-on-crime and institutional distrust

DSA materials explicitly call for demilitarizing police, disempowering police unions, redirecting funds, and moving toward abolishing police and prisons as protectors of “the rich.” Related policies include ending cash bail and mandatory minimums.

Where similar approaches were tried in U.S. cities (2020–2022 period), homicide and violent crime rose sharply in many progressive jurisdictions before partial reversals. High-trust order requires credible deterrence. Treating policing primarily as a class or racial instrument rather than a public good for protecting negative rights correlates with worse outcomes for the poorest neighborhoods.

### 3. Foreign policy and civilizational hostility

DSA chapters and national statements after October 7, 2023, included language framing the Hamas attacks as resistance to “apartheid” or “settler-colonialism,” with some local chapters expressing solidarity with “resistance.” The organization is strongly anti-Zionist, opposes military aid to Israel, and treats the U.S. as an imperialist power.

This is not neutral anti-war sentiment. It aligns with a broader pattern of viewing Western liberal democracies as the primary villains while downplaying or contextualizing the agency of Islamist terrorist groups and authoritarian regimes. For a country whose security depends on alliances and deterrence, this worldview is strategically corrosive.

### 4. Constitutional and institutional radicalism

Recent DSA platform language and statements from national co-chairs have included talk of abolishing or fundamentally restructuring the Senate, the presidency, the Supreme Court, ICE, and borders, alongside long-term goals of largely ending prisons and achieving public ownership of major corporations.

Even if framed as “democratic,” these are not incremental reforms. They treat core mediating institutions of the American system as illegitimate obstacles rather than hard-won constraints on power. History shows that movements which view constitutional limits as temporary barriers tend to expand state power once they gain it.

### 5. Political method and coalition

DSA grows by primarying mainstream Democrats from the left, using identity + economic populism. It prioritizes activist intensity over broad governability. Once in office, the gap between rhetoric (overturn capitalism) and the constraints of running a complex city or district produces either disappointment among supporters or pressure toward more radical measures. Mamdani’s mayoralty is the current live test case of this tension.

**Comprehensive list of major failed socialist experiments**

This list focuses on systems that implemented core socialist features — large-scale public/collective ownership of the means of production, central planning, suppression or heavy restriction of private property and market prices — and that produced clear, documented failures in living standards, mass death, economic collapse, or political breakdown. It excludes pure social democracies (Nordic countries), which retain private ownership and market allocation as the economic core.

### 1. Soviet Union (1917–1991)

- War Communism, collectivization, and central planning.
- Outcomes: Holodomor famine (Ukraine, 1932–33, millions dead); Great Terror; chronic shortages; technological lag; eventual systemic collapse and dissolution in 1991. GDP per capita and consumer living standards far below Western market economies by the end.

### 2. Maoist China – Great Leap Forward (1958–1962) and Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)

- Forced collectivization, backyard steel furnaces, elimination of private farming incentives.
- Outcomes: Great Chinese Famine — estimates of 15–55 million excess deaths (most common scholarly range ~30 million), the largest famine in recorded history. Cultural Revolution produced further chaos, persecution, and economic disruption. China only achieved sustained growth after market-oriented reforms under Deng Xiaoping.

### 3. Khmer Rouge Cambodia (Democratic Kampuchea, 1975–1979)

- Radical agrarian socialism, abolition of money, cities, and private property; forced rural collectivization.
- Outcomes: Approximately 1.5–2 million deaths (roughly 25% of the population) from execution, starvation, and forced labor — one of the highest proportional death tolls of any modern regime.

### 4. North Korea (1948–present)

- Strict central planning, collective agriculture, personality cult, isolation.
- Outcomes: Chronic famine (notably mid-1990s, hundreds of thousands to millions dead); permanent mass poverty; extreme repression; ongoing dependence on external aid while the leadership maintains nuclear weapons and luxury. Stark contrast with market-oriented South Korea.

### 5. Cuba (1959–present)

- Nationalization of industry and agriculture, central planning, suppression of private enterprise.
- Outcomes: Persistent shortages, low productivity, mass emigration, dependence on Soviet then later Venezuelan and other subsidies. Living standards and economic freedom remain far below regional market-oriented peers despite high literacy/health metrics achieved at high opportunity cost.

### 6. Venezuela (Chávez–Maduro era, roughly 1999–present)

- Nationalizations, price controls, currency controls, expropriation of private businesses, heavy state direction of the oil sector.
- Outcomes: One of the largest peacetime economic collapses in modern history. GDP contracted dramatically (living standards fell ~70%+ in the worst years); hyperinflation; mass emigration (millions fled); widespread shortages of food and medicine. Oil-rich country reduced to crisis.

### 7. Eastern Bloc satellites (1945–1989/91)

- East Germany, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Albania — Soviet-imposed central planning and collectivization.
- Outcomes: Chronic shortages, lower productivity and innovation than Western counterparts, political repression, and eventual mass rejection (1989 revolutions). East Germany’s collapse relative to West Germany remains one of the cleanest controlled comparisons.

### 8. Ethiopia under the Derg (Mengistu, 1974–1991)

- Marxist-Leninist military regime, collectivization, “Red Terror.”
- Outcomes: Famine (1983–85, hundreds of thousands to millions dead), civil war, economic ruin, and eventual overthrow.

### 9. Other notable cases

- **Mozambique and Angola** (post-independence Marxist regimes): Prolonged civil war, economic collapse, eventual abandonment of strict socialism.
- **Nicaragua under the Sandinistas** (1979–1990): Nationalizations, economic mismanagement, hyperinflation, and electoral defeat.
- **Chile under Allende** (1970–1973): Rapid nationalizations and price controls leading to shortages, inflation, and political crisis (ended by coup).
- **Yugoslavia** (Tito’s market socialism variant): Relative success for a time, but structural weaknesses contributed to violent breakup in the 1990s.
- **Albania under Hoxha**: Extreme isolation and Stalinist planning; one of Europe’s poorest countries by the end.

### Pattern across cases

Common features of the failures include:

- Destruction of price signals and private incentives
- Politicization of production and investment
- Suppression of exit and criticism
- Resulting misallocation, shortages, capital destruction, and (in the worst cases) mass famine or emigration

Successful high-income countries with large welfare states (Nordics, etc.) did **not** follow this path; they preserved private ownership, competitive markets, and high economic freedom while adding redistribution. Equating those systems with the experiments above is a category error.

This record is why skepticism of programs that expand public ownership, suppress markets, and treat private property as illegitimate remains evidence-based rather than merely ideological.

**Why people support or join the DSA despite the historical record**

It is not primarily about IQ. Several overlapping factors explain it:

- **Moral framing over empirical results**. Many supporters treat socialism as a moral identity (“care for the poor/workers/oppressed”) rather than a set of institutional mechanisms with a track record. Once it becomes a signal of compassion or anti-establishment virtue, contradictory evidence is discounted as propaganda, “not real socialism,” or the fault of external sabotage.
- **Selective historical memory**. Failures are attributed to authoritarianism, sanctions, or “not democratic enough,” while Nordic success is claimed as proof. The distinction between market economies with large welfare states versus systems that suppress private ownership and price signals is routinely blurred.
- **Youth and low personal exposure to scarcity**. In rich countries, younger cohorts have grown up with abundance produced by market systems. Abstract critiques of “capitalism” feel costless. They have not lived through empty shelves, hyperinflation, or the surveillance state that accompanied many socialist experiments.
- **Institutional capture and status**. Universities, certain media, NGOs, and activist networks reward anti-capitalist language. DSA offers community, purpose, status, and a ready-made villain (corporations, “the rich,” “imperialism”). That is psychologically powerful.
- **Gradualism illusion**. Democratic socialists insist they only want reforms through elections. Historically, once the principle that the state should dominate the “commanding heights” or treat private property as conditional is accepted, the logic pushes toward more control when results disappoint.

None of this requires conspiracy. It is normal human tribalism, status-seeking, and motivated reasoning applied to economics and power.

**Socialism vs. Communism — clear distinction**

**Socialism** (broadly): Collective or state ownership/control of the means of production (factories, land, major industries), with distribution according to some notion of need or equality. It can, in theory, retain elections, some private small property, and markets in limited form. “Democratic socialism” claims the transition and the system itself will remain democratic.

**Communism** (in the Marxist sense): A further stage after socialism. Classless, stateless, moneyless society in which property is fully common and distribution is “from each according to ability, to each according to need.” In practice, every regime that called itself communist used a heavily authoritarian socialist state as the supposed transitional phase (the “dictatorship of the proletariat”). No society has reached the stateless communist end-state.

In real-world usage:

- Communist parties and regimes implemented one-party rule, central planning, and suppression of private capital.
- Socialist parties and governments varied more — some stayed within democratic frameworks and mixed economies; others moved toward heavy nationalization and planning.

Yes, in its formal ideology and platform direction, even if most members would reject the label “communist” or the Soviet model.

- DSA explicitly describes itself as a socialist organization seeking to move beyond capitalism. Its materials speak of collective ownership, democratizing the economy, and treating major corporations and industries as needing public control.

- National and chapter rhetoric frequently frames capitalism itself as the root problem and treats liberal democratic institutions as obstacles when they block socialist goals.
- The organization contains Marxist, Marxist-Leninist, and revolutionary socialist caucuses. While the public-facing brand is “democratic,” the underlying analysis is that capitalism cannot be merely regulated into justice; it must be transformed.

Most DSA elected officials operate inside the Democratic Party and pursue incremental policies. That is tactical. The organization’s stated end-goal is not a slightly more generous Nordic-style welfare capitalism; it is a break with private ownership as the dominant form of economic organization. Whether that would remain “democratic” once achieved is the open historical question the record of prior experiments answers poorly.

In short: people support it because it feels morally righteous and offers belonging. The economic and institutional direction it points toward has been tried, under varying conditions, and repeatedly produced stagnation, shortages, repression, or collapse when taken seriously. That gap between moral self-image and empirical results is what makes the persistence of the project striking.

Socialism vs. Communism: The Core Differences

|Feature|Socialism|Communism|
|---|---|---|
|**Economic Control**|Major industries (energy, healthcare, banking) are publicly owned; private property and small business can co-exist.|All property, industry, and resources are collectively owned; private property is abolished entirely.|
|**Distribution**|Wealth and goods are distributed based on individual contribution and effort ("To each according to their contribution").|Goods are distributed purely based on need, regardless of individual output ("To each according to their need").|
|**Political System**|Can operate within a multi-party democratic framework; changes are made through voting and reforms.|Historically relies on a single-party state and authoritarian rule; achieved through revolutionary overthrow.|
|**The State**|The state remains an active, organized entity to manage public services and distribute resources fairly.|In theory, the state eventually "withers away" into a classless, moneyless, borderless society.|

Is the DSA Longing for that Direction?

To understand if the DSA is moving toward these systems, it is necessary to separate their **public political messaging** from their **official organizational platform**.

1. The Public Image: Social Democracy

In regular political campaigns, DSA politicians (like Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez or Rashida Tlaib) usually champion policies common in Western Europe. They campaign for a robust welfare state, universal healthcare (Medicare for All), free college, and stronger labor unions. This is technically **Social Democracy**—a system that keeps capitalism but heavily taxes it to fund social safety nets. [[1](https://www.cityandstateny.com/politics/2024/07/real-story-behind-dsas-decision-unendorse-aoc/398024/), [2](https://jacobin.com/2025/09/dsa-convention-mamdani-gaza-socialism), [3](https://reason.com/2019/07/06/socialism-is-back-and-the-kids-are-loving-it/), [4](https://www.vox.com/policy-and-politics/2018/6/27/17509604/alexandria-ocasio-cortez-democratic-socialist-of-america), [5](https://www.maciverinstitute.com/perspectives/dsas-socialist-wave-hits-wisconsin)]

2. The Official Platform: Democratic Socialism

The DSA's formal, internal platform goes much further than European social democracy. Their explicit goal is to **end capitalism entirely**. [[1](https://www.niskanencenter.org/inside-the-democratic-socialist-uprising/), [2](https://convergencemag.com/podcast/envisioning-a-united-socialist-future-a-conversation-with-dsas-national-co-chairs-ashik-siddique-and-megan-romer/), [3](https://www.facebook.com/RollingStone/posts/zohran-mamdani-on-whether-democratic-socialists-and-centrists-can-coexisti-think/1395198165801579/), [4](https://www.facebook.com/washingtonpost/posts/analysis-democratic-socialists-have-been-winning-in-new-york-colorado-and-washin/1408884241103447/)]

- **Moving Toward Socialism**: The DSA platform openly longs for a socialist economy. They advocate for worker-owned cooperatives, the nationalization of major corporate sectors, and the decommodification of survival needs (housing, food, healthcare). They believe capitalism is inherently exploitative and cannot be fixed with simple reforms. [[1](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DYmzb66xdrp/), [2](https://www.instagram.com/reel/Da3j118yM9O/), [3](https://www.governing.com/politics/why-americans-neednt-fear-a-dose-of-democratic-socialism), [4](https://www.wunc.org/2018-07-26/what-you-need-to-know-about-the-democratic-socialists-of-america), [5](https://iep.utm.edu/socialis/)]
- **Rejecting Soviet-Style Communism**: The DSA explicitly rejects the authoritarian, single-party communism seen in the Soviet Union or Maoist China. The "Democratic" in their name is a deliberate boundary. They believe that economic collectivization must only happen alongside a multi-party democracy, free elections, and protected civil liberties. [[1](https://polsci.institute/political-theory/critiques-of-marxism-democratic-socialism/), [2](https://www.facebook.com/TheHill/posts/cnns-chief-white-house-correspondent-kaitlan-collins-pushed-back-on-president-do/1381840793804338/)]

The Mechanism of Polarization: How Entryism Works

Entryism does not require a fringe movement to win over a majority of the population. Instead, a disciplined, highly motivated minority leverages the unique structure of American politics to reshape a major party from within. [[1](https://www.aei.org/op-eds/dsa-vanguard-of-the-radical-professionals/), [2](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/07/dsa-socialist-leftist-takeover/687989/), [3](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/10/magazine/american-socialism-democratic-party.html)]

- **Primary Infiltration**: Because general election turnout is high but primary election turnout is notoriously low, a small, highly organized faction can disproportionately influence who wins the nomination. [[1](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/07/dsa-socialist-leftist-takeover/687989/), [2](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/815944), [3](https://quizlet.com/396401705/unit-6-flash-cards/)]
- **The Hostage Dilemma**: Once an insurgent candidate wins the primary, the parent party faces a brutal choice: back the radical nominee to prevent the opposing party from winning, or abandon them and guarantee defeat. Parties almost always choose to back the insurgent, which slowly legitimizes the radical faction. [[1](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/07/dsa-socialist-leftist-takeover/687989/)]
- **The Overton Window Shift**: As more insurgents take office, ideas that were once considered politically unthinkable gradually shift into mainstream legislative debate. [[1](https://www.instagram.com/reel/Da6RrTnSprF/), [2](https://goodparty.org/blog/article/how-overton-window-affects-politics-policy), [3](https://www.dangerousspeech.org/libraries/neo-nazis-and-white-supremacists-are-running-for-u-s-congress-in-2018)]

Historical Examples of Radicalizing Entryism

History shows that entryism frequently functions as a ratchet mechanism—once a political party shifts toward radicalism, it rarely shifts back without a severe electoral collapse. [[1](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/07/dsa-socialist-leftist-takeover/687989/)]

- **The Militant Tendency (UK, 1970s–1980s)**: A Trotskyist group used entryism to infiltrate the British Labour Party. They successfully seized control of local party branches and major city councils, shifting Labour so far to the left that the party became electorally unviable for a generation, enabling nearly two decades of Conservative rule under Margaret Thatcher. [[1](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/07/dsa-socialist-leftist-takeover/687989/), [2](https://www.sourcewatch.org/index.php/Entryism)]
- **The Goldwater Insurgency (US, 1964)**: Right-wing activists staged an internal coup within the Republican Party to nominate Barry Goldwater. While Goldwater suffered a landslide defeat, his insurgent movement laid the ideological and organizational groundwork for the Reagan Revolution and the modern conservative movement. [[1](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-american-studies/article/donald-trump-and-the-turn-to-rightwing-populism-in-the-republican-party-19902024/0F2E019C9441E9A0BBF6009211CF31A5), [2](https://modernagejournal.com/trump-conservative-counterrevolution/254439/), [3](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/04/11/magazine/i-thought-i-understood-the-american-right-trump-proved-me-wrong.html), [4](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/postwarera/1970s-america/a/conservative-challenges-in-the-1960s-and-1970s)]

The Historical Verdict: Is an Ever-Shifting Movement Inevitable?

Political analysts argue that the DSA's entryism is triggering an asymmetric but deeply parallel version of what happened to the Republican Party. [[1](https://www.instagram.com/reel/Da6RrTnSprF/), [2](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/07/dsa-socialist-leftist-takeover/687989/)]

The primary danger highlighted by historical trends is **ideological escalation**. In order for insurgent movements to maintain their energy, passion, and fundraising base, they must constantly push boundaries. What begins as a demand for universal healthcare gradually escalates into demands for state-owned grocery stores, the abolition of prisons, and the deconstruction of the U.S. Constitution. [[1](https://www.yakimaherald.com/opinion/opinion-the-dsa-platform-carries-a-strong-stench-of-1917/article_7ac97ca1-2362-4bf6-b09e-1d4dafb7c610.html), [2](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/07/dsa-socialist-leftist-takeover/687989/)]

While the DSA currently lacks the nationwide numbers to win a presidential election, historical trends suggest that their strategy is exceptionally effective at shifting the ideological baseline of the Democratic Party, ensuring that Western political discourse will continue to polarize further away from the center. 1. Weaponize the Primary: Show Up

The DSA wins because they understand that primary elections suffer from notoriously low voter turnout. In many urban districts, a candidate can win a primary with as little as 15% to 20% of registered voters participating. [[1](http://www.cpreview.org/articles/2024/7/direct-democracy-imperiled-analyzing-attacks-on-the-initiative-in-south-dakota), [2](https://www.cityandstateny.com/politics/2019/10/the-party-of-gentrifiers-is-moving-into-brooklyn/176830/), [3](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/10-tips-tackling-political-polarization-workplace-beyond)]

- **Vote in June/August, Not Just November**: The general election is irrelevant if the moderate option is already eliminated. You must treat the Democratic primary as the real election.
- **Organize Low-Turnout Demography**: DSA campaigns rely heavily on younger, highly ideological voters. Moderates can successfully counter this by mobilizing reliable, high-turnout voting blocs—such as older voters, suburban families, and church communities—who generally favor pragmatic governance over structural revolution. [[1](https://sfarchive.dsausa.org/issues/winter-2022/how-can-the-left-win-in-working-class-america/), [2](https://cityjournal.substack.com/p/are-dsa-mayors-the-future-of-the), [3](https://www.city-journal.org/article/san-francisco-moderates-score-big-wins), [4](https://informationmatters.eu/how-to-avoid-disinformation-regarding-voting-and-campaigns/)]

2. Force the Focus on Local Governance

DSA candidates frequently campaign on grand, national ideological narratives (anti-capitalism, foreign policy, geopolitical conflicts). Moderates win when they force the debate back to practical municipal management.

- **Demand Competency Over Ideology**: Pivot the debate toward tangible quality-of-life issues like fixing public transit, filling potholes, managing city budgets, and lowering crime.
- **Highlight Real-World Tradeoffs**: When DSA candidates propose abstract policies like "abolishing prisons" or state-run retail, moderates must directly challenge them on the immediate logistical fallout, tax increases, and job losses that would affect local working-class families.

3. Build Moderate Coalition Infrastructure

Historically, moderate Democrats have relied on the national party establishment to protect them, whereas the progressive left has built its own aggressive, grassroots fundraising and volunteering machines. To win, moderates must replicate this infrastructure. [[1](https://www.semafor.com/article/04/29/2026/progressive-groups-we-dont-need-the-democratic-party-to-cause-a-blue-wave), [2](https://www.compactmag.com/article/why-the-democratic-party-cant-moderate/)]

- **Fund Counter-Weight Organizations**: Support groups explicitly designed to protect moderate incumbents and elevate pragmatic candidates, such as the Democratic Majority for Israel (DMFI), New Democrat Coalition, or localized centrist political action committees.
- **Leverage Labor and Small Business Partnerships**: While the DSA captures the backing of certain progressive unions, moderates can build powerful counter-coalitions by partnering with local business owners, chambers of commerce, and traditional building-trades unions who favor economic stability and infrastructure growth over radical market disruption. [[1](https://www.vox.com/america-actually/492599/new-york-7th-district-primary-antonio-reynoso-claire-valdez-nydia-velazquez-mamdani)]

4. Co-opt the Populist Energy

Voters often turn to the DSA not because they are deeply read in Marxist theory, but because they are genuinely frustrated by systemic issues like unaffordable housing or healthcare costs. [[1](https://redstarcaucus.org/zenithv3-dsacadre/), [2](https://time.com/5774891/jon-favreau-democrats-swing-voters-the-wilderness/), [3](https://time.com/5422714/what-is-democratic-socialism/)]

- **Offer Pragmatic Alternatives**: Simply saying "no" to the DSA is a losing strategy. Moderate candidates must offer clear, actionable, market-backed alternatives. For example, instead of advocating for rent control or the socialization of housing, a moderate should aggressively push for zoning deregulation to rapidly build more private supply.
- **Define "Real Progress"**: Frame moderate policies as the only path to _actual_ results, labeling the DSA’s sweeping, unconstitutional platform as a recipe for legislative gridlock that delivers zero tangible help to struggling families.
