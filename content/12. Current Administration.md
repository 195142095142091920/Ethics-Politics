#### Zohran Mamdani
**Mayor of New York City (in office January 1, 2026)** · **Grade: Directionally opposed**

**Status as of Nov 19, 2026:** Open. Mayor Mamdani was scheduled to sign Romanch's Law at City Hall on November 19, 2026; the horse-carriage drivers' union said it would challenge the law in federal court immediately after signing.

Zohran Mamdani is New York City's first Muslim mayor and a member of the [[11. Modern Political Parties#Democratic Socialists of America and the Progressive Wing|Democratic Socialists of America]]. That page already grades DSA as not directionally aligned. His [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Socialism|socialism]] and his [[4. Religion & Secularism#Islam and Extremism|Islam]] each trigger the [[4. Religion & Secularism#^office-bar|office bar]]: adherents of those [[Glossary Index#^g-excluded-ideologies|excluded ideologies]] should not hold sworn authority roles. The bar is a [[Glossary Index#^g-civic-disabilities|civic disability]], not a penalty for belief. Private belief, speech, and home practice stay free.

The overlaps are real. He has said he will sign Romanch's Law, the City Council bill that ends the horse-carriage industry by June 2028, and on November 17, 2026 he announced a worker transition plan, including medallion buybacks and job training. As of November 17 the bill was still unsigned; unless he signs or vetoes it, the 30-day deadline is November 21, after which it becomes law without a signature. That direction fits [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Animal Rights and Sentientism|Animal Rights and Sentientism]] and [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Animal Cruelty Law|Animal Cruelty Law]].

He kept Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch, did not defund the NYPD, and through October 2026 the department reported the fewest shooting incidents, shooting victims, and murders for the first ten months of any year in its records. Shooting incidents had already reached a then-record low in 2025, so the decline predates his term. In March 2026 he and Commissioner Tisch made it departmental policy to release body-camera footage of critical incidents within 30 calendar days, formalizing a practice already used under Tisch. That overlaps with the body-camera requirement in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Police Power and Accountability|Police Power and Accountability]].

The conflicts run the other way, and they set the direction. In June 2026 the Rent Guidelines Board, most of whose members he appointed, froze rents on one- and two-year rent-stabilized leases covering about one million apartments, effective October 1, 2026 through September 30, 2027. I oppose rent control ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness|Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness]]).

He is opening five city-run grocery stores, with a 30 percent discount on a core basket that includes all produce, meat, and seafood. The first is planned for 2027, and all five by 2029. City-run stores conflict with the limited economic role I want for the state ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Economic System|Economic System]]), and discounting meat conflicts with ending animal agriculture ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Animal Rights and Sentientism|Animal Rights and Sentientism]]).

He sought higher taxes on high earners and large corporations. Those hikes were not enacted; Governor Hochul opposed them. He did win a surcharge on second homes worth more than $1 million. I want a low [[Glossary Index#^g-flat-tax|flat tax]] ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Taxation|Taxation]]).

On February 6, 2026, Executive Order 13 barred federal immigration officers from using city-owned lots as staging or processing sites without a formal agreement or a warrant. In November 2026 he demanded that ICE stop using city lots and threatened legal action. Entry should be closed by default, with enforcement ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]).

On his first day, as part of revoking a set of his predecessor's executive orders, he withdrew the city's use of the IHRA working definition of antisemitism and the ban on city-agency boycotts of Israel. In June 2026 he said he cannot tell you he supports Israel as a Jewish state; he has said he supports Israel as a state with equal rights for all. I support Israel ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Israel and the Middle East|Israel and the Middle East]]).

He campaigned on free buses, universal childcare from six weeks to age five, and a $30 [[Glossary Index#^g-minimum-wage|minimum wage]] by 2030. Free buses were not implemented in 2026. Childcare progress so far is a limited state-funded program for 2-year-olds, not the full promise. A $30 wage floor by 2030 has been introduced in the City Council but is not law. I would abolish the minimum wage ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Labor and Unions|Labor and Unions]]), and public pre-K is left out of my K-12 funding ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#K-12 Funding|K-12 Funding]]).

##### Directional Alignment
**Directionally opposed.** The overlaps on the carriage ban, on keeping the police commissioner and the force funded, and on body-camera release are real. Sentient rights carry massive weight in my grading, and the carriage ban is why the grade is Directionally opposed rather than Opposed. Democratic socialism, the office bar, rent control, city-run groceries, higher taxes, limits on immigration enforcement, and his position on Israel all run against my framework.

#### Kathy Hochul
**Governor of New York (re-elected November 3, 2026, to a second full term)** · **Grade: Directionally opposed**

**Status as of Nov 19, 2026:** Resolved. Governor Hochul defeated Bruce Blakeman on November 3, 2026, and begins a second full term in January 2027.

Kathy Hochul is a Democrat and New York's governor since 2021. I already note, in the Mamdani entry, that she blocked his proposed state tax hikes on high earners and large corporations in 2026. That is a real overlap with the low [[Glossary Index#^g-flat-tax|flat tax]] I want ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Taxation|Taxation]]).

The overlaps go a little further. She has put record sums into law enforcement, including more than $900 million added in the FY 2027 budget, and she restored some judicial discretion on bail while keeping the 2019 bail-reform framework. Those steps sit closer to funded policing than to defund, though they stop well short of the mandatory capital sentences I want ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Police and Law Enforcement|Police and Law Enforcement]]; [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Deterrence and the Justification for Capital Punishment|Capital Punishment]]).

The conflicts run the other way, and they set the direction. The same FY 2027 budget enacted a package that bans 287(g) agreements, bars state and local agencies and schools from helping with immigration enforcement, and requires a judicial warrant before immigration officers can enter non-public areas of state or municipal buildings. Sanctuary rules should be abolished, and entry should be closed by default, with enforcement ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]; [[Glossary Index#^g-sanctuary-cities|Sanctuary cities]]).

She raised the state [[Glossary Index#^g-minimum-wage|minimum wage]] and indexed it to inflation, is putting the state on a path to universal childcare and statewide pre-K, made school meals free, and sent inflation-refund checks. I would abolish the minimum wage ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Labor and Unions|Labor and Unions]]), and public pre-K is left out of my K-12 funding ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#K-12 Funding|K-12 Funding]]). She also signed a pied-à-terre surcharge on New York City second homes worth $5 million or more, the tax Mamdani did win, and she opposed Mamdani's other hikes rather than flattening the code.

Her administration spent years trying to stop New York City from enforcing its foie gras sales ban. A mid-level state court held in March 2026 that Albany cannot block the city ban, though a separate industry suit still keeps the ban from taking effect. That direction conflicts with ending force-feeding and animal agriculture ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Animal Rights and Sentientism|Animal Rights and Sentientism]]; [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Animal Cruelty Law|Animal Cruelty Law]]).

##### Directional Alignment
**Directionally opposed.** Blocking the Mamdani income and corporate hikes, and funding the police, are real overlaps. They do not change the direction: the 287(g) ban and the rest of the ICE-limits package, the minimum-wage floor, universal childcare and pre-K, and the state's fight against the foie gras ban all run against my framework.

#### Letitia James
**Attorney General of New York (re-elected November 3, 2026, to a third term)** · **Grade: Directionally opposed**

**Status as of Nov 19, 2026:** Open. James won a third term on November 3, defeating Republican Saritha Komatireddy. She faces no pending criminal charges: the October 2025 federal mortgage-fraud indictment was dismissed without prejudice, two later grand juries declined to reindict, and she has denied wrongdoing. The government's appeal, United States v. Letitia James, No. 25-4673 (4th Cir.), was consolidated with the Comey appeal, No. 25-4674, and argued on September 15, 2026. The docket's last entries, dated November 5, 2026, are a counsel substitution; no opinion had been issued as of November 19. Separately, New York's Court of Appeals is scheduled to hear the civil fraud case against Donald Trump, in which an appellate court already threw out the monetary penalty, next year.

Letitia James is a Democrat and has been New York's attorney general since 2019. The mortgage case is unresolved on appeal and is not a basis for this grade; she is presumed innocent of those charges.

The record of the office is the basis. She has used it for repeated lawsuits against federal immigration policy, including challenges involving birthright citizenship, DACA, and immigration-enforcement conditions on transportation funding. Sanctuary rules should be abolished, and entry should be closed by default, with enforcement ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]).

She brought the state's civil fraud case against Trump and his businesses and won a liability finding; the roughly $500 million penalty was later thrown out on appeal, and she is still pursuing the suit. She also won a judgment against the National Rifle Association that required repayment by executives and compliance reforms at the organization. I support the right to keep and bear arms, with background checks and red-flag laws for qualified buyers, not a campaign to disable a civil-rights group ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Gun Rights|Gun Rights]]).

I found no substantial overlap with my positions on borders, capital sentencing, a flat tax, or ending animal agriculture. Antitrust actions against large firms also sit against my view that big should not be illegal ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Antitrust|Antitrust]]).

##### Directional Alignment
**Directionally opposed.** The office's immigration suits and the NRA case run against my framework on borders and on guns. The dismissed mortgage indictment is pending on appeal and does not change that grade.

#### Mike Lawler
**U.S. Representative, New York's 17th District (in office through January 3, 2027)** · **Grade: Directionally aligned**

**Status as of Nov 19, 2026:** Resolved. Lawler conceded to Democrat Cait Conley on November 3, 2026, and remains in office through January 3, 2027.

Mike Lawler is a Republican from Rockland County who has held NY-17 since January 2023. He is often described as one of the more bipartisan members of the House. I vote Republican as the best directional fit ([[11. Modern Political Parties#Republican Party and the MAGA Faction|Republican Party]]).

The overlaps are on the main axes. He voted Yea on H.R. 29, the Laken Riley Act, House Roll Call 6, on January 7, 2025. Entry should be closed by default, with enforcement, and that vote aligns with removing illegal immigrants who commit crimes ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Border Security and Immigration Enforcement|Border Security and Immigration Enforcement]]). In a district with a large Jewish and Orthodox community, coverage of the 2026 race treated him as the pro-Israel incumbent ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Israel and the Middle East|Israel and the Middle East]]). He voted Yea on passage of H.R. 1, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, House Roll Call 145, on May 22, 2025, and Aye on the motion to concur in the Senate amendment, House Roll Call 190, on July 3, 2025. That law included a temporary rise in the state-and-local tax deduction cap to $40,000 for five years for households making up to $500,000, a provision he publicly claimed as a SALT Caucus result. That is closer to tax relief than to progressive-rate hikes, though a deduction for high-tax states is not the low [[Glossary Index#^g-flat-tax|flat tax]] I want ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Taxation|Taxation]]).

The conflicts are narrower, and they do not set the direction. The SALT cap increase is a carve-out, not tax simplification. I found no record that he supports ending animal agriculture, and I found no capital-sentencing record that matches my mandatory-death rule. Those are the usual Republican gaps, not a reversal of direction ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Animal Rights and Sentientism|Animal Rights and Sentientism]]; [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Deterrence and the Justification for Capital Punishment|Capital Punishment]]).

##### Directional Alignment
**Directionally aligned.** The Laken Riley vote and the two H.R. 1 votes run with my framework. The SALT carve-out and the usual Republican silence on animal agriculture are real gaps, and they do not reverse the direction.

#### Abdul El-Sayed
**U.S. Senator-elect from Michigan (takes office January 3, 2027)** · **Grade: Opposed**

**Status as of Nov 19, 2026:** Resolved. The Associated Press called the race for El-Sayed on November 4, 2026. He defeated Republican Mike Rogers and will succeed Gary Peters. He will be the first Muslim member of the U.S. Senate.

Abdul El-Sayed is a Democrat, a physician, and a former Detroit and Wayne County health official. I already set out the full case, including the 2012 Morsi statement, the March 1, 2026 campaign call, and his stated platform, in [[13. News Commentary#Case: Abdul El-Sayed, Michigan Senate race, 2026|Case: Abdul El-Sayed, Michigan Senate race, 2026]]. That case is resolved. This entry is the officeholder grade.

His [[4. Religion & Secularism#Islam and Extremism|Islam]] triggers the [[4. Religion & Secularism#^office-bar|office bar]]: adherents of those [[Glossary Index#^g-excluded-ideologies|excluded ideologies]] should not hold sworn authority roles ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Role of Religion in Government|Role of Religion in Government]]; [[4. Religion & Secularism#Oath of Office and Perjury|Oath of Office and Perjury]]). The bar is a [[Glossary Index#^g-civic-disabilities|civic disability]], not a penalty for belief. Private belief, speech, and home practice stay free. That is my proposal, not current law: Article VI bans religious tests today, which is why I would change the Constitution rather than ignore it. Under my framework he could not hold this seat.

The policy conflicts are independent of the bar, and they set the same direction. He ran on Medicare for All, a wealth tax and higher top rates, abolishing ICE with a pathway to citizenship, an arms embargo on Israel including defensive weapons, and funding UNRWA. I oppose Medicare for All, I want a low [[Glossary Index#^g-flat-tax|flat tax]], entry should be closed by default with enforcement, and I support Israel ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Healthcare|Healthcare]]; [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Taxation|Taxation]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Israel and the Middle East|Israel and the Middle East]]). Domestic Policy & Governance#Socialism|Socialism]]; [[11. Modern Political Parties#Democratic Socialists of America and the Progressive Wing|DSA]]).

The overlaps, set out in the S13 case, are narrower: Ukraine aid, some AI security rules, opposing Citizens United, and protecting speech and peaceful protest. They do not change the direction.

##### Directional Alignment
**Opposed.** The office bar alone would keep him from this seat under my framework. Apart from his faith, Medicare for All, the wealth tax, abolishing ICE, and the Israel arms embargo run against my positions at the foundation. The S13 case remains the full write-up.

#### Banks allowed to check business owners' immigration status
**November 19, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

**Status as of Nov 19, 2026:** Open. Proposed rule published November 19, 2026. Comments are due January 19, 2027.

The Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) proposed changes to the Customer Due Diligence Rule under the Bank Secrecy Act. The rule governs what banks and other covered institutions must learn about the owners of companies that open accounts. The proposal carries out section 3(b) of Executive Order 14406 of May 19, 2026, which warned of "the credit risks posed by the extension of mortgage and auto loans, credit cards, and other consumer credit to the inadmissible and removable alien population." The proposal makes explicit that, "where warranted by other risk indicators or supervisory concerns," an institution "may obtain additional information necessary to resolve material compliance concerns," including information relevant to whether a company's beneficial owners "possess lawful immigration status and employment authorization in the United States." The check is permitted, not required. FinCEN says the proposal "does not specify situations that present heightened risks, instead deferring that judgment to financial institutions." The proposal would also let institutions obtain the required ownership information from third-party sources.

Entry ought to be closed by default and the law enforced ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Border Security and Immigration Enforcement|Border Security and Immigration Enforcement]]), and federal law already bars knowingly hiring a worker who is not authorized to work. Letting a bank confirm that the owners of a company are lawfully present and authorized to work, once other risk indicators are already there, closes one route around those rules. The details differ on privacy. My rule is that collecting personal data needs consent or a clear, limited justification tied to stopping rights violations, and that open-ended collection faces a higher bar ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Privacy Rights|Privacy Rights]]). Tying the check to existing risk indicators is a limit. Leaving every trigger to each bank's judgment, with none written into the rule, is not a clear one. Critics say the rule turns banks into immigration agents. It does not make a bank decide anyone's status or require any check; it lets an institution ask for more information when other risk indicators are already present, which is ordinary know-your-customer work. The final rule ought to name the triggers.

#### Fixed terms for foreign students struck down
**November 16, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

**Status as of Nov 17, 2026:** Open. Vacated by the district court; the administration's appeal of the September stay is before the First Circuit.

A Homeland Security rule published July 17, 2026 would have ended "duration of status," which lets foreign students (F), exchange visitors (J) and foreign journalists (I) stay as long as they keep their status and progress in their programs. In its place it set fixed admission periods capped at four years for students and exchange visitors, with extensions granted at the agency's discretion. It also limited changes of program and joint degrees. The department said fixed terms would let it check periodically whether these visitors are complying and reduce overstays. On November 16, 2026, in *Presidents' Alliance on Higher Education and Immigration v. Department of Homeland Security* (D. Mass., No. 1:26-cv-13799), Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV vacated the rule. He had already postponed it in September. He found the rule exceeded the department's authority and wrote, "Put bluntly, DHS is seeking to prevent or discourage the very activity—providing educational opportunities for foreign students in the United States—that Congress sought to permit and encourage."

Student visas are one of the named entry paths I keep, and every path runs under values vetting ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]). A fixed term with a check before any extension fits that: a visitor is re-examined rather than admitted once and left alone for as long as a program runs. The objection is that four years is too short for many degrees and that discretionary extensions disrupt honest students. That objection lands on the details, not the direction. An extension tied to documented progress and a fresh values check answers it. Rules on changing majors or joint degrees do nothing for vetting, and the court was right that they reach past the purpose of the visa. The cleaner route is for Congress to write fixed terms and periodic re-vetting into the statute, so the policy does not depend on a rule a court can set aside. See also [[12. Current Administration#Student internship limits blocked|Student internship limits blocked]].

#### Labor test for employer-sponsored green cards
**November 16, 2026** · **Grade: Aligned**

**Status as of Nov 17, 2026:** Open. Proposed rule published November 17, 2026 (91 FR 73916). Comments close January 19, 2027.

The Labor Department proposes to rebuild the labor market test that employers must pass before sponsoring a foreign worker for a green card (permanent labor certification, or PERM). It would eliminate Schedule A, the list of occupations exempt from the test, which has named only nurses and physical therapists since 1991. The department says the Immigration and Nationality Act requires a finding that no able, willing, qualified and available U.S. workers exist for each position, so it cannot exempt a whole occupation, and that more than 2,000 comments on an earlier request for information offered no reliable method to keep the list. The rule would also strengthen recruitment requirements, add safeguards for U.S. workers laid off by the sponsoring employer or by third-party staffing firms, widen notice of filing to U.S. workers, and write electronic filing into the regulations. It cites Executive Orders 14159 and 14431.

American labor needs should be met first through the domestic workforce, and only then by immigration, which I rank behind values fit and assimilation ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Skilled-Worker Visas (H-1B)|Skilled-Worker Visas (H-1B)]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]). A test applied to each job, rather than a standing exemption for whole occupations, does that. The strongest objection is the nursing shortage: the Health Resources and Services Administration projects a 10% shortfall of registered nurses in 2027, 24% outside metropolitan areas. The rule does not bar foreign nurses. Where no qualified American applies, the test certifies the job, so a real shortage shows up in the recruitment results. What ends is certification without looking, on a list the department says it could not verify.

#### Highway funds withheld from New York over immigrant truck licenses
**November 13, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

**Status as of Nov 16, 2026:** Open. The Second Circuit set aside the withholding on November 13, 2026. The government can seek rehearing or certiorari.

The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration withheld 4% of New York's fiscal 2027 federal-aid highway funds, about \$73.5 million, because the state issued commercial driver's licenses to non-domiciled immigrants that stayed valid for five or eight years instead of expiring with the holder's lawful-presence documents. In an audit sample of 200 such licenses, 101 outlasted the documents; about 32,600 New Yorkers held one. The agency conceded that the regulations in force when it audited New York contained no expiration-matching requirement. That requirement came later, in a final rule published February 13, 2026, and effective March 16, 2026, after an earlier interim rule was stayed by the D.C. Circuit. In *State of New York v. U.S. Department of Transportation*, the Second Circuit (Judge Nathan, joined by Judge Merriam) granted New York's petition and set the determination aside. Judge Schwartz concurred in the judgment only, writing that the case turns on the text alone. The Transportation Department said, "We've put a stop to the madness," and says about 30,000 such licenses have been canceled nationwide.

A license that lets a non-citizen drive a truck for years after his lawful presence ends is a work permit the law does not give him, and the law ought to be enforced as written ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]). The current rule, which requires these licenses to expire with lawful presence, is the direction I want. But agencies cannot go beyond the rules that authorize them, and a penalty for breaking a requirement that was not yet written fails that test ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Government Structure and Checks|Government Structure and Checks]]). The court was right, and the rule now in force fixes the gap the proper way.

#### Citizenship checks for HUD housing aid
**November 12, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

**Status as of Nov 12, 2026:** Open. Final rule. Takes effect December 14, 2026.

HUD's final rule under Section 214 of the Housing and Community Development Act of 1980 requires every applicant and recipient in covered housing programs to prove U.S. citizenship or eligible immigration status, at any age, ending the exemption for people 62 and older. Prorated aid to "mixed" families, where some members are ineligible, becomes temporary pending verification instead of running indefinitely, and a deferral of termination of up to 18 months stays available. HUD estimates that about 20,000 mixed-status families received assistance in December 2024, about 78 percent of them with children. It rejected grandfathering current families because low turnover would delay moving the aid to fully eligible families on waiting lists by more than a decade.

Non-citizens should not draw housing aid. A green-card holder who takes it should be removed after a court finding ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Removal of Non-Citizen Residents and Loss of Citizenship|Removal of Non-Citizen Residents]]), and the safety floor ought to offer emergency shelter, not housing ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness|Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness]]). Verifying status before paying is the direction I want. Commenters warned that mixed-status families, most with children, would be evicted. HUD's answer holds: there is more demand than aid, so every subsidy kept by an ineligible household is one denied to an eligible family on the waiting list. The details differ: I hold that a U.S.-citizen child keeps any benefit the child qualifies for, while this rule ends a mixed family's whole subsidy after the deferral.

#### Deportations called "too tough"

#### Big Bend border wall
**October 21, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

**Status as of Nov 11, 2026:** Open. On November 9 the Supreme Court stayed the district court's injunction pending a petition for certiorari; construction may resume.

In 2026 the Secretary of Homeland Security issued three waivers under section 102 of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act for border barriers and roads in the Big Bend Sector of Texas, which takes in 517 miles of the Rio Grande, almost a quarter of the southwest border: Secretary Kristi Noem's of February 17 (about 164 miles of barrier), and Secretary Markwayne Mullin's of May 15 (roads and about 17 miles of vehicle barrier inside Big Bend National Park) and July 27 (about 160 miles in the Big Bend and Del Rio sectors). Each found the area one "of high illegal entry," citing more than 89,000 apprehensions between ports of entry in the sector from 2021 to 2025, and waived, within the project areas, laws including the National Environmental Policy Act, the Endangered Species Act, the Clean Water Act and the Migratory Bird Treaty Act. The May and July waivers also list the National Park Service Organic Act. On October 2, U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone halted all construction in the sector "until and unless" the government complies with those laws. Her October 14 opinion found the challengers likely to win under the major questions doctrine: over thirty years the sector's annual apprehensions ran between 0.3% and 2.2% of the border-wide total, and "the Big Bend Sector has the lowest rate of illegal entry along the entire U.S.-Mexico border." On October 21 the Solicitor General asked the Supreme Court to stay her order and summarily reverse it (*Mullin v. Friends of the Ruidosa Church*, No. 26A560). The application argues that the statute allows only constitutional claims against a waiver, and that "leaving a 300-plus-mile gap in border defenses would act as a dangerous magnet for cartels and other bad actors to exploit as other routes close off." On November 9, 2026, the Supreme Court granted the stay in an unsigned opinion, with Justice Jackson noting she would deny it, and declined to summarily reverse. It held the government "exceedingly likely to prevail" on its argument that the district court lacked jurisdiction, because the challengers' claim, "however it is labeled," alleges only that the Secretary exceeded his statutory authority: "There is no such thing as a 'constitutional' major-questions claim." It also cited the government's sworn estimate of "nearly half a million dollars in costs per day" that cannot be recouped, and held that Congress's waiver provision "reflects a clear preference for expeditious border construction" over the aesthetic, recreational and environmental harms the challengers alleged. Construction may proceed while the government's petition for certiorari is pending.

The court's evidence holds on its own terms: by apprehensions, in total and per mile, Big Bend is the least-crossed sector on the border, and the waivers set aside wildlife law inside a national park. That finding goes to whether the statute's "areas of high illegal entry" reaches this sector, which is now before the Supreme Court. My position is a heavily secured and fortified border along its whole length, not only in its busiest sectors ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Border Security and Immigration Enforcement|Border Security and Immigration Enforcement]]), so building here is the direction I want. The details differ on habitat. The United States ought to build in and alongside nature rather than demolishing it, and site extraction away from protected species' habitat ([[9. Energy & Environment#Conservation vs Development|Conservation vs Development]]). A blanket waiver of wildlife law across a national park does not meet that. My rule for barriers in protected habitat is in [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Border Barriers and Protected Habitat|Border Barriers and Protected Habitat]]. The Court's ruling settles who may hear the challenge, not whether a blanket waiver inside a national park is good policy, so my habitat objection stands.

#### Jones Act waiver extended

#### SNAP work-requirement rule
**November 10, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

**Status as of Nov 11, 2026:** Open. Proposed rule. Comments close December 10, 2026.

The Department of Agriculture proposed rules to write the 2025 reconciliation law's SNAP changes into regulation. Adults subject to the time limit can receive SNAP for only three months in 36 unless they work or train 20 hours a week. #### Lower home-buying and refinancing costs
**October 22, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

**Status as of Oct 28, 2026:** Open.

Executive Order 14438 of October 22, 2026, "Decreasing Housing Costs for the American People," directs the Federal Housing Finance Agency to have Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac pilot automatic cancellation of mortgage insurance once a borrower's equity, measured at current market value, meets the requirement, for mortgages made after the pilot is established. Domestic Policy & Governance#Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness|Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness]]). It also serves my goal of young adults affording a home early ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Births and Family Formation|Births and Family Formation]]). The details differ in two places. Domestic Policy & Governance#Government Home Lending|Government Home Lending]]). And tying federal grants to local permitting rules reaches into land use that belongs to states and localities ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Federalism|Federalism]]). The task force only studies this so far.

Federal law already ends private mortgage insurance automatically once the balance is scheduled to reach 78% of the home's *original* value. Measuring equity at *current* value lets owners in rising markets drop it sooner, at no cost to taxpayers. The home builders' trade group, the National Association of Home Builders, estimates that regulation makes up 23.8% of a new home's price, about \$93,870. A trade group has a stake in that number, and the exact share is contested. That does not change my conclusion: rules that protect no one add cost at any share, and removing them lowers the price without a subsidy. Cheaper direct federal loans do the opposite and move the cost onto every taxpayer.

#### Replacement tariffs
**February 24, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally opposed**

**Status as of Nov 11, 2026:** Open.

After the Supreme Court held on February 20, 2026, that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs ([[12. Current Administration#Reciprocal tariffs|Reciprocal tariffs]]), the President imposed a 10% global import surcharge under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, which allows up to 150 days. It expired on July 24, 2026. On July 23 he imposed tariffs of 10% or 12.5% on all goods from 60 economies under Section 301, after the U.S. Trade Representative found they had not banned, or not effectively enforced bans on, imports made with forced labor, with exemptions for some products and for USMCA-compliant imports. A separate Section 301 action sets 25% on Brazil. Section 232 national-security tariffs continue on steel, aluminum, copper, autos, semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, lumber and other goods. On October 23, 2026, a proclamation swapped some products under the Canadian motor-vehicle duties, removing plywood, some paper products, certain machinery and refrigeration equipment and packing crates and adding certain valves, carpets and waste products, effective October 30.

Defenders point out that each of these laws, unlike IEEPA, was written by Congress and requires a finding or an investigation before duties are imposed. That is true, and it is why the grade is directional rather than full opposition. It does not meet my rule. I hold tariffs to be good in theory but questionable in practice, used cautiously and targeted at leverage, strategic industries and national-security threats, and set by Congress, with executive tariffs only under clear, narrow statutory authority, for a stated purpose and a fixed period ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Trade and Economics|Trade and Economics]]). A global surcharge under a balance-of-payments law, which the government's own brief to the Federal Circuit said had no "obvious application" to trade deficits, is not narrow authority. Punishing forced labor is a sound purpose, but a flat rate on every good from 60 economies taxes far more than the goods made with forced labor. And 50% duties on an ally's goods are the broad tariffs I reject, not targeted leverage against a threat.

#### Peace declaration with Russia and China
**November 19, 2026** · **Grade: Mixed**

**Status as of Nov 19, 2026:** Open. A joint statement, not an agreement. The nuclear-risk dialogue it announces has no date.

On November 19, 2026, in Shenzhen on the sidelines of the APEC summit, President Trump met Russia's Vladimir Putin and China's Xi Jinping in the first three-way meeting of the three leaders. The White House released the "U.S.-Russia-China Heads of State Declaration toward Peace." In it the three governments committed to "reform the UN" and said they "plan to increase the production and supply of refined petroleum products." They "recognized that the principle of state consent and respect for immunities of State officials are of paramount importance," and agreed "that the International Criminal Court and other similar bodies must not abuse their jurisdiction." They also "reaffirmed that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought," and said they would hold a dialogue "on reducing nuclear risks and preventing the proliferation of nuclear weapons." The declaration does not mention Ukraine. Before the meeting Trump greeted Putin as "my friend." In his opening remarks he said the three were "uniquely positioned to discuss how to reduce the number of nuclear weapons on Earth" and, on Iran, "We're not going to let Iran have a nuclear weapon." Afterward Putin told reporters that Ukraine and Europe were "not yet ready" to make the compromises needed to end the war, and said that "when it comes to today's conflicts, the realities on the ground will have to be accepted." The International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for Putin in March 2023 for the unlawful deportation of children from occupied Ukraine.

Two points fit my positions. A nuclear-risk and nonproliferation dialogue with the other two largest nuclear powers serves the deterrence I rely on against nuclear states, and Trump tied it to keeping a weapon from Iran ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#North Korea and Nuclear Rogue States|North Korea and Nuclear Rogue States]]; [[12. Current Administration#War with Iran|War with Iran]]). The ICC clause matches my rule that no foreign tribunal holds authority over a nation's sovereign acts, a rule I apply to every nation, not only America ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Global Institutions|Global Institutions]]). The conflict is with whom it was signed and what it leaves out. Russia is an aggressor that the United States ought to weaken by helping the countries that fight it, and Russia and China are adversaries insofar as they work to destabilize the United States ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Russia, Ukraine, and China/Taiwan|Russia, Ukraine, and China/Taiwan]]). A joint "peace" declaration that says nothing about Ukraine lends the aggressor standing, at a moment when Putin is demanding that the "realities on the ground" be accepted, and it asks nothing of him in return. Defenders say talking to rivals is how nuclear war is avoided; Trump said, "Too often past leaders have avoided talking to nations with whom they had disagreements." Talking is right, and I would keep the nuclear channel. But a nuclear dialogue needs no shared statement of friendship. Nothing in this one constrains Russia in Ukraine or China over Taiwan. My sovereignty rule still means the ICC has no claim on Putin; the answer to Russia's war is helping Ukraine win it, not a declaration with the man waging it.

#### Greenland defense agreement
**September 22, 2026** · **Grade: Mixed**

**Status as of Nov 19, 2026:** Open. Denmark's parliament approved the agreement 109–0 on November 19, after Greenland's parliament approved it on November 3. It becomes legally binding once Denmark formally notifies the United States that both parliaments have consented. The terms for each new defense area are still to be negotiated.

The United States, the Kingdom of Denmark and the Government of Greenland signed an agreement in New York amending the 1951 agreement on the defense of Greenland. It allows the United States to modernize and expand Pituffik Space Base and to establish new Defense Areas at Narsarsuaq and Mestersvig on terms still to be mutually agreed, lets either party propose further Defense Areas, and lets U.S. aircraft fly over and land anywhere in Greenland and U.S. public vessels move undersea in its territorial waters. It bars states outside NATO from military installations or a persistent military presence in Greenland, and bars investors from states outside NATO, NATO partners and the EU from control, significant influence or sensitive access in critical infrastructure and resource extraction unless the parties agree. It names a Golden Dome defense system as a shared interest, reaffirms "the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Kingdom of Denmark" and the Greenlandic people's "right to self-determination," has no end date, and requires an independent Greenland to remain in NATO and take on Denmark's obligations under it. No territory changes hands and the agreement grants no U.S. resource rights. It enters into force only after Greenland's and Denmark's parliaments approve it. Greenland's parliament debated it on October 12, 2026 and referred it to committee.

I support this agreement for U.S. security. It expands U.S. defense where Russia and China seek a foothold, keeps their militaries and capital out of a strategic territory ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Military Space|Military Space]]), and comes through an alliance that makes the United States safer without giving up its sovereignty ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Alliances and NATO|Alliances and NATO]]). I want the United States doing what it needs to for its own security. I dislike the pressure tactics and threats against a friendly country that came before it, and I would prefer it had been reached more diplomatically ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Interventionism vs Non-Intervention|Interventionism vs Non-Intervention]], [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sovereignty and Borders|Sovereignty and Borders]]). If the security concern is overwhelming and truly justified, I would still get behind that justification, which is why I land on Mixed rather than lower. The way it was pursued keeps it from Aligned. The Congressional Research Service counts 18 U.S. service members killed and 824 wounded as of September 15, and cites the rights group HRANA's count of 3,636 Iranians killed by April 7, including 1,701 civilians, and a Congressional Budget Office estimate of \$38 billion in operating costs as of August 1. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Interventionism vs Non-Intervention|Interventionism vs Non-Intervention]], [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Nuclear Weapons and WMD|Nuclear Weapons and WMD]]). The details differ in three places:

- **Civilians.** Civilians are absolutely protected, except foreign actors who forfeit protection by hostile conduct ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#^hostile-forfeit|Hostile conduct, removal, and entry]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Laws of War|Laws of War]]), and sparing Iran's people is a requirement. The Minab investigation should be finished and published.
- **The June memorandum.** Lifting all sanctions and a \$300 billion reconstruction plan run against my use of sanctions alongside military deterrence ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Sanctions and Economic Statecraft|Sanctions and Economic Statecraft]]) and against my rule of no nation-building once the regime is destroyed. I judge the current administration too soft on Iran, and the memorandum is the clearest case.

#### Endangered species "harm" narrowed
**September 14, 2026** · **Grade: Opposed**

**Status as of Nov 12, 2026:** Open. In force since September 14, 2026; challenged in court.

The Fish and Wildlife Service and the National Marine Fisheries Service rescinded the regulatory definition of "harm" under the Endangered Species Act, effective September 14, 2026. Since 1981 (FWS) and 1999 (NMFS) it had covered significant habitat modification or degradation that actually kills or injures listed wildlife, and the Supreme Court upheld it in *Babbitt v. Sweet Home* (1995). The same day, FWS Director Brian Nesvik issued a memorandum adopting Justice Scalia's dissent in that case: "take" now reaches only "an affirmative act that is intentionally and purposefully directed at a particular animal." Under it, a ship that strikes a whale by accident has not taken it, and felling a tree is not a take of the bats roosting in it unless the tree is felled to kill them. NOAA's general counsel issued an identical memorandum. On November 11, 42 members of Congress (41 Democrats and one independent) asked Interior and Commerce to explain the legal basis by November 30.

My rules protect each sentient animal, not a species count ([[12. Current Administration#Bear baiting allowed in Alaska preserves|Bear baiting]]), and an animal killed by a foreseeable strike or by the loss of its habitat is just as dead as one shot. Whoever causes that harm ought to answer for it, the way polluters do ([[9. Energy & Environment#Pollution and Liability|Pollution and Liability]], [[9. Energy & Environment#Regulation and Conservation|Regulation and Conservation]]). The agencies argue that after *Loper Bright* a regulation cannot reach past its statute, and I agree that rules carry no more authority than the law behind them ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Government Structure and Checks|Government Structure and Checks]]). The statute itself puts "harm" and "kill" in the definition of "take," though, and the Supreme Court upheld the habitat definition. If the reading is doubtful, Congress ought to write habitat protection into the Act, not leave whales and bats exposed while the agencies narrow it.

#### Firing of prosecutor Maurene Comey ruled unlawful
**October 26, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally opposed**

**Status as of Oct 27, 2026:** Open. The Justice Department says it will appeal. The court reserved judgment on her First Amendment claim.

On July 16, 2025, the Justice Department fired Assistant U.S. Attorney Maurene Comey, a criminal prosecutor in the Southern District of New York for nearly ten years and the daughter of former FBI Director James Comey, by an email that terminated her "[p]ursuant to Article II of the United States Constitution and the laws of the United States" and gave no other reason. The Department later said then-Attorney General Pam Bondi directed it. The Department argued that the Attorney General could remove her at will and that Congress cannot limit the removal of federal prosecutors at all. On October 26, 2026, in *Comey v. Department of Justice* (No. 25-cv-7625), U.S. District Judge Jesse Furman held that the firing violated the Civil Service Reform Act, which allows removal "only for such cause as will promote the efficiency of the service" and only after notice and a chance to be heard, as well as the separation of powers and her Fifth Amendment due-process rights. He wrote that the Department's Article II argument "flies in the face of nearly two centuries of Supreme Court precedent" on Congress's power to limit the removal of inferior officers, and ordered retroactive reinstatement and back pay through December 20, 2025, when she took another job. He did not decide her claim that she was fired because of her father or her perceived politics. A Department spokesperson said it "disagrees with this decision and will appeal in due course."

The government ought to let elected officials control the people who set policy, and the Attorney General and the U.S. Attorneys, who direct prosecutions, serve at the President's pleasure for that reason. Career staff are different: no one is fired for party or political speech, and removal for poor performance or misconduct comes with written reasons, a chance to answer, a decision within 30 days and one quick appeal ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Civil Service Protections|Civil Service Protections]]). The Department's strongest point is that prosecutors wield the executive power itself, so the President must be able to remove any of them. A line prosecutor carries out charging policy that politically accountable officials set and can change at will. Control runs through them, not through firing career staff without a stated reason. Here the Department gave none, no notice and no hearing, and claimed a power to do the same to every prosecutor in the country. That is the at-will reach into ordinary career staff that my rule calls the spoils system by another name, and it fails my due-process rule whatever the motive was ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Due Process and Rule of Law|Due Process and Rule of Law]]). Whether she was fired because of her father is not proven, and I don't treat it as proven. The grade may change after the appeal. The same at-will theory, applied to policy-influencing positions, is graded in [[12. Current Administration#Schedule Policy/Career|Schedule Policy/Career]].

#### Suit to hold Alaska's Senate certification
**October 22, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally opposed**

**Status as of Nov 14, 2026:** Open. Dismissed for lack of standing on November 1. The Justice Department has appealed to the Ninth Circuit.

On October 22, 2026, the Justice Department sued Alaska and its election officials in federal court (*United States v. State of Alaska*, No. 3:26-cv-00376, D. Alaska). The complaint says Daniel J. Sullivan, a first-time candidate in the U.S. Senate race, is running "with the intention of misleading supporters of Senator Sullivan," Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan, and that his "sole purpose" is to help Democrat Mary Peltola. Alaska's Division of Elections had found in June that his candidacy was filed "to confuse or mislead" and kept him off the ballot, but a state trial court reversed it and the Alaska Supreme Court summarily affirmed. The suit, brought under the National Voter Registration Act's purpose of protecting "the integrity of the electoral process," asks the court to bar certification of any result in which Sen. Sullivan loses but would have won had every vote for Daniel J. Sullivan gone to him, pending an investigation into whether those votes "were meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan." In the August primary Peltola had 49.5%, Sen. Sullivan 41.4% and Daniel J. Sullivan 2.5%. On the sample ballot he appears as "Sullivan, Daniel J. Jr." with no party listed, above "Sullivan, Dan S. (Registered Republican) Incumbent." President Trump said of the suit, "I'm the one that said, 'do it.'" Sen. Lisa Murkowski said the department "should never have filed this lawsuit."

On November 1, Judge Sharon L. Gleason dismissed the suit, holding that the Justice Department lacked standing because it "failed to satisfy its burden to show that its alleged injury is redressable by a court order." She wrote that it offered "no workable plan as to how it proposes to segregate ballots cast for Daniel J. Sullivan on Election Day and then conduct a court-ordered unspecified 'investigation' of those ballots in a manner that would preserve voter secrecy." The department appealed the same day, saying the court had not disputed its claim that Daniel J. Sullivan "entered the race as a frivolous candidate to confuse voters." It had amended the suit to cite a New York Times report that people tied to Peltola's campaign helped recruit him, which her campaign denies, and it is separately investigating the matter criminally. No one has been charged. On November 9 voting-rights groups, including the League of Women Voters and Common Cause, moved to dismiss the appeal. As of that date the department had not asked the appeals court for an injunction.

A candidacy filed to fool voters is a fraud on them, and a state should be able to keep one off the ballot under rules written in advance. Alaska's own election director found exactly that. So the concern is real. The remedy is what I oppose. Election rules are fixed in law before each election, not changed during or after it ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Election Administration|Election Administration]]), and this suit asks a court to hold up a count only if one named candidate loses, then to guess what voters "meant." Ranked-choice voting, which I would require everywhere, already answers the spoiler worry: a voter who ranks both Sullivans loses nothing, because a vote for an eliminated candidate moves to the voter's next choice ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Ranked-Choice Voting|Ranked-Choice Voting]]). Supporters of the suit will say a one-in-forty share could swing a race decided by a point or two. If so, the fix is a clear ballot-access rule before the next election, not a federal suit under a voter-registration law to reach into a state's certification ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Federalism|Federalism]]). It also runs against my rule for federal enforcement near an election ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Federal Enforcement Near Elections|Federal Enforcement Near Elections]]). The grade is directional rather than full opposition because the decoy problem it points at is genuine. My rule for deceptive candidacies is in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Ballot Access and Deceptive Candidacies|Ballot Access and Deceptive Candidacies]].

#### Schedule Policy/Career
**January 20, 2025** · **Grade: Mixed**

**Status as of Nov 16, 2026:** Open.

Executive Order 14171 reclassifies policy-influencing career positions so they can be removed more easily. Executive Order 14410 of June 3, 2026 carried it out, moving about 8,000 positions into Schedule Policy/Career, 97% of them at GS-15 or Senior Level. The positions keep career status and merit hiring but become removable at will for poor performance, misconduct, corruption or subverting presidential directives. Unions and nonprofit groups have sued, and no court has ruled on the merits.

Accountability and a smaller workforce match my position ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Public-Sector Size and Civil Service|Public-Sector Size and Civil Service]]). The government ought to limit at-will status to genuinely policy-setting positions and keep a firm ban on firing anyone for party or political speech ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Civil Service Protections|Civil Service Protections]]). I set the condition here that if the order reaches ordinary career staff rather than genuinely policy-setting roles, the grade drops. Supporters say these are senior roles that shape policy, and many are. But 8,000 positions chosen largely by pay grade, not by a showing that each one sets policy, reach past the policy-setting roles my rule allows, so the grade drops to Mixed. The same at-will theory applied to a career prosecutor was rejected in court in [[12. Current Administration#Firing of prosecutor Maurene Comey ruled unlawful|Firing of prosecutor Maurene Comey ruled unlawful]].

#### Remote access to advanced AI chips restricted
**November 16, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

**Status as of Nov 19, 2026:** Open. Published in the Federal Register on November 19, 2026 (91 FR 74970) and effective that day. Comments are due January 19, 2027. Data centers already supplying significant computing power to companies headquartered in Country Group D:5 or Macau must obtain a license within 90 days of the effective date.

The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security released an interim final rule, which it says was "written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council," to stop companies headquartered in China, Macau or other arms-embargoed countries (Country Group D:5) from renting advanced American chips through data centers in third countries. Exporting those chips to a data center that supplies significant computing power to such companies now requires a license, and data centers already serving them must obtain one within 90 days; commercial recommender systems are exempt. The rule also requires a license to ship the most advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil unless the buyer is headquartered in the United States and meets reporting requirements, tightens the presumptions for chip foundries and packagers, and formally rescinds the never-enforced AI Diffusion Rule. It follows the Nvidia scandal: Megaspeed, a Singapore company that was one of Nvidia's biggest customers in Southeast Asia, is under U.S. criminal investigation for allegedly smuggling American chips into China, and Treasury Secretary Bessent blamed Commerce for not acting against it sooner.

Restrictions on technology transfer should target adversaries and national-security threats such as China ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Sanctions and Economic Statecraft|Sanctions and Economic Statecraft]]), and AI's national-security risks are a federal matter ([[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#AI and Automation|AI and Automation]]). Closing the rental route to chips that cannot be shipped to China directly is that. The details differ in two places. The new license requirement falls on trading partners that are not adversaries, where I would restrict trade only for a national-security threat ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Trade and Economics|Trade and Economics]]); a case for it rests on documented diversion through those countries, and the Singapore case supports it for that route. And the rule took effect before any public comment. The rescission of the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule only formalizes what Commerce announced in May 2025; that rule was never enforced.

#### Renaming AI "Super Intelligence"
**September 29, 2026** · **Grade: Mixed**

**Status as of Nov 8, 2026:** Open.

Executive Order 14434 makes it the policy of the administration that, "to the maximum extent permitted by law," the executive branch uses "Super Intelligence" and "SI" in place of "Artificial Intelligence" and "AI" in official correspondence, websites, reports and other non-statutory documents. It keeps the statutory definition of artificial intelligence, leaves existing regulations, contracts and grants unchanged, and directs the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology to propose, within 60 days, legislative language for a federal definition.

The order changes words, not rules, and I have no settled position on what the government calls the technology. Asking Congress for a statutory definition matches my view that national AI rules should be set by Congress in statute rather than by executive order ([[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#AI and Automation|AI and Automation]]). The new name fits less well: I treat a dangerous superintelligence as an existential risk to prepare against, and applying the term to today's systems blurs that line.
