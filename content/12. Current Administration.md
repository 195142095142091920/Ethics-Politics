Grades reflect my positions, set out in the sections linked from each entry; descriptions are drawn from the official text of each action.

#### Big Bend border wall
**October 21, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

In 2026 the Secretary of Homeland Security issued three waivers under section 102 of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act for border barriers and roads in the Big Bend Sector of Texas, which takes in 517 miles of the Rio Grande, almost a quarter of the southwest border: Secretary Kristi Noem's of February 17 (about 164 miles of barrier), and Secretary Markwayne Mullin's of May 15 (roads and about 17 miles of vehicle barrier inside Big Bend National Park) and July 27 (about 160 miles in the Big Bend and Del Rio sectors). Each found the area one "of high illegal entry," citing more than 89,000 apprehensions between ports of entry in the sector from 2021 to 2025, and waived, within the project areas, laws including the National Environmental Policy Act, the Endangered Species Act, the Clean Water Act and the Migratory Bird Treaty Act; the May and July waivers also list the National Park Service Organic Act. On October 2, U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone halted all construction in the sector "until and unless" the government complies with those laws. Her October 14 opinion found the challengers likely to win under the major questions doctrine: over thirty years the sector's annual apprehensions ran between 0.3% and 2.2% of the border-wide total, and "the Big Bend Sector has the lowest rate of illegal entry along the entire U.S.-Mexico border." On October 21 the Solicitor General asked the Supreme Court to stay her order and summarily reverse it (*Mullin v. Friends of the Ruidosa Church*, No. 26A560). The application argues that the statute allows only constitutional claims against a waiver, and that "leaving a 300-plus-mile gap in border defenses would act as a dangerous magnet for cartels and other bad actors to exploit as other routes close off."

The court's evidence holds on its own terms: by apprehensions, in total and per mile, Big Bend is the least-crossed sector on the border, and the waivers set aside wildlife law inside a national park. That finding goes to whether the statute's "areas of high illegal entry" reaches this sector, which is now before the Supreme Court. My position is a heavily secured and fortified border along its whole length, not only in its busiest sectors ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Border Security and Immigration Enforcement|Border Security and Immigration Enforcement]]), so building here is the direction I want. The details differ on habitat. I build in and alongside nature rather than demolishing it, and I site extraction away from protected species' habitat ([[9. Energy & Environment#Conservation vs Development|Conservation vs Development]]); a blanket waiver of wildlife law across a national park does not meet that. My rule for barriers in protected habitat is in [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Border Barriers and Protected Habitat|Border Barriers and Protected Habitat]]. The grade may change after the Supreme Court acts.

#### Enforcement at houses of worship
**October 20, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

On January 20, 2025, the Department of Homeland Security rescinded the long-standing "sensitive locations" policy, letting immigration officers use "discretion" and "common sense" to make arrests at places such as churches, mosques and synagogues; ICE now lets supervisors approve operations "at or near" them case by case. On October 20, 2026, in *Mennonite Church USA v. DHS*, a 2–1 panel of the D.C. Circuit (Judges Pan and Wilkins; Judge Rao dissenting) held that 27 Christian and Jewish groups likely have standing to challenge the change and sent the case back to the district court to reconsider a preliminary injunction. The policy stays in effect for now.

I would remove illegal immigrants forcefully and allow no sanctuary that obstructs federal law ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Border Security and Immigration Enforcement|Border Security and Immigration Enforcement]], [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Federalism|Federalism]]), and a place of worship should not shield anyone from the law. That matches the direction of the rescission. I also require due process in each case ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Due Process and Rule of Law|Due Process and Rule of Law]]), and the policy leaves the limits to supervisors' discretion rather than fixed rules, so the details differ. My rule for arrests at places of worship is in [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Enforcement at Houses of Worship|Enforcement at Houses of Worship]].

#### Third-country removals allowed to resume
**September 29, 2026** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

In *Department of Homeland Security v. D.V.D.*, the Supreme Court stayed a district court judgment that had vacated the administration's guidance on removing people with final orders to countries not named in those orders, and agreed to hear the case, with argument set for December 9, 2026. Under the guidance, people can be sent to countries that have promised not to persecute or torture them without further steps; for other countries, the government gives notice and generally waits at least 24 hours for a fear claim. Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson would have denied the stay.

Removal to a safe third country matches my position ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Removal of Non-Citizen Residents and Loss of Citizenship|Removal of Non-Citizen Residents]]). It differs in detail: I would never return anyone to persecution or torture, and I require notice and a real chance to be heard ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Due Process and Rule of Law|Due Process and Rule of Law]]), and a 24-hour window, or none where the country has given assurances, may fall short of that: I count a third country as safe only after review of the person's case, not on assurances alone ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Removal of Non-Citizen Residents and Loss of Citizenship|Removal of Non-Citizen Residents]]). The grade may change after the Court rules.

#### Limits on universal injunctions
**June 27, 2025** · **Grade: Directionally aligned (details differ)**

In *Trump v. CASA*, arising from challenges to Executive Order 14160, the Supreme Court held that federal courts generally may not issue universal injunctions.

The ruling is procedural. I keep court review of executive action ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Due Process and Rule of Law|Due Process and Rule of Law]]), and the ruling narrows the remedy without removing that review. Authority & Civil Liberties#Government Structure and Checks|Government Structure and Checks]]). The ruling matches the first point and leaves class actions open; it differs in detail because it does nothing to speed Supreme Court review.

#### Reciprocal tariffs
**April 2, 2025** · **Grade: Directionally opposed**

Executive Order 14257 imposed a baseline tariff on most imports, with higher country rates, under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA).

I hold tariffs to be good in theory but questionable in practice, used cautiously and targeted at leverage, strategic industries, and national-security threats ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Trade and Economics|Trade and Economics]]). Broad baseline tariffs go beyond that. On February 20, 2026, in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, the Supreme Court held 6–3 that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs, and Executive Order 14389 ended the IEEPA duties the same day. On September 21, 2026, Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins announced steps under the Animal Welfare Act: a 2027 proposal to strengthen care standards for dogs, more than 2,000 "regulatory refresher mailings" to dog-breeding facilities, a new Animal Protection Bureau of enforcement specialists, a planned 20% increase in Inspector General agents working on animal fighting and other matters, and a review within six months of every dog breeder who surrendered a USDA or equivalent state license in the past two years. USDA reported that in 2026 it denied or terminated 8 dog-breeder licenses, suspended 6 and revoked 5, and with the Justice Department pursued a serious-danger case against a breeder. On October 6, in Iowa, Rollins said of past practice, "those days are over. We are now in the active process of shutting down these puppy mills." In the ASPCA's pending suit over USDA's licensing of Iowa breeders, including Steve Kruse (*ASPCA v. Vilsack*, D.D.C. No. 1:24-cv-02682), USDA's lawyers argue, as Iowa Capital Dispatch reports, that none of the Act's enforcement provisions are mandatory, that enforcement is at the agency's discretion, and that the case is moot because three of the breeders no longer hold licenses.

USDA's argument follows settled law: since *Heckler v. Chaney* (1985), an agency's decision not to enforce is presumed to be committed to its discretion and unreviewable, unless the statute itself sets guidelines for enforcement. The enforcement record is also real: in June 2026 the Justice Department obtained a restraining order against one of the Iowa licensees and inspected her kennel, and after the county sheriff seized her dogs she was charged with 34 counts of neglect, to which she has pleaded not guilty. Stronger enforcement against cruel breeders points my way, since I treat cruelty to a sentient animal as a rights violation and a crime ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Animal Cruelty Law|Animal Cruelty Law]], [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Animal Rights and Sentientism|Animal Rights and Sentientism]]). Two things pull the other way. I would shut commercial breeding mills down outright, not license them under better standards ([[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]). And under a law that leaves enforcement wholly to the agency, no one can compel it to act on documented violations. My rule making enforcement a duty is in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Animal Cruelty Law|Animal Cruelty Law]].

#### Voter-status sweeps and ballot demands
**October 21, 2026** · **Grade: Mixed**

On August 31, 2026, according to the court's account of the record, the Department of Homeland Security issued an internal "Surge Initiative Guidance" directing Homeland Security Investigations agents to prioritize field investigations questioning people about their voter eligibility in California, Connecticut, Georgia, Missouri, Nevada, New York, Pennsylvania, Washington and Wisconsin. The target lists drew on about 48.2 million records DHS received "through independent sources" and 50.6 million voter-roll records from the Justice Department, taken from commercial copies for states that would not share their rolls; during the surge, agents were to complete at least 40 reviews, taking no more than twelve minutes to clear a person from a list. On October 15 the Justice Department sued California, asking the court to make the state set aside, before counting, the mail ballots of more than 20,000 people it calls "likely noncitizens" and to verify their citizenship. On October 21, in *California Nurses Association v. Mullin* (N.D. Cal.), Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín enjoined election-related field operations and interviews in the nine states that involve people DHS knows, suspects or should reasonably suspect are U.S. citizens, until polls close on November 3. The order is stayed until 5 p.m. on October 23 to allow an appeal.

The California list has documented errors. Los Angeles County found that only 7,035 of the names had addresses in the county; in a sample of 200, 112 registrations were already canceled or inactive, and where birthplace was meaningfully recorded, 38.9% said they were born in the United States. Acting Deputy Attorney General Trent McCotter said, "There may be some names on there who it turns out ultimately are citizens, we're not perfect." The same week, the Justice Department charged 28 people on the list, all lawful permanent residents. The injunction cites another federal court's finding that noncitizen voting is "exceedingly rare."

Rare is not never, as the charges show, and clean rolls are my position: elections are run so that only eligible voters vote and every valid ballot is counted ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Election Administration|Election Administration]]). I graded the order that sends states lists of confirmed citizens Aligned ([[12. Current Administration#Citizenship verification in elections|Citizenship verification in elections]]), and prosecuting each person who registered or voted unlawfully fits the same rule. Two parts run against it. Election procedures are fixed in law before each election rather than changed during it, and a demand three weeks before Election Day to hold back 20,000 mail ballots, on a list the government says may include citizens, would change the count during the election and hold up valid ballots. Questioning citizens at home on twelve-minute database checks is also not the record matching that cleans a roll. My rule for enforcement near an election is in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Federal Enforcement Near Elections|Federal Enforcement Near Elections]].

It differs in detail: the hearing is closed, run by a White House committee rather than an independent body, and capped at four hours.
