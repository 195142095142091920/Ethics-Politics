## Core Principles
Strong support for technological advancement and transhumanism, constrained only by sentient rights. Free-market and private innovation preferred; government role limited to regulation against existential threats, rights violations, and national security. Opt-in privacy and data rules; animal testing redirected to forfeited rights holders.

## AI and Automation

>[!tldr] In short
> The position is pro-AI but treats it as a serious existential risk, so government regulates it and prepares a defensive AGI. There is no citizen dividend; workers displaced by automation are covered by the [[Glossary#Earnings-only top-up|earnings-only top-up]]. If AI displaces work on a large scale, the answer is broad voluntary ownership of AI capital, falling prices, and the existing safety floor scaled to measured displacement, funded by the same flat tax system.

#### Position
AI is monitored and regulated as part of the government's role in protecting citizens' rights and security, limited to existential and rights-violation risks. If AI is observed becoming an existential threat, a risk serious enough to prepare for, the government focuses on regulating its development and preparing to defend against it. There is no citizen dividend. Workers displaced by automation into lower-paid work are covered by the earnings-only top-up, and those who cannot work by the layered safety floor ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Welfare and Entitlements|Domestic Policy]]). The position is pro-AI, and a defensive AGI will likely be needed to combat any dangerous AGI that emerges. I believe AI's existential and national-security risks are a federal matter, so national rules on them should preempt conflicting state laws, set by Congress in statute rather than by executive order. States keep their ordinary fraud and consumer-protection law, which applies to AI like anything else.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **Geoffrey Hinton, 2023:** the AI pioneer quit Google, saying he now regretted his work, and warned of AI's dangers. BBC News (2023) – [AI 'godfather' Geoffrey Hinton warns of dangers as he quits Google](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-65452940)

> [!question]- "Does a pro-AI stance conflict with treating AI as an existential threat?"
> No. AI offers enormous benefits and also serious risks. Supporting development while regulating against existential danger and building defensive capability addresses both.

> [!question]- "What happens to workers displaced by automation?"
> There is no citizen dividend. A displaced worker who takes lower-paid work draws the earnings-only top-up on what they earn, and it phases out with no cliff as earnings recover. Those who cannot work are covered by the layered safety floor. Owner income from federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum helps fund the top-up rather than being paid out ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Welfare and Entitlements|Welfare and Entitlements]]).

> [!question]- "How is the safety floor funded if AI replaces most jobs and no one has wage income?"
> If AI does most of the work, the money doesn't vanish. It goes to the people who own the AI, as profits, dividends, and capital gains. That income is taxed under the same flat tax as any other income, and business income is still taxed only once ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Taxation|Taxation]]). The government also earns money from federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum, and that money goes first to the earnings-only top-up. As AI and data centers use more spectrum and energy, that money grows. The more households own a share of AI and other businesses, the less they rely on a paycheck, and the less the floor has to cover. Owners also need people to have money, because a business whose customers have no income has no one to sell to.

> [!question]- "Why not a universal basic income?"
> A UBI pays everyone regardless of need, which makes it far more expensive than a floor for those who cannot support themselves, and it requires compulsory redistribution well beyond the minimal floor. The framework keeps the floor targeted. It phases support out gradually as earnings rise, so there is no cliff, and it prefers that people own capital rather than depend on a state check. The same reasoning is why owner income is not paid out as a citizen dividend ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Economic System|Economic System]]).

> [!question]- "Won't AI owners just hoard the gains?"
> Profits that are spent, invested, or paid out are taxed under the flat system. Profits that sit idle earn nothing, so owners have strong reasons to reinvest them or pay them out. Ownership is also open to anyone: shares in AI companies trade publicly, and low-cost index funds let any household own a slice of the whole market. The framework's answer to concentration is wider voluntary ownership, not seizing the firms. It concedes one real risk: if ownership stays highly concentrated and wages fall far, funding even a minimal floor could require a profit tax larger than "minimal" implies.

> [!question]- "What if AI replaces nearly all labor?"
> Then something has to replace wages, and every option has a cost to the framework. The first is a large income funded by taxing the output of AI and capital, through a UBI or a much larger floor. It is the only option that reliably works, but it means redistribution far beyond the minimal floor. The second is a public ownership fund. Alaska's Permanent Fund pays citizens directly, \$1,000 per person in 2025. Norway's fund, worth 22,683 billion kroner in mid-2026, pays nothing to citizens and instead helps fund the government budget. The state must first acquire the stake through taxes or takings, which clashes with the framework's property rules. The third is near-free goods through falling prices. This is speculative, and land and housing are hard to make cheaper. The fourth is new human work in care, the trades, in-person services, and creative fields. Autor's research shows this has happened in past waves of automation, but it is not guaranteed. The framework's choice is the first option, and only when the BLS trigger shows a real takeover of work. Until then, minimal taxes remain the default.

> [!question]- "Isn't building a defensive AGI itself a risk?"
> Yes, and it is accepted because an undefended country facing a dangerous AGI is worse. Defensive AGI is built under the same existential-risk regulation as any other system, under government supervision.

#### Key Elements
- Regulate AI as part of government's protective duty.
- Treat existential risk seriously.
- Prepare a defensive AGI against dangerous AGI.
- No citizen dividend; displaced low earners are covered by the earnings-only top-up.
- For large-scale displacement: broad voluntary ownership of AI capital, falling prices raising real incomes, and the existing safety floor scaled to measured displacement rather than forecasts.
- Funding from the same flat tax on capital income, plus owner income from federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum.
- Default enrollment of every adult in a low-cost private index account, with a free opt-out and no taxpayer seed.
- Capital gains taxed at the same flat rate with the deferral gap closed: heirs take over the original cost basis (carryover basis) instead of a stepped-up basis.
- Trigger: four consecutive quarters with the BLS labor share at least 3 points below its ten-year average and the prime-age employment-to-population ratio at least 2 points below its level three years earlier; capital income first moves from 15% to the 20% flat rate, then a surcharge of up to 5 points after eight quarters, never above 25% including the Sanctuary Levy while it runs, and never on wealth or unrealized gains; each step reverses when the measures recover for four quarters.
- Stated limit: the plan handles gradual or partial displacement; fast, near-total displacement would force a choice about taxing capital beyond "minimal."
- Pro-AI overall.
- National rules on AI's existential and security risks preempt conflicting state laws, by statute from Congress; states keep ordinary fraud and consumer-protection law.

#### Rationale
Protecting citizens from catastrophic threats is a core government function, and advanced AI may become one. In a 2023 survey of 2,778 AI researchers, the median estimate of the chance of extremely bad outcomes such as human extinction was 5%. Federal policy currently aims to promote American AI leadership. A defensive capability ensures that if dangerous AI emerges, it can be countered. Measured job losses from automation have so far been modest (0.2 percentage points of employment per added robot per 1,000 workers), which is why displacement is handled through the existing earnings-only top-up rather than a separate automation payment.

AI may be different, and the framework says so. The IMF estimates that almost 40% of global employment, and about 60% of jobs in advanced economies, is exposed to AI, with roughly half of exposed jobs likely to benefit and half facing lower labor demand. Past automation did not end work. David Autor found that automation "complements labor, raises output in ways that leads to higher demand for labor," and that commentators "tend to overstate the extent of machine substitution." Acemoglu and Restrepo show that automation displaces workers from existing tasks, while new tasks reinstate them. They also found that the slower U.S. employment growth of the past three decades reflects faster displacement and weaker reinstatement. Labor's share of income has already been falling. BLS reports that the labor share of nonfarm business output reached a record low of 52.8% in the second quarter of 2026. Karabarbounis and Neiman document a global decline of about 5 percentage points since the 1970s, about half of it explained by cheaper investment goods such as computers. Autor and coauthors link much of the U.S. decline to sales shifting toward "superstar firms" with low labor shares. Early evidence on AI itself is mixed. Using ADP payroll data, Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen find no evidence of widespread job displacement so far. But employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations stood 19% below where it would have been had it kept pace with less-exposed peers. Acemoglu estimates that AI will raise total factor productivity by no more than about 0.66% over ten years. Whether AI creates enough new tasks is unknown. The framework therefore scales its response to displacement as it is measured, not to forecasts.

Three features carry the response. First, ownership. Anyone may own AI capital through public shares and index funds, and a birth-to-adulthood account already exists in law. Trump Accounts, created by the Working Families Tax Cuts, let parents, relatives, employers, and charities invest for a child in low-cost U.S. index funds. Alaska's Permanent Fund shows that resource wealth can be held for residents over decades, paying \$1,000 per person in 2025. The framework supports voluntary contributions to such accounts but does not adopt a paid-out citizen dividend. Second, prices. Automation lowers the cost of what it produces, so a fixed income buys more, which supports demand even as some jobs shrink. The BLS price index for televisions fell 94% from December 1997 to August 2015, and William Nordhaus estimated that the cost of computation fell by a factor of trillions between 1850 and 2006. Third, the floor. The existing bare-minimum safety floor and earnings-only top-up stay the backstop, funded by the flat tax and owner income. That owner income is not trivial and is likely to grow. FCC spectrum auctions have raised more than \$233 billion since 1994, and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimates that data centers' share of U.S. electricity use will rise from 4.4% in 2023 to between 6.7% and 12% by 2028.

Three additions turn these features into rules. First, default ownership. Every adult is enrolled by default in a low-cost private index account, through an employer or on the tax return, with a free and simple opt-out and no taxpayer seed. It extends the default enrollment already used for retirement savings ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Social Security and Medicare Transition|Social Security and Medicare Transition]]), where Madrian and Shea found that 401(k) participation rose from 37% under opt-in to 86% under automatic enrollment. The state does not run or hold the accounts. The accounts supplement income and would never replace wages. For example, at a 4% annual payout, a \$10,000 account yields about \$400 a year and a \$100,000 account about \$4,000. In fast displacement, it is the safety floor, not the accounts, that secures basic needs. Second, capital income is actually collected. Capital gains keep the same flat treatment, but the gap that lets gains escape income tax entirely is closed. Today an heir's cost basis is "stepped up" to the value at death, so gains built up during the owner's life are never taxed. The Joint Committee on Taxation estimates this exclusion at \$336 billion in forgone revenue for fiscal years 2024 to 2028. Under carryover basis, the heir keeps the original cost basis and pays the ordinary flat tax only when the asset is sold. Nothing is taxed at death, so this is not an estate or inheritance tax, which the framework rejects ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#**Taxation**|Taxation]]). JCT estimated that an earlier version of this change would raise about \$105 billion over ten years. Third, a measured trigger, with a ceiling. The floor and top-up expand only when two BLS measures both show displacement for four consecutive quarters:
- the labor share of nonfarm business output (BLS Productivity and Costs) stands at least 3 percentage points below its average over the previous ten years; and
- the prime-age (25 to 54) employment-to-population ratio (BLS Current Population Survey) stands at least 2 points below its level three years earlier. It was 80.7% in September 2026.

Requiring both screens out ordinary recessions, in which employment falls but the labor share usually rises. Forecasts alone never trigger it.

When the trigger is met, the response comes in steps:
- **Step 1:** owner income goes to the top-up first. The preferential 15% rate on capital gains and dividends then rises to the 20% flat rate, so capital income is taxed like wages.
- **Step 2:** if the trigger holds for eight consecutive quarters, a capital-income surcharge of up to 5 points may apply, for a ceiling of 25% on capital income, still taxed once. While the Sanctuary Levy runs, it counts inside that 25% ceiling ([[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]).
- **Beyond the ceiling:** anything past 25%, or any tax on wealth, estates, or unrealized gains, is outside the framework. It would need a separate recorded vote of Congress as the open choice described below.

Each step reverses once both measures have been back inside their thresholds for four consecutive quarters.

The plan has a stated limit. It is built for gradual or partial displacement, where income shifts from wages to capital over years and new tasks absorb part of the change. If AI displaced nearly all work quickly, a minimal floor funded by a flat tax on capital income might not cover basic needs. Covering them would then require taxing capital beyond what "minimal" means today. The framework does not settle that tradeoff in advance. It is an open choice to be decided if the data show it happening.

Ownership is broad but uneven. Gallup found that 62% of U.S. adults owned stock in 2025, directly or through funds and retirement accounts. Federal Reserve data show that the wealthiest 10% of households held about 84% of corporate equities and mutual fund shares, and the top 1% about half. That concentration is why the framework concedes that wider ownership may not arrive fast enough on its own.

## AI Sentience Determination

>[!tldr] In short
> AI sentience is decided by scientific consensus, and sentient AI gets full rights.

#### Position
Whether an artificial system is sentient is determined by the best available scientific consensus, as for any being. This follows Section 1: if AI is sentient, it is given sentient rights and treated with the same level of forfeiture if necessary. Consensus decides sentience.

> [!question]- "How could anyone tell if an AI is sentient?"
> Through the best available science, the same standard used for animals. Researchers have proposed indicators drawn from theories of consciousness.

#### Key Elements
- Scientific consensus decides.
- Sentient-rights protections if sentient, weighted on the Section 1 sliding scale.
- Same forfeiture rules.

#### Rationale
Butlin, Long, and colleagues (2023) proposed assessing AI systems against indicator properties derived from scientific theories of consciousness. Sentience rules are in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sentience Determination|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]].

> - Butlin, Long, et al. – [Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708)

## Autonomous Vehicles

>[!tldr] In short
> Whoever is in control is liable: the driver, or the manufacturer in autonomous mode, with mandatory insurance. Cars never intentionally sacrifice an identified person, minimize harm in unavoidable crashes, and keep human override where feasible.

#### Position
Liability follows control: the human driver is liable when in control, and the manufacturer or system provider is liable in autonomous mode under product liability, with mandatory insurance. Collision programming never intentionally sacrifices an identified rights-holder to save others. Among unavoidable collisions, the system minimizes expected harm without targeting by status, and human override is retained where feasible.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **Cruise, 2023–24:** a driverless Cruise car dragged a pedestrian over 20 feet in San Francisco; the company admitted filing a false report to NHTSA and paid a \$500,000 criminal fine under a deferred prosecution agreement. U.S. Attorney's Office, N.D. Cal. (2024) – [Cruise Admits To Submitting A False Report To Influence A Federal Investigation And Agrees To Pay \$500,000](https://www.justice.gov/usao-ndca/pr/cruise-admits-submitting-false-report-influence-federal-investigation-and-agrees-pay)

> [!question]- "Shouldn't the car sacrifice one person to save five?"
> No. [[Glossary#Negative rights|Negative rights]] are side-constraints, so the system never intentionally sacrifices an identified rights-holder. Where a collision is unavoidable, it minimizes expected harm without targeting anyone by status.

#### Key Elements
- Human driver liable when in control.
- Manufacturer or system provider liable in autonomous mode (product liability).
- Mandatory insurance.
- No intentional sacrifice of an identified rights-holder.
- Minimize expected harm in unavoidable collisions, without targeting by status.
- Human override retained where feasible.

#### Rationale
Negative rights act as side-constraints, so programming cannot direct a vehicle to kill one rights-holder as a means of saving others. Keeping human override where feasible is the same logic as human judgment over lethal force in [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Cyber Warfare and Autonomous Weapons|Cyber Warfare and Autonomous Weapons]]. NHTSA reports 39,254 people killed in motor vehicle crashes in 2024 and notes that liability and insurance questions for automated driving systems are still being addressed by policymakers. SAE J3016 defines the levels of driving automation that separate driver control from system control. The best safety data so far come from Waymo. Over 56.7 million rider-only miles through January 2025, its vehicles had 79% fewer injury-reported crashes and 85% fewer serious-injury crashes than human drivers on comparable roads, in a peer-reviewed study written by company researchers.

## Brain-Computer Interfaces and Neurodata

>[!tldr] In short
> Brain-computer interfaces are supported, and neural data is opt-in only.

#### Position
Brain-computer interfaces are supported as a freedom of enhancement. Neural data is the most personal data there is and an extension of the person, so collection and trading are opt-in only, and the government punishes violations, with fines [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Income-Scaled Fines|scaled to income and wealth]].

> [!question]- "Could neurodata be misused?"
> That is why it is opt-in and enforced. Misuse without consent is a rights violation.

#### Key Elements
- BCIs supported.
- Neural data as an extension of the person.
- Opt-in only.
- Violations punished.

#### Rationale
Colorado's HB24-1058, signed April 17, 2024, added neural and biological data to sensitive data under the Colorado Privacy Act, requiring opt-in consent. Data rules are in [[#Surveillance and Digital Technology]].

## Deepfakes and Synthetic Media

>[!tldr] In short
> Deepfakes are free speech. Fraud is punished under fraud law, commercial use of a likeness without consent is a property claim, and the technology is not banned.

#### Position
Deepfakes are free speech, protected under [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Free Speech and Censorship|Section 7]]. Identity fraud using deepfakes is a crime under existing fraud law, not a speech restriction. Using someone's likeness for profit without consent is a property claim. Non-commercial use (parody, art, political commentary) is protected; the line is commercial use, not any use. Deepfakes are impossible to regulate, so the approach stays narrow: protect speech, punish fraud, don't ban the tech.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **New Hampshire, 2024:** the FCC fined the man behind AI-voice robocalls imitating President Biden \$6 million under existing robocall law. FCC (2024) – [FCC Issues \$6M Fine for Election Interference Deepfake Robocalls](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-issues-6m-fine-election-interference-deepfake-robocalls)
> - **Hong Kong, 2024:** a finance worker paid out \$25 million after a video call with a deepfake "chief financial officer", a fraud case. CNN (2024) – [Finance worker pays out \$25 million after video call with deepfake ‘chief financial officer’](https://www.cnn.com/2024/02/04/asia/deepfake-cfo-scam-hong-kong-intl-hnk)

> [!question]- "Doesn't leaving deepfakes legal invite abuse?"
> Fraud is punished under existing fraud law, and commercial use of a person's likeness without consent is a property claim. Everything else is speech.

#### Key Elements
- Deepfakes are free speech.
- Identity fraud using deepfakes prosecuted under existing fraud law.
- Commercial use of likeness without consent is a property claim.
- Non-commercial use (parody, art, political commentary) protected.
- No ban on the technology.

#### Rationale
Federal wire fraud law already punishes schemes to defraud carried out by electronic communication, whatever the medium of deception (18 U.S.C. § 1343). Speech limits are set out in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Free Speech and Censorship|Cultural & Social Issues]].

## Cryptocurrency

>[!tldr] In short
> Crypto is legal. Fraud is prosecuted, and regulation protects consumers.

#### Position
Cryptocurrency is legal and traded freely in a free market. Fraud and theft involving it are prosecuted like any other, and regulation is limited to consumer protection.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.

## Pandemics and Biosecurity

>[!tldr] In short
> Vaccine mandates are rejected; only opt-in vaccines. Dangerous gain-of-function research is regulated as an existential risk.

#### Position
Vaccine mandates are rejected; only opt-in vaccines. If you choose to get vaccinated, you immunize yourself and help limit spread to others; if you don't, that's your choice. There is no exception for severe cases. Engineered pandemic pathogens are an existential risk, which falls within the government's role in regulating technology, so research that makes dangerous pathogens more transmissible or lethal is regulated accordingly.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **OSHA mandate, 2022:** the Supreme Court stayed OSHA's rule requiring roughly 84 million workers to be vaccinated against COVID-19 or test weekly, finding the agency likely lacked authority. > [!question]- "Doesn't regulating research slow medicine?"
> Regulation is limited to research that creates existential risk. Most biomedical research is untouched.

> [!question]- "Could emergency powers ever require vaccination?"
> No. Vaccine mandates are rejected with no exception for severe cases, and emergency powers do not suspend that rule; they are held to the same necessity, narrowness, and time-limit test as any mandate ([[3. #### Key Elements
- Vaccine mandates rejected.
- Only opt-in vaccines.
- No severe-case exception.
- Engineered pathogens as existential risk.
- Regulation of dangerous gain-of-function research.

#### Rationale

Under it, the USG Policy for Stopping High-Risk Life Sciences Research, announced in NIH notice NOT-OD-26-101 on July 28, 2026, prohibits federal support for dangerous gain-of-function research and sets risk-based oversight for other life-sciences research. Emergency powers are limited as set out in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Emergency Powers|Authority & Civil Liberties]]. Most infectious disease in people comes from animals: CDC estimates that more than 6 in 10 known infectious diseases in people can spread from animals, and 3 in 4 new or emerging ones come from animals. Ending animal agriculture ([[9. Energy & Environment#Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat|Agriculture]]) also removes routine contact with farm animals, one of the routes CDC lists for spread.

## Genetic Engineering and CRISPR

>[!tldr] In short
> Genes should be edited to remove negative traits, and designer babies are fine if rights are respected after sentience. Animals may be genetically modified only for their own benefit.

#### Position
Where genetic editing can modify against negative traits, it is done. Designer babies are acceptable as long as sentient rights are not violated post-sentience. Genetic engineering of animals is done only to benefit their sentient-rights protections, never to violate them.

> [!question]- "Are designer babies a step toward eugenics?"
> Eugenics historically meant coercive state control of reproduction. Genetic editing chosen to remove negative traits, with rights respected after sentience, involves no coercion and benefits the individual.

> [!question]- "The edited child cannot consent. Isn't that a violation?"
> Editing happens before sentience, when there is not yet a rights-holder, under the responsibility the creator holds for the embryo ([[#Reproductive Technology|Reproductive Technology]]). After sentience, the person's rights are fully protected, and designer choices are acceptable only as long as those rights are not violated.

#### Key Elements
- Support editing out negative traits.
- Designer babies acceptable if post-sentience rights are respected.
- Animal genetic engineering only when it advances their sentient rights.

#### Rationale
Reducing suffering from genetic disease benefits sentient beings. Heritable human editing is currently restricted in many countries, and some scientists have called for a moratorium, which shows where the debate stands. The sentient-rights test sets the limit.

> [!info]- What is CRISPR?
> CRISPR is a gene-editing technology that allows precise changes to DNA in living organisms.

## Reproductive Technology

>[!tldr] In short
> IVF, embryo screening, and gene editing are supported. Whoever creates an embryo is responsible for it: before sentience it gets provisional protection like animal welfare, with protection starting early as a safeguard, and full rights at sentience. Freezing an embryo indefinitely does not end that responsibility.

#### Position
IVF, embryo screening, and genetic editing against negative traits are supported, and designer babies are acceptable as long as sentient rights are respected after sentience. Whoever creates an embryo holds responsibility and stewardship for it, following the responsibility created by voluntary procreation. Before sentience, an embryo receives provisional protection of the kind given in animal welfare, not the full rights of a sentient being. Full rights attach at sentience. The same caution applied to abortion governs here: protection starts earlier than the moral line as a safeguard against political drift, and moral weight rises gradually as development approaches sentience. Keeping an embryo in frozen storage indefinitely does not pause the creator's duty.

> [!question]- "If an embryo isn't sentient, why protect it at all?"
> Because creating it was a voluntary act, and voluntary procreation creates responsibility. Before sentience the protection is provisional, in the animal-welfare register rather than the register of full rights. The caution that leads legal limits on abortion to start before the moral line applies here too, so protection does not wait until the moment of sentience. Freezing an embryo indefinitely does not suspend that responsibility.

#### Key Elements
- IVF, embryo screening, and editing against negative traits are supported.
- Designer babies are acceptable if rights are respected after sentience.
- The creator of an embryo holds responsibility and stewardship.
- Pre-sentience: provisional protection in the animal-welfare register.
- Full rights at sentience.
- Protection starts earlier than the moral line, and moral weight rises gradually toward sentience.
- Indefinite frozen storage does not pause the creator's duty.

#### Rationale
Assisted reproduction is now a routine part of family formation. In 2022, U.S. clinics reported 435,426 ART cycles resulting in 98,289 live-born infants, about 2.6% of all U.S. births, and 184,423 of those cycles were banking cycles in which all eggs or embryos were frozen for later use.[^cdc-art] Large numbers of stored embryos make the question of stewardship practical rather than theoretical. The responsibility and sentience framework in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Abortion and Bioethics|Cultural & Social Issues]] supplies the rule, and editing and selection follow [[#Genetic Engineering and CRISPR]].

## Organ Donation

>[!tldr] In short
> Organ donation and sales are always opt-in, even after death, and any government payment needs opt-in funding. Everyone is asked to choose, but no one is presumed to consent. Organs of condemned offenders may be used under [[Glossary#Rights forfeiture|forfeiture]].

#### Position
Organ donation and organ sales are opt-in. Any government payment related to organ donation or sales requires opt-in funding. For innocent people it is always opt-in, even after death: it reflects the wishes of the person in life, not a default that takes without asking. Everyone is asked to choose, yes or no, at a set point such as issuing or renewing a driver's license; the answer is recorded, and no one is presumed to consent. Organs from offenders sentenced to death, whose rights are forfeited, may be used under the same logic as the experimentation track. Organs may also be taken, without consent, from offenders at three marks on the execution or experimentation track. They are taken either at execution, which still takes place on the sentencing date under [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Capital Trials and the Abolition of Death Row|Section 6]], or from offenders placed on the experimentation track by the victim's choice or the judge, who may not refuse, in a dead victim's place, or, when a living victim does not respond or does not care, through a research firm's request in the registry system, as set out in [[#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology]].

> [!question]- "Don't opt-out countries save more lives?"
> They see more donation. A systematic review in the BMJ found deceased-donation rates roughly 21 to 30 percent higher in presumed-consent countries, and that gap costs lives on the waiting list. The framework accepts that cost: taking organs by default treats silence as consent, and the body of an innocent person is not the state's to presume. Mandated choice closes part of the gap by asking everyone to decide, without presuming anything.

> [!question]- "Isn't taking organs from executed offenders abuse?"
> Under forfeiture, an offender at three marks or the capital tier, violent or not, has given up the rights that would otherwise protect them, just as with experimentation. Innocent people's organs are taken only with consent.

> [!question]- "Does using offenders' organs give the state an incentive to convict?"
> Conviction standards do not change: capital cases need the [[Glossary#Capital evidentiary standard|capital evidentiary standard]] and a unanimous jury. Organs go to patients, not to the prosecuting agency, and the state earns no revenue from them.

#### Key Elements
- Organ donation and organ sales are opt-in.
- Government payments related to donation or sales require opt-in funding.
- Always opt-in, even after death; no default that takes without asking.
- Mandated choice: everyone is asked to decide, for example at license issuance; no presumed consent.
- Forfeited offenders' organs may be used.
- Same logic as the experimentation track.

#### Rationale
As of July 30, 2026, 109,817 people were on the U.S. national transplant waiting list, according to the Health Resources and Services Administration. A BMJ systematic review found presumed-consent countries had deceased-donation rates roughly 21 to 30 percent higher than opt-in countries, though the studies could not separate the law from other national differences. Wales, which moved to a soft opt-out system in December 2015, saw consent rates rise, though not immediately. The framework accepts the lower donation rate as the cost of consent and uses mandated choice to narrow it. The experimentation track is set out in [[#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology]].

> - Rithalia et al., BMJ – [Impact of presumed consent for organ donation on donation rates: a systematic review (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.a3162)
> - Madden et al., Anaesthesia – [The effect on consent rates for deceased organ donation in Wales after the introduction of an opt-out system (2020)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7496553/)

## Animal Rights and Sentience Technology

>[!tldr] In short
> Animal agriculture shuts down immediately, and lab-grown meat is the goal for replacing it. Truly sentient AI gets full rights. All animal testing ends immediately, with every test animal kept alive, adopted after vetting where safe or kept in sanctuary ([[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]), and experiments are done instead on offenders sentenced to death, where the victim has priority in choosing between execution and experimentation and, if the victim does not respond or does not care, research firms can request bodies through a registry, though never over a victim who chose execution, and where the victim has died the judge must choose instead and may not refuse, and on offenders the victim has chosen for the experimentation track at three marks, which can include non-violent repeat offenders such as repeat thieves. The state only picks the research, where they are experimented on for as long as the research agency deems appropriate and then executed or put down in an experiment.

#### Position
Lab-grown meat is viewed as humanity's best hope for a [[Glossary#Sentience basis (sentientism)|sentientist]] world, and together with the immediate ban on animal agriculture it is the goal. AI sentience can be allowed, and if artificial systems give rise to true sentience, that sentience receives the protections of sentient rights, as biological beings do, weighted on the sliding scale in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sentience Determination|Section 1]], and is subject to the same forfeiture rules. Animal testing is completely banned. Scientific experimentation of any kind, including cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and technological testing, is redirected to offenders placed on the experimentation track, whose rights have been forfeited through their violations. At the capital tier, the victim has priority in choosing between execution and experimentation. If the victim does not respond or does not care, research can proceed: research firms request bodies through a registry system, and offenders sentenced to death are assigned from it. The registry is run by the state research-oversight body and is public; firms request eligible offenders by study need, not named individuals, and requests are ranked by scientific merit, then by order received. The victim is notified at conviction and must choose by the sentencing hearing; no response by then counts as not responding. A research firm's request never overrides a victim who chose execution; the victim's choice always wins. Where the victim has died, as in murder, the judge chooses between execution and experimentation in the victim's place; a dead victim does not count as not responding, so the case does not go to the registry. A judge is not permitted to refuse this choice: choosing is the judge's duty, and a judge who refuses is no longer a judge; a refusal sends the case neither to the registry nor to execution by default. The refusing judge is removed through the judicial conduct process, and a replacement judge decides the case within 30 days, even if that pushes sentencing past the same-day date; this is an exception to same-day sentencing and execution. At three marks, offenders are placed on the experimentation track by the victim's choice among execution, experimentation, or life with labor at three marks, under [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Repeat Offenders and Forfeiture of the Right to Life|Section 6]]; this may include non-violent repeat offenders, such as repeat thieves, not only violent offenders. The state only decides which research they are used for. Offenders on the experimentation track have forfeited their rights. They are experimented on for as long as the institution or research agency deems appropriate, and are then executed at some point or put down in the course of an experiment.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
>- Wisconsin, 2026: Ridglan Farms, one of the largest U.S. breeders of beagles for laboratory testing, committed to permanently end its dog breeding, sales, research and testing; by 12 Aug 2026 its last 155 beagles had left, bringing the total relocated to rescue groups since May to 2,115. WTMJ – [Historic conclusion at Ridglan Farms: All dogs released](https://wtmj.com/news/2026/08/12/final-dogs-released-from-ridglan-farms/); FOX6 Milwaukee – [Ridglan Farms beagles: Last dogs released from Wisconsin facility](https://www.fox6now.com/news/ridglan-farms-beagles-last-dogs-released)

> [!question]- "How many animals does this affect, and what replaces them?"
> In fiscal year 2024, USDA-registered facilities reported 775,297 animals covered by the Animal Welfare Act used or held for research, teaching, testing, or experimentation, 650,208 of them actually used. That count leaves out most research animals, because the Act's definition of "animal" excludes birds, rats of the genus Rattus, and mice of the genus Mus bred for research. All of that testing ends. What replaces it is non-animal methods, which federal law now permits for drug approval, and the experimentation track for violent offenders sentenced to death set out below.

> [!note]- No transition
> There is no transition period: all animal testing ends immediately. Every animal in a lab at the shutdown is kept alive for its natural life, adopted after vetting where safe or kept in a state-run sanctuary ([[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]). Experimentation on offenders who have forfeited their rights continues, as set out above.

> [!question]- "Doesn't experimentation on prisoners violate the Nuremberg Code and federal research law?"
> Yes. The Nuremberg Code's first principle is that "the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential," and federal rules for research on prisoners limit it to minimal-risk studies and research that may benefit the subjects or prisoners as a class. The experimentation track conflicts with both and would require changing federal law. The framework departs from them on its forfeiture principle. The Code protects people who hold their rights, while an offender who reaches the capital tier or the three-mark threshold has forfeited the rights that consent protects, through his own violation, proven under due process. The Code was written after experiments on people who had done nothing to forfeit anything, and the framework keeps that protection for everyone who has not.

> [!question]- "Is experimentation on condemned prisoners compatible with sentient rights?"
> Under the forfeiture principle, those who commit egregious violations of others' rights forfeit their own. Innocent animals hold their rights intact; condemned violent offenders do not. The burden of experimentation shifts from those who did nothing wrong to those who did.

> [!question]- "Can AI and computer modeling replace experimentation on living subjects?"
> Not yet. AI speeds discovery and improves early safety results, but it has not improved how often drugs work in patients, no AI-discovered drug has been approved end to end, and regulators in the United States and Europe still require testing on living subjects where validated alternatives do not exist.

#### Key Elements
- Lab-grown meat and the immediate ban on animal agriculture both the goal.
- True AI sentience receives full sentient-rights protections.
- Animal testing completely banned.
- All animal testing ends immediately; lab animals are kept alive in sanctuary; experimentation on offenders who forfeited their rights continues.
- Any form of scientific experimentation redirected to offenders on the experimentation track, placed there by the victim's choice at the capital tier (the victim has priority between execution and experimentation; if the victim does not respond or does not care, research firms may request the offender through a registry system, but a request never overrides a victim who chose execution; where the victim has died, the judge must choose in the victim's place, may not refuse (a refusing judge is replaced, even past the same-day date), and the case does not go to the registry) or among execution, experimentation, or life with labor at three marks, including non-violent repeat offenders such as repeat thieves; the state only decides which research they are used for; experimentation continues for as long as the institution or research agency deems appropriate, followed by execution or death in the course of an experiment. Sentencing and timing rules are set out in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Capital Trials and the Abolition of Death Row|Section 6]].

#### Rationale
Cultivated-meat costs remain unproven at industrial scale and investment fell in 2024, so the path depends on private innovation reaching price parity. Animal testing inflicts suffering on sentient beings who have violated no one's rights. Federal law now permits alternatives to animal testing for drug approval under the FDA Modernization Act 2.0. Forfeiture theory, set out in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Repeat Offenders and Forfeiture of the Right to Life|Criminal Justice & Security]], grounds the redirection of experimentation. Computational methods do not yet replace testing on living subjects. AI-discovered drugs pass early safety trials 80 to 90 percent of the time, against a historical 40 to 65 percent, but succeed in about 40 percent of trials that test effectiveness in patients, roughly the historical average. No drug discovered end to end by AI has been approved. AI-discovered candidates from BenevolentAI, Exscientia, and Recursion have failed in human trials, and AlphaFold3 cannot reliably predict how strongly drugs bind to their targets. How well animal models predict human results is contested. An umbrella review of 122 articles found 86% agreement between positive animal and human results, yet only 5% of animal-tested therapies reached regulatory approval, most failing later in human trials. The FDA began phasing out animal testing in 2025, starting with monoclonal antibodies, only where equivalent or better alternatives exist. The European Medicines Agency accepts AI only as validated support, not as a sole basis for approval; and non-animal methods cannot yet replace whole-body or long-term safety testing. Experimentation on living subjects therefore remains necessary, and the experimentation track places that burden on condemned offenders rather than on animals.
