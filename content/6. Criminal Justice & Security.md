## Core Principles
Retribution, incapacitation, and the protection of victims' sentient rights take priority over rehabilitation. Those who murder or rape, or who repeatedly violate the rights of others, forfeit the protections they denied their victims. Punishment is swift, certain, and public, and capital punishment applies only where guilt is established beyond any real doubt. Enforcement is heavy, with military-style action against cartels and high-crime threats.

## Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty

#### Position
Voluntary murder is punished by death. Rape established under the same evidentiary standard is punished by death as well. The sentence is mandatory once guilt is established; it is not left to the discretion of a jury weighing sympathy for the offender. The offender's mental competence, intellectual capacity, or understanding of the proceedings does not reduce the sentence, because none of these change what was done to the victim. Killings that are involuntary rather than intended are handled outside the capital track.

> [!question]- "Hasn't the Supreme Court already ruled that mandatory death sentences, executing the mentally incompetent, and executing for rape are unconstitutional?"
> Yes. Under current doctrine all three are barred. *Woodson v. North Carolina* (1976) struck down mandatory death sentences for murder. *Ford v. Wainwright* (1986) and *Atkins v. Virginia* (2002) bar executing the insane and the intellectually disabled. *Coker v. Georgia* (1977) and *Kennedy v. Louisiana* (2008) bar the death penalty for rape where the victim was not killed. The position here does not pretend otherwise. It is a normative claim about what the law ought to be. Achieving it would require the Court to revisit those holdings or a constitutional amendment. The Eighth Amendment itself does not prohibit capital punishment; *Gregg v. Georgia* (1976) upheld it. The dispute is over which offenses and which offenders qualify, and that line has been drawn by the Court rather than by the text.

#### Key Elements
- Death is the mandatory sentence for voluntary murder.
- Rape proven to the capital evidentiary standard is a capital offense.
- Incompetence, intellectual disability, or diminished understanding is not a bar to execution.
- Involuntary killings are not capital offenses.
- Offenders' assets pay restitution to victims' families; taxpayers do not, beyond voluntary donations.

#### Rationale
Under the rights-forfeiture principle, an agent who deliberately violates the most basic negative rights of another cannot claim the full protection of those same rights. Murder destroys the victim's subjective experience entirely and permanently. Rape is among the gravest violations of bodily integrity short of death. The response is matched to the severity of the act: proportionality in this framework means the punishment reflects the seriousness of the violation. For the worst violations, that is the most serious punishment available.

The refusal to exempt incompetent offenders follows from where moral weight is placed. The framework is concerned with outward rights violations, not with the inner condition of the violator. The victim is equally dead whether the killer understood the proceedings or not. Discretionary sentencing introduces inconsistency: two offenders who commit the same act receive different outcomes depending on the jury, the county, and the quality of mitigation evidence. A mandatory rule treats like cases alike. The historical risk with mandatory statutes, noted in Woodson v. North Carolina, was that juries refused to convict to avoid the sentence; the clear-cut evidentiary standard narrows capital cases to those where guilt is not in serious dispute, which reduces that pressure.

Restitution follows the same logic of reciprocity. The offender, not uninvolved taxpayers, bears the cost of the harm. Where an offender is executed or serving a long sentence, restitution is drawn from their accounts and assets.

## Evidentiary Standard

#### Position
Execution happens only when guilt is absolutely clear, without a shadow of a doubt. Ordinary proof beyond a reasonable doubt is not enough for a capital outcome. The standard requires corroborating objective evidence: body-camera or surveillance footage, multiple eyewitnesses backed by camera footage, physical evidence such as a body, and similar proof that does not depend on a single witness or on inference alone. Cases that fall short of this bar do not go to the capital track.

> [!question]- "Innocent people have been sentenced to death. Doesn't any death penalty guarantee that the state will eventually kill an innocent person?"
> The risk is real and the evidence for it is serious. Gross and colleagues (2014) estimated that at least 4.1 percent of defendants sentenced to death in the United States are likely innocent. That figure is the strongest argument against capital punishment as it currently operates, and it is the reason for this standard. Most known wrongful capital convictions rested on mistaken eyewitness identification, false confessions, unreliable informants, or flawed forensic testimony. Those are exactly the kinds of evidence a corroboration requirement excludes as a sufficient basis. Wrongful execution is treated as a rights violation by the state, with full accountability for those responsible. No human process reaches zero error. The claim here is narrower: restricting execution to cases with objective corroboration reduces the error rate far below the current system's.

#### Key Elements
- The capital standard is higher than proof beyond a reasonable doubt.
- Objective, corroborated evidence is required, not testimony or inference alone.
- Cases that fail the standard leave the capital track.
- Wrongful execution is treated as a rights violation by the state.

#### Rationale
A swift system with no long appeals is defensible only if guilt is certain at the front end. The longer the period between sentence and execution, the more the system relies on appeals to catch mistakes. This framework removes that period, so the burden moves to the trial itself. The evidentiary bar does the work that decades of appeals were meant to do.

Corroboration matters because the main sources of wrongful conviction are subjective. A recording, a body, and independent witnesses who agree with the recording are much harder to fabricate or misread than a single identification made under stress. The bar is deliberately strict. Its purpose is to make the capital track narrow, reliable, and limited to cases where the facts are not in serious dispute.

> [!info]- What counts as clear-cut evidence?
> Evidence that establishes both the act and the identity of the offender without depending on a single uncorroborated source. Examples include body-camera or surveillance footage of the act, multiple eyewitnesses whose accounts are confirmed by camera footage, and physical evidence such as a body. A confession alone, a single eyewitness, or circumstantial inference alone does not meet the standard.

##### Sources
- Gross, O'Brien, Hu & Kennedy – [Rate of False Conviction of Criminal Defendants Who Are Sentenced to Death](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1306417111) (PNAS, 2014)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Nobody is executed unless the proof is overwhelming and backed by objective evidence such as video and physical evidence, not just testimony. Because there is no long appeals process, the trial itself has to get it right.

## Capital Trials and the Abolition of Death Row

#### Position
Capital trials are capped at a maximum of 30 days. Death row is abolished as a concept. There is no waiting period between sentencing and execution: the sentence is carried out on the sentencing date, for effect and to increase deterrence, except where the state assigns the offender to the experimentation track under [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Section 10]]. Nobody is held for years or decades under a death sentence, except on the experimentation track, and nobody is held idle for life as a substitute for one.

> [!question]- "Doesn't removing the appeals process just mean executing people before their mistakes can be found?"
> Appeals exist mainly because the trial is not trusted to reach the right answer. In the current system, the gap between sentence and execution often runs to decades. That gap brings cost and uncertainty without a reliable gain in accuracy, since most of the time is procedural. This framework addresses accuracy at the trial stage instead: only cases meeting the clear-cut evidentiary standard reach a capital verdict. If the evidence needs years of review to settle, the case does not belong on the capital track. The tradeoff is explicit: a much narrower set of capital cases in exchange for finality within those cases.

#### Key Elements
- Capital trials last no longer than 30 days.
- Death row does not exist.
- Execution takes place on the sentencing date, except under the experimentation track in [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Section 10]], which also sets out the evidence that experimentation on living subjects remains necessary.
- Only cases meeting the clear-cut evidentiary standard are eligible.

#### Rationale
Delay defeats both the retributive and the protective purpose of the sentence. A punishment carried out twenty years after the crime is disconnected from the act, burdens victims' families with decades of hearings, and costs the public far more than the sentence itself. The current system keeps the severity of the death penalty while removing its certainty and speed. That combination fails on every count: it is not swift, it is not certain, and it is enormously expensive.

A fixed trial window with immediate execution keeps the formal finding of guilt by a court, which the rule of law requires, while removing indefinite delay. Combined with the evidentiary standard, it limits the death penalty to the cases where speed is justified because the facts are not in serious dispute. Capital trials under current rules have run well past 30 days (a California review cited by the state's 2008 commission put the average at 79 days), so the cap depends on the narrower capital track: only cases meeting the clear-cut standard qualify.

The cost of delay is concrete. The Federal Bureau of Prisons puts the average annual cost of holding one federal inmate at \$47,162 for fiscal year 2024. Across 45 states, Vera Institute data put the average at \$33,274 per inmate in 2015. Every year a condemned prisoner spends waiting adds that cost again, on top of the cost of the appeals themselves.

> Capital trials take 30 days at most, and the sentence is carried out the day it is handed down. There is no death row. The strict evidence rule is what makes this speed acceptable.

## Repeat Offenders and Forfeiture of the Right to Life

#### Position
After a fixed repeat-offender threshold is reached, the offender forfeits the right to life. The original formulation set that threshold at a third theft conviction, with sentences rising for each conviction before it. The precise number is left open and is to be fixed in statute, but the principle is settled: someone who robs and steals again and again, and is caught each time, has shown that the community cannot be protected by ordinary sentences. Once the threshold is crossed, only two outcomes exist. The offender is executed, or, if judged competent and well behaved, is placed in compelled labor for life. A later disqualifying act ends the labor status and results in execution.

> [!question]- "Executing someone for theft is wildly disproportionate. How can stealing ever justify death?"
> The sentence does not answer the last theft by itself. It answers a pattern. A single theft is punished with a finite sentence. The capital consequence attaches only after the offender has been convicted repeatedly, served sentences each time, and returned to violating others' rights. At that point the question is no longer what one theft deserves. It is whether the community must keep absorbing an offender who has shown, through repeated choices, that they will not respect the rights of others. Forfeiture in this framework scales with the pattern as well as the act. Repeated predation erodes the trust a free society depends on, and the state's first duty is to protect the innocent from that erosion. The lifelong labor alternative also means execution is not the only outcome past the threshold.

#### Key Elements
- A fixed repeat-offender threshold triggers forfeiture of the right to life; the original threshold was a third theft conviction, and the number is left open.
- Sentences increase with each conviction before the threshold.
- Past the threshold there are two outcomes: execution, or lifelong compelled labor for offenders judged competent and well behaved.
- A disqualifying act while in compelled labor results in execution.

#### Rationale
The rights-forfeiture principle holds that those who refuse to respect the basic negative rights of others cannot claim the full protection of those rights. Repetition matters to that judgment. A first offense may reflect a single bad choice. A pattern of offenses, each followed by punishment and release, shows a settled disposition to treat other people's property and safety as available for the taking. Each release under that pattern exposes new victims to a known risk.

The state exists first to protect negative rights. Continuing to release a known repeat predator into the community, or to house them indefinitely at public expense, is treated as a failure of that duty. Once the number is fixed in statute, the threshold makes the consequence predictable in advance: every offender knows where the line is, and every conviction before it is an explicit warning. Property offenders reoffend at the highest rate of any group (78.3% rearrested within five years in BJS data), and California's three-strikes law cut arrests among offenders facing a third strike by 17–20%; the statutory number should be set with that evidence.

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Repeat offenders who keep committing crimes after being punished lose their right to life once they pass a fixed threshold, originally the third theft conviction. They are either executed or, if they are competent and well behaved, put to work for life.

## Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor

#### Position
Idle permanent incarceration is abolished in its entirety. Life sentences served in a cell and indefinite warehousing of prisoners end; every long-term sentence, including lifelong compelled labor past the repeat threshold, is served as work. Every custodial sentence is served as compelled labor on government projects such as building, cleaning, and the production of goods. Sentences for first and second offenses are greatly increased in length, so that early offenses are not treated as cheap strikes before the threshold. Rights forfeited by an offender are forfeited for the duration of the sentence, and past the repeat threshold they are forfeited for life.

> [!question]- "Isn't lifelong compelled labor just slavery? Doesn't the Thirteenth Amendment forbid it?"
> The Thirteenth Amendment prohibits slavery and involuntary servitude "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted." Compelled labor imposed on a convicted offender falls within that stated exception, so the Constitution does not forbid it. The moral objection is a separate question and deserves a direct answer. Slavery as historically practiced seized people who had violated no one's rights, on the basis of race, and held them and their children as property. Compelled labor here applies only to individuals convicted of violating others' rights, as a consequence of their own choices. Under the forfeiture principle, an offender's claim against compelled labor is lost through the violation, just as a claim to liberty is lost through imprisonment. It is also the alternative to execution past the threshold, not an addition to it. The offender's labor offsets the cost their crimes have imposed on the public.

#### Key Elements
- Idle permanent incarceration, including life without parole in a cell, is abolished.
- All custodial sentences are served as compelled labor on government projects.
- Sentences for first and second offenses are greatly increased in length.
- Lifelong compelled labor is available past the repeat threshold only to offenders judged competent and well behaved; others are executed.

#### Rationale
Idle incarceration costs the public heavily while giving nothing back to victims or the community. State and local governments spent \$87 billion on corrections in 2021. The Federal Bureau of Prisons reports an average cost of \$47,162 per federal inmate per year (FY2024), and Vera Institute data put the 2015 state average at \$33,274. These averages include fixed costs such as facilities and administration, so removing one prisoner saves less than the average figure. Even so, a life sentence commits taxpayers to housing, feeding, and guarding an offender for decades, which is a burden on people who did nothing wrong.

Compelled labor turns the sentence into productive work that offsets part of that cost. It is not claimed to pay for incarceration in full. The federal model, Federal Prison Industries, receives no annual appropriation and runs as a self-funding revolving fund. In fiscal year 2025 it employed 19,560 inmates and reported \$14.98 million in net income, though it posted a \$37.4 million loss from operations, made up by interest and other income. Its output is real but small relative to the cost of the prison system. The stronger case for structured prison work lies in outcomes: the Washington State Institute for Public Policy estimates that correctional-industries programs return about \$4,975 in benefits per participant, largely through reduced reoffending.

Longer sentences for early offenses address a gap the threshold would otherwise create. If only the threshold carried serious consequences, the first and second offenses would look cheap by comparison. Raising their cost means every offense carries weight, and the threshold is not the only deterrent in the system. Longer sentences also serve incapacitation and desert, not only deterrence, and they operate alongside the certainty measures in this section: expanded policing, swift trials, and execution on the sentencing date.

> [!info]- What is compelled labor?
> Required work on government projects (construction, cleaning, production of goods, and similar tasks) performed by convicted offenders as their sentence. It replaces idle time in a cell. For sentences below the repeat threshold it lasts for a finite term. Past the threshold, for offenders judged competent and well behaved, it lasts for life.

## Rejection of Restorative Justice

#### Position
Restorative justice is rejected as a goal of the criminal process. Reconciliation between offender and victim, victim-offender mediation, and community reintegration are not what punishment is for. The criminal process exists to protect the innocent, give offenders what their violations deserve, and remove ongoing threats. Victims are owed restitution from the offender, not a process designed around the offender's restoration. Reduced reoffending that follows from compelled labor is a welcome side effect, not a purpose of punishment.

> [!question]- "Doesn't restorative justice reduce reoffending and give victims a voice that punishment ignores?"
> Victims are given a direct material remedy through restitution paid from the offender's own assets, and the protection of the community is placed at the center of the process. Restorative programs make the offender's reintegration a central purpose of the system. This framework places the victim's rights and the safety of everyone else first. Whatever value mediation has as a private, voluntary arrangement, the state's criminal process is not organized around it.

#### Key Elements
- The criminal process is not organized around reconciliation, mediation, or reintegration.
- Punishment serves protection, desert, and incapacitation.
- Restitution comes from the offender, not the public.

#### Rationale
Under the framework, a violation of negative rights calls for a response matched to the violation. Restorative approaches move the focus from what was done to the victim toward the future of the person who did it. The protective and retributive purposes of punishment are the basis of the system, so a model built around the offender's restoration does not fit it.

>[!tldr]- Summary
> The justice system is about protecting victims and the public and giving offenders what they deserve, not about reconciling offenders with victims or reintegrating them. Victims are repaid by the offender.

## Public Executions

#### Position
Executions are carried out in public or recorded and made openly available, including by livestream. The state's use of lethal force is visible to the citizens in whose name it is carried out, not hidden behind closed doors or presented as a quiet medical procedure. Public execution changes only the manner and visibility of the sentence. It does not lower the evidentiary standard, the trial process, or any other requirement.

> [!question]- "Isn't public execution barbaric spectacle? Shouldn't the state at least keep it out of sight?"
> Keeping executions out of sight does not make them less real. It makes them less accountable. When the state takes a life, the public that authorized it has a claim to see what is done in its name. Secrecy allows both sides to avoid the reality: supporters avoid confronting what they support, and officials avoid scrutiny of how it is carried out. Transparency serves accountability first. It also serves the deterrent purpose that only works if potential offenders see that the sentence is real and certain.

#### Key Elements
- Executions are public, or recorded and openly available, including by livestream.
- Visibility applies to the method of execution only; evidentiary and trial standards are unchanged.
- The purposes are accountability to the public and visible certainty of punishment.

#### Rationale
A government exercising its gravest power should do so in the open. Transparency is consistent with the broader demand in this framework for accountability of officials, who are held to higher standards than ordinary citizens. It also connects the punishment to its purpose: a sentence intended to show that grave violations bring certain consequences cannot show anything if it is hidden.

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Executions are public or openly recorded, so citizens can see what the state does in their name. This changes nothing about the evidence or trial rules.

## Deterrence and the Justification for Capital Punishment

#### Position
The empirical research on whether capital punishment deters murder is, at best, insufficient to support a conclusion either way. That is not evidence that the death penalty fails to deter. It is evidence that the studies cannot tell. The weakness of the findings largely reflects the system being studied: slow trials, decades of appeals, and uncertain outcomes. The reforms set out above (swift trials, no death row, execution on the sentencing date, and public visibility) are designed to make the punishment certain and swift, which are the conditions under which deterrence is most expected to operate. The justification for the death penalty does not depend on a deterrence claim. It rests on retribution, incapacitation, and the forfeiture of rights by those who commit the gravest violations.

> [!question]- "If the research can't show that the death penalty deters, isn't the case for it gone?"
> No. The National Research Council's 2012 review found that the existing studies are "not informative" about whether capital punishment increases, decreases, or has no effect on homicide rates, and recommended they not be used to inform policy in either direction. A finding that the research is uninformative is not a finding of no effect. The case for capital punishment here was never built on deterrence statistics. It rests on desert and protection: a murderer has forfeited the right to life, and an executed offender cannot harm anyone again. Deterrence is a possible additional benefit, not the foundation.

#### Key Elements
- The deterrence evidence is insufficient to support a conclusion in either direction.
- Insufficient evidence is not evidence against deterrence.
- The current system's delays and uncertainty undercut any deterrent effect it might have.
- The reforms aim for certainty and swiftness, which the deterrence literature identifies as the factors that matter most.
- The justification rests on retribution, incapacitation, and rights forfeiture.

#### Rationale
The National Research Council panel identified recurring flaws in the deterrence literature. Studies failed to account for the effect of non-capital alternatives such as life imprisonment. They relied on untested assumptions about how potential offenders perceive the risk of execution. Their results shifted with small changes in statistical specification. The panel concluded that this body of work cannot settle the question.

The broader deterrence research points in a consistent direction. Daniel Nagin's reviews conclude that the certainty of apprehension and punishment has far more consistent support as a deterrent than the severity of punishment. The American death penalty as currently practiced is severe but neither certain nor swift. Execution is rare, comes decades after the crime, and is surrounded by uncertainty about whether it will happen at all. Under those conditions weak deterrence findings are what the theory would predict. Historical evidence also suggests visibility matters: Phillips (1980) found that homicides in London dropped in the weeks following heavily publicized executions, though the effect was short-lived.

None of this proves that a swift, certain, public death penalty would deter. It shows that the existing studies did not test one. The case stands on other grounds. Retribution gives the offender what the violation deserves. Incapacitation ensures the offender never violates anyone's rights again. Reciprocity holds that those who reject the basic framework of non-aggression cannot claim its full protections. These justifications hold regardless of what future deterrence research shows.

> [!info]- What is the difference between certainty and severity of punishment?
> Certainty is the likelihood that an offender will actually be caught and punished. Severity is how harsh the punishment is if it happens. Deterrence research generally finds that certainty has a stronger and more reliable effect on behavior than severity. A harsh punishment that is unlikely or delayed for decades deters less than people expect.

## Police Power and Accountability

#### Position
Police are heavily funded and expanded, and officers are well paid and well respected in proportion to the danger and responsibility of the work. That status carries a higher standard: officers who break the law face greater criminal liability and punishment than ordinary citizens. Misconduct is investigated by nonpartisan, independent third parties that operate like compliance audit firms, not by the department under review. Body cameras are federally mandated for every officer who deals with the public. Current use-of-force standards are treated as largely adequate. Patrol requires one high bar of physical fitness and strength for all officers. Officers who do not meet that bar are not assigned to patrol; they are assigned to verbal confrontations in the field and administrative work, and female officers in this group also handle interactions with women.

> [!question]- "Does higher liability for officers conflict with heavy funding and support for police?"
> No. Pay, respect, and power are paired with stricter accountability, not shielded from it. Doctrines that let officials escape personal consequences for rights violations, such as qualified immunity as currently applied under the "clearly established law" test, sit in tension with the rule that those with greater power answer to a higher standard. Strong policing and strict liability are treated as parts of the same commitment: enforcement exists to protect sentient rights, and an officer who violates them is held to account more severely, not less.

#### Key Elements
- Heavy funding and expansion of police forces, with high pay and public respect.
- Higher criminal liability and punishment for officers who break the law than for ordinary citizens.
- Independent, nonpartisan third-party investigation of misconduct.
- Federal body-camera mandate for all public-facing officers.
- Current use-of-force standards retained.
- One high physical standard for all patrol officers; officers who do not meet it are assigned to verbal confrontations and administrative roles, with female officers also handling interactions with women.

#### Rationale
Policing is the main instrument for protecting sentient and property rights in daily life, and an understaffed or poorly paid force cannot provide that protection. The authority to use force is also the authority most open to abuse, so accountability must grow with power. Independent investigation removes the conflict of interest in departments judging their own officers. Body cameras create a record that protects both the public and officers acting lawfully; a randomized trial in Rialto, California found that shifts with cameras saw fewer use-of-force incidents and fewer complaints than shifts without them. The use-of-force framework set by the Supreme Court in Graham v. Connor, which judges force by the objective reasonableness of an officer on the scene, and the limit on deadly force against fleeing suspects in Tennessee v. Garner, are treated as an adequate baseline. Federal data has captured fewer than half of fatal police shootings, so judging whether the baseline stays adequate depends on the body-camera record and on complete reporting. Patrol work can require physically overpowering resisting suspects, so physical standards are set by the demands of the job.

> [!info]- What is qualified immunity?
> Qualified immunity is a judge-made doctrine that shields government officials from personal civil liability under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 unless their conduct violated "clearly established" statutory or constitutional rights that a reasonable person would have known. It grew from Pierson v. Ray (1967) and took its modern form in Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982). It concerns civil suits, not criminal prosecution.

## Drug Policy

#### Position
Marijuana is legal, and use of other drugs is decriminalized, but drug use in public areas is prohibited. Full prohibition would be ideal in a perfect society, but it does not work in practice and is not pursued. Cartels are designated as terroristic national-security threats and destroyed with military force. Enforcement resources move away from private users and toward the organizations that traffic drugs through violence.

> [!question]- "Does decriminalization contradict a hardline stance on crime?"
> No. Private drug use by an adult does not by itself violate the sentient rights of others, and prohibition has failed to stop it while feeding the black market that funds cartels. The hardline stance is aimed at rights violators: cartels that murder, traffic, and corrupt governments are treated as terrorist enemies, and public use that imposes on others remains banned. Decriminalization narrows enforcement to where harm to others actually occurs.

#### Key Elements
- Marijuana legal; decriminalization of other drug use and possession for personal use.
- Ban on drug use in public areas.
- Full prohibition acknowledged as ideal but unrealistic.
- Cartels designated as terrorist national-security threats.
- Military force used to destroy cartel organizations.

#### Rationale
The framework limits state coercion to protecting the rights of others, and private consumption falls outside that line while public use spills onto others. Portugal decriminalized the personal possession of all drugs in 2001, replacing criminal penalties with administrative responses, and the European drug agency's country reports track that model. On cartels, the United States designated several cartels and transnational gangs, including the Sinaloa Cartel and Cartel de Jalisco Nueva Generación, as Foreign Terrorist Organizations in February 2025, following a January 2025 executive order. That designation treats cartels as security threats rather than ordinary criminal enterprises, which matches the framework's position.

> [!info]- What is the difference between decriminalization and legalization?
> Decriminalization removes criminal penalties for using or possessing drugs, though use can still be restricted or handled through civil or administrative measures. Legalization makes production and sale lawful and regulated. The position here is that marijuana is legal and other drugs are decriminalized for use, without a legal commercial market for them.

## Border Security and Immigration Enforcement

#### Position
Illegal immigrants are removed from the country by force. The border is heavily secured and fortified, and border crossings are defended with lethal force when necessary. Sanctuary cities are abolished at the federal level. ICE is expanded in size, with one reform: all officers wear body cameras.

> [!question]- "Can the federal government abolish sanctuary cities without violating states' rights?"
> Immigration and border control are federal powers, as the Supreme Court affirmed in Arizona v. United States. Federal law already bars state and local governments from restricting their officials from sharing immigration-status information with federal authorities under 8 U.S.C. § 1373. Abolishing sanctuary jurisdictions is treated as enforcing a federal responsibility that local governments have no authority to obstruct.

#### Key Elements
- Forceful removal of illegal immigrants.
- Heavily fortified and secured borders.
- Lethal force authorized to defend border crossings when necessary.
- Federal abolition of sanctuary cities.
- Expansion of ICE, with body cameras required for all officers.

#### Rationale
A nation that cannot control who enters cannot enforce its own laws or decide who joins the political community, and the high-trust society the framework aims for depends on enforced law. Unlawful entry is a violation of the nation's law, and removal is the remedy. Sanctuary policies place local politics above federal law and shield people the law requires to be removed. Expanding ICE gives enforcement the capacity it lacks, and the body-camera requirement applies the same accountability standard set for police. ICE and CBP publish enforcement and encounter statistics that document the scale of the task.

> [!info]- What is a sanctuary city?
> A sanctuary city or jurisdiction is a local government that limits cooperation with federal immigration enforcement, for example by refusing to honor ICE detainer requests or restricting information sharing with federal authorities.

## Terrorism and Domestic Extremism

#### Position
Domestic terror threats are taken extremely seriously. Targeted surveillance drawn from public data is used to fight them, within the limits in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Privacy Rights|Authority & Civil Liberties]], and private companies may share flagged data with government agencies when users have given clear, specific, separate consent to government sharing, not consent buried in terms of service. Islamist and communist ideologies are monitored and treated as threats, with communism flagged at the same level of danger as Nazism. Anti-Western and anti-constitutional ideologies are treated as threats. Monitoring uses only public data and lawful process and imposes no legal penalty for belief. Islamist sympathy toward foreign terror agents and anti-Western governments is monitored, and it is punished as a threat of terror only where it amounts to a true threat or incitement to imminent violence.

> [!question]- "Does monitoring ideologies conflict with free speech absolutism?"
> Speech is protected up to true threats and incitement to imminent violence, the same as for any other ideology. Monitoring uses public data, where no expectation of privacy exists, and private data only through clear, specific, separate user consent. Islamist sympathy is monitored and punished as a threat of terror only where it amounts to a true threat or incitement to imminent violence. The government's disgust with Islamism and communism is expressed in its official voice through official condemnation, no platforms, no funding, and no public honors.

#### Key Elements
- Domestic terrorism treated as a top-priority threat.
- Targeted use of public surveillance data, within the limits in Authority & Civil Liberties.
- Private companies may share flagged user data with government agencies with clear, specific, separate user consent.
- Islamist and communist ideologies monitored as threats, communism at the same severity as Nazism.
- Anti-Western and anti-constitutional ideologies treated as threats.
- Islamist sympathy for foreign terror agents or anti-Western governments monitored, and punished as a terror threat only where it amounts to a true threat or incitement to imminent violence.

#### Rationale
Terrorism is a direct attack on sentient rights and on the trust a free society depends on. Public spaces and public data carry no expectation of privacy, so using them for security is consistent with the framework's privacy rules, while private data remains protected unless the user consents. Communism and Nazism are both treated as totalitarian ideologies that have produced mass killing, so they share the same threat level. That parity reflects each ideology's historical record, not current incident counts; federal data currently show racially or ethnically motivated and anti-government violent extremists committing the most domestic-terror incidents, and violent movements of that kind fall under the anti-constitutional threats already monitored. Federal law defines domestic terrorism as dangerous criminal acts intended to intimidate civilians or coerce government, and the designation process for foreign terrorist organizations provides the basis for treating support of foreign terror agents as a threat.

> [!info]- How does U.S. law define domestic terrorism?
> Under 18 U.S.C. § 2331(5), domestic terrorism covers acts dangerous to human life that violate criminal law, occur primarily within the United States, and appear intended to intimidate or coerce a civilian population, influence government policy by intimidation or coercion, or affect government conduct by mass destruction, assassination, or kidnapping.

## Gun Crime and Violence

#### Position
High-crime and gang areas receive intensive policing with swift and brutal crackdowns. Mass incarceration is modeled on the scale of El Salvador's approach to gang violence, carried out under full due process: individual charges, counsel, and trial. Riots, road blockades, and takeovers are swiftly and brutally shut down. Zero tolerance applies to criminals. The only focus of law enforcement is protecting the victims of perpetrators who violate sentient rights.

> [!question]- "Does mass incarceration fit with abolishing permanent incarceration?"
> Mass incarceration here describes the scale of removing gang members and violent offenders from the streets, not idle life sentences. Those incarcerated fall under the same rules set out in the sections above: compelled labor, restitution to victims, and the capital track for murder, rape, and repeat offenders.

#### Key Elements
- Intensive policing in high-crime and gang areas.
- Swift and brutal crackdowns on gang activity.
- Mass incarceration on the scale of El Salvador's, under full due process (individual charges, counsel, trial).
- Zero tolerance for riots, road blockades, and takeovers.
- Law enforcement focused solely on protecting victims of sentient-rights violations.

#### Rationale
Gun violence is concentrated in specific areas and among gang networks, so enforcement is concentrated there. El Salvador's large-scale arrests of gang members under its state of exception, in place since 2022, are the model for the scale of sustained gang incapacitation only. Here it is carried out under full due process, with individual charges, counsel, and trial; the suspension of due process under El Salvador's state of exception is rejected. Official homicides fell to 1.9 per 100,000 in 2024, though that count excludes gang members killed in confrontations, and human-rights monitors documented more than 260 deaths in custody from 2022 to mid-2024. Riots and blockades violate the rights of people and property owners who did nothing wrong, and tolerating them invites more. Directing law enforcement only at those who violate sentient rights keeps state power aimed at actual harm. FBI national crime statistics track violent crime and are the measure for these policies.

> [!info]- What is meant by zero tolerance?
> Zero tolerance means every offense in the targeted categories is met with enforcement and punishment, without informal leniency or warnings. Here it applies to criminals who violate others' rights and to riots, blockades, and takeovers.

## Victims' Rights

#### Position
Offenders forfeit their rights when they violate the rights of others. Restitution to the victim is paid by the offender. The offender serves a fixed compelled-labor term, set at sentencing in proportion to the harm; labor earnings and opt-in donations are applied to restitution, and any unpaid balance does not extend custody. Where the offender has a longer sentence or a death sentence, payment comes from the offender's bank accounts and assets. Uninvolved taxpayers fund no restitution beyond opt-in donations.

> [!question]- "What happens when an offender cannot pay?"
> The sentence is a fixed labor term set at sentencing, and the earnings it produces, together with opt-in donations, go toward the debt. An unpaid balance does not extend custody. Where the offender is serving a longer sentence or is executed, accounts and assets are used. The cost never shifts to taxpayers who had no part in the crime.

#### Key Elements
- Rights forfeited upon violating the rights of others.
- Restitution paid by the offender.
- A fixed, proportional compelled-labor term set at sentencing; earnings and opt-in donations go to restitution, and an unpaid balance does not extend custody.
- Restitution drawn from the offender's accounts and assets in long or capital sentences.
- No taxpayer-funded restitution beyond voluntary opt-in donations.

#### Rationale
The harm belongs to the person who caused it, so the duty to repair it does too. Forfeiture theory holds that those who violate others' rights lose the claim to the protections they denied. Federal law already recognizes the principle in part: the Mandatory Victims Restitution Act requires restitution for many federal crimes of violence and property offenses, and the Crime Victims' Rights Act guarantees victims full and timely restitution as provided by law. The position extends that principle by applying labor earnings and the offender's assets to repayment and keeping uninvolved taxpayers out of it. Most offenders have few assets (federal prosecutors classed 91% of \$110 billion in outstanding restitution as uncollectible in 2016), so compelled-labor earnings and opt-in donations carry most of the load where assets fall short.

> [!info]- What is restitution?
> Restitution is payment ordered from an offender to a victim to cover losses caused by the crime, such as medical costs, lost income, and damaged or stolen property. It differs from a fine, which is paid to the state.

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Offenders lose their rights when they violate others'. They pay the victim back through a fixed labor term set at sentencing, and their assets are used if they are serving a long sentence or executed. Taxpayers pay nothing except through voluntary donations.
