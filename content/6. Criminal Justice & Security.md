## Core Principles
Retribution, incapacitation, and the protection of victims' sentient rights take priority over rehabilitation. Those who murder, commit violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, or human trafficking, or who repeatedly violate the rights of others, forfeit the protections they denied their victims. Punishment is swift, certain, and public, and capital punishment applies only where guilt is established beyond any real doubt. Enforcement is heavy, with military-style action against cartels and high-crime threats.

## Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty

#### Position
Voluntary murder is punished by death. Violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, and human trafficking established under the same evidentiary standard are punished by death as well; they are egregious rights violations. The same four cruelty acts, extreme violence, intentional neglect (a chosen pattern of keeping it up), beating without drawing blood, and psychological abuse (withholding food, water, or shelter, not out of neglect but as deliberate punishment), are capital when done to a person if they cause lasting injury. Lasting injury is harm, physical or psychological, that requires ongoing treatment, does not heal within a recovery period measured in months, or leaves permanent damage. This includes neglect and other acts that leave lasting psychological harm. At the capital tier, lasting injury, including psychological injury, must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt through evaluation by a licensed clinician for humans, or a veterinarian or veterinary behaviorist for animals, documenting harm that requires ongoing treatment, persists for months, or is permanent. Below the capital tier, lasting injury must be proven by clear and convincing evidence, with the same clinician or veterinary evaluation. Without lasting injury, they are egregious, the [[#Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor|egregious tier]]: three marks at once, reaching the threshold: execution, experimentation, or life with labor, as the victim chooses. Beating means force beyond ordinary discipline: a closed fist, an object, or repeated strikes; a swat on the rear as ordinary discipline is not beating. See [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Animal Cruelty Law|Animal Cruelty Law]]. The sentence is mandatory once guilt is established; it is not left to the discretion of a jury weighing sympathy for the offender. Juveniles fall under the same rules: a minor whose crime is egregious enough reaches the capital tier or three marks like an adult. See [[#Juvenile Offenders]]. The offender's mental competence, intellectual capacity, or understanding of the proceedings does not reduce the sentence, because none of these change what was done to the victim. Incompetence alone does not lead to execution. An offender who was completely unaware and incompetent is removed from society, imprisoned or confined, because they are too dangerous to be around, as set out in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Free Will and Responsibility|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]. But an offender who cannot be changed from incompetence and remains a threat, a person who is violent and shows no sign of change, is a threat to society and is not kept alive merely for the sake of keeping them alive; they deserve to die. The same holds for an animal in confinement that remains a threat. See [[#Insanity Defense]]. Attempted murder is on the capital track: a deliberate attempt to kill is treated as murder whether or not the victim dies. So is conduct that knowingly imposes an extreme, unjustified risk of death on others, where the person understands the danger and goes ahead anyway with blatant disregard for life and rights. This applies even if no one is harmed. Street racing through traffic at extreme speed is one example. The test is awareness and severity: the person knew the danger and the risk was grave, not merely careless. Ordinary accidents and ordinary negligence stay outside the capital track and are handled as [[#Lesser Homicide|lesser homicide]].

> [!question]- "Hasn't the Supreme Court already ruled that mandatory death sentences, executing the mentally incompetent, and executing for rape are unconstitutional?"
> Yes. Under current doctrine all three are barred. *Woodson v. North Carolina* (1976) struck down mandatory death sentences for murder. *Ford v. Wainwright* (1986) and *Atkins v. Virginia* (2002) bar executing the insane and the intellectually disabled. *Coker v. Georgia* (1977) and *Kennedy v. Louisiana* (2008) bar the death penalty for rape where the victim was not killed. The position here does not pretend otherwise. It is a normative claim about what the law ought to be. Achieving it would require the Court to revisit those holdings or a constitutional amendment. The Eighth Amendment itself does not prohibit capital punishment; *Gregg v. Georgia* (1976) upheld it. The dispute is over which offenses and which offenders qualify, and that line has been drawn by the Court rather than by the text.

#### Key Elements
- Death is the mandatory sentence for voluntary murder.
- Juveniles fall under the same rules: a minor whose crime is egregious enough reaches the capital tier or three marks like an adult.
- Violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, and human trafficking proven to the capital evidentiary standard are capital offenses.
- Extreme violence, intentional neglect, beating without drawing blood (force beyond ordinary discipline), and withholding food, water, or shelter as deliberate punishment are capital when done to a person if they cause lasting injury, physical or psychological; otherwise they are egregious: three marks at once: execution, experimentation, or life with labor.
- Incompetence, intellectual disability, or diminished understanding is not a bar to execution where agency is present. Incompetence alone does not lead to execution; the offender is removed from society, imprisoned or confined. An offender who cannot be changed from incompetence and remains a threat deserves to die.
- Attempted murder, and knowingly imposing an extreme risk of death with blatant disregard for life, are capital even if no one dies; ordinary accidents and negligence are not.
- Offenders' own pre-existing accounts and assets, and forfeited property, pay restitution to victims and their families first; this is not payment for labor, and taxpayers do not pay, beyond voluntary donations.

#### Rationale
Under the rights-forfeiture principle, an agent who deliberately violates the most basic negative rights of another cannot claim the full protection of those same rights. Murder destroys the victim's subjective experience entirely and permanently. Rape is among the gravest violations of bodily integrity short of death. The response is matched to the severity of the act: proportionality in this framework means the punishment reflects the seriousness of the violation. For the worst violations, that is the most serious punishment available.

The refusal to exempt incompetent offenders follows from where moral weight is placed. The framework is concerned with outward rights violations, not with the inner condition of the violator. The victim is equally dead whether the killer understood the proceedings or not. Execution still requires agency: a person who was completely unaware and incompetent did not act as an agent, so they are removed from society, imprisoned or confined rather than executed. Discretionary sentencing introduces inconsistency: two offenders who commit the same act receive different outcomes depending on the jury, the county, and the quality of mitigation evidence. A mandatory rule treats like cases alike. The historical risk with mandatory statutes, noted in Woodson v. North Carolina, was that juries refused to convict to avoid the sentence; the clear-cut evidentiary standard narrows capital cases to those where guilt is not in serious dispute, which reduces that pressure.

Restitution follows the same logic of reciprocity. The offender, not uninvolved taxpayers, bears the cost of the harm. Where an offender is executed or serving a long sentence, restitution is drawn from their own pre-existing bank accounts and assets, and forfeited property and assets go to restitution, victims first. This is not payment for labor; it is the offender's own existing resources.

## Lesser Homicide

#### Position
A killing in self-defense that was excessive but close to justified is a lesser homicide, with a lighter sentence set by the judge. No duty to retreat and no location limit stay as they are. The prosecution bears the burden of proving intent for the lesser charge, and of proving any aggravating factor that pushes a killing to the capital track, not just the act itself. Self-defense itself is set out in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Self-Defense and Defense of Others|Section 3, Authority & Civil Liberties]].

> [!question]- "Why not treat any killing beyond justified self-defense as murder?"
> A person who was really under attack and went too far did not set out to violate anyone's rights; the aggressor started the encounter. Murder on the capital track requires a deliberate violation. A near-justified killing is still a wrong and is punished, but more lightly, and the prosecution has to prove the intent behind it.

#### Key Elements
- Excessive but close-to-justified self-defense killing: lesser homicide, lighter sentence set by the judge.
- No duty to retreat and no location limit, unchanged.
- Prosecution proves intent for the lesser charge, and any aggravating factor that moves a killing to the capital track.

#### Rationale
Current federal law already separates killing with malice from killing without it: manslaughter is the unlawful killing of a human being without malice. This framework keeps that line for self-defense that went too far, so a near-justified killing is not treated as murder on the capital track, while the prosecution carries the burden on intent and on any aggravating factor that would move a killing to the capital track.

##### Sources
- Cornell LII – [18 U.S.C. § 1112: Manslaughter](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1112)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> A self-defense killing that went too far but was close to justified is a lesser homicide with a lighter sentence set by the judge. The prosecution must prove intent, and must prove any aggravating factor for a capital charge.

## Human Trafficking

#### Position
Trafficking a person by force, fraud, or coercion, for labor or for sex, takes a person's body and labor without consent. Human trafficking, adult or child, is an egregious rights violation and is capital, as is kidnapping ([[#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty]]). The same strict evidentiary standard applies.

> [!question]- "Doesn't legal prostitution make trafficking easier?"
> Legal, regulated prostitution among consenting adults is separate from trafficking ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Drugs and Vice|Drugs and Vice]]). Consent is the line: once force, fraud, or coercion enters, it is trafficking, and the regulated trade is where that line can be enforced in the open.

#### Key Elements
- Human trafficking, adult or child, by force, fraud, or coercion is capital.
- Kidnapping is capital.
- Both are egregious rights violations, proven to the capital evidentiary standard.
- Consensual, regulated adult sex work stays legal; consent is the line.

#### Rationale
Federal law already makes sex trafficking by force, fraud, or coercion, and sex trafficking of any child, a federal crime. In preliminary federal child-welfare data for fiscal year 2024, sex trafficking was recorded as a circumstance in 570 entries into foster care.

##### Sources
- Cornell LII – [18 U.S.C. § 1591: Sex trafficking of children or by force, fraud, or coercion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1591)
- ACF Children's Bureau – [The AFCARS Dashboard, FFY 2024](https://acf.gov/sites/default/files/documents/cb/2024-afcars-dashboard-printable.pdf)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Human trafficking of adults or children, and kidnapping, are egregious rights violations and are capital. Consensual adult sex work stays legal; force, fraud, or coercion makes it trafficking.

## Evidentiary Standard

#### Position
Execution happens only when guilt is absolutely clear, without a shadow of a doubt. Ordinary proof beyond a reasonable doubt is not enough for a capital outcome. The standard requires corroborating objective evidence: body-camera or surveillance footage, multiple eyewitnesses backed by camera footage, physical evidence such as a body, and similar proof that does not depend on a single witness or on inference alone. Cases that fall short of this bar do not go to the capital track.

> [!question]- "Innocent people have been sentenced to death. Doesn't any death penalty guarantee that the state will eventually kill an innocent person?"
> The risk is real and the evidence for it is serious. Gross and colleagues (2014) estimated that at least 4.1 percent of defendants sentenced to death in the United States are likely innocent. That figure is the strongest argument against capital punishment as it currently operates, and it is the reason for this standard. Most known wrongful capital convictions rested on mistaken eyewitness identification, false confessions, unreliable informants, or flawed forensic testimony. Those are exactly the kinds of evidence a corroboration requirement excludes as a sufficient basis. Wrongful execution is treated as a rights violation by the state, with full accountability for those responsible. No human process reaches zero error. The claim here is narrower: restricting execution to cases with objective corroboration reduces the error rate far below the current system's.

#### Key Elements
- The capital standard is higher than proof beyond a reasonable doubt.
- Objective, corroborated evidence is required, not testimony or inference alone.
- Cases that fail the standard leave the capital track.
- Wrongful execution is treated as a rights violation by the state.

#### Rationale
A swift system with no long appeals is defensible only if guilt is certain at the front end. The longer the period between sentence and execution, the more the system relies on appeals to catch mistakes. This framework removes that period, so the burden moves to the trial itself. The evidentiary bar does the work that decades of appeals were meant to do.

Corroboration matters because the main sources of wrongful conviction are subjective. A recording, a body, and independent witnesses who agree with the recording are much harder to fabricate or misread than a single identification made under stress. The bar is deliberately strict. Its purpose is to make the capital track narrow, reliable, and limited to cases where the facts are not in serious dispute.

> [!info]- What counts as clear-cut evidence?
> Evidence that establishes both the act and the identity of the offender without depending on a single uncorroborated source. Examples include body-camera or surveillance footage of the act, multiple eyewitnesses whose accounts are confirmed by camera footage, and physical evidence such as a body. A confession alone, a single eyewitness, or circumstantial inference alone does not meet the standard.

##### Sources
- Gross, O'Brien, Hu & Kennedy – [Rate of False Conviction of Criminal Defendants Who Are Sentenced to Death](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1306417111) (PNAS, 2014)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Nobody is executed unless the proof is overwhelming and backed by objective evidence such as video and physical evidence, not just testimony. Because there is no long appeals process, the trial itself has to get it right.

## Wrongful Convictions and Compensation

#### Position
A wrongful conviction is a rights violation by the state. Compensation is owed when an official finding, either vacatur of the conviction by a court or a determination by a pardon board, establishes the person's innocence based on evidence that emerged after the trial. The prosecuting authority pays that compensation. An ordinary acquittal is not a finding of wrongful conviction and carries no compensation. Officials whose misconduct caused the conviction, such as fabricating or hiding evidence or coercing a confession, are held liable under the higher standard applied to those with great responsibility. A wrongful execution carries full accountability for those responsible.

> [!question]- "Why shouldn't everyone who is acquitted be compensated?"
> An acquittal means the state did not prove guilt to the required standard; it is the trial working as designed, not a finding that the state wronged the defendant. Compensation is reserved for convictions later overturned on new evidence through a formal finding, where the record shows the state punished an innocent person.

#### Key Elements
- Wrongful conviction is treated as a rights violation by the state.
- Compensation requires an official finding (vacatur or pardon-board determination) based on after-the-fact evidence.
- The prosecuting authority pays.
- Routine acquittals are not compensated.
- Officials who caused a wrongful conviction through misconduct are held liable.
- The capital evidentiary standard is the main safeguard.

#### Rationale
Wrongful convictions are documented at scale. The National Registry of Exonerations recorded 3,767 exonerations in the United States from 1989 through 2025, and Gross and colleagues estimated that at least 4.1% of defendants sentenced to death are likely innocent. As of August 2026, 38 states and the District of Columbia had statutes compensating the wrongly convicted. Placing the cost on the prosecuting authority ties the consequence to the party that brought the case, and requiring a formal finding on new evidence separates proven errors from ordinary verdicts. Officials' heightened liability follows [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Due Process and Rule of Law|Authority & Civil Liberties]].

##### Sources
- National Registry of Exonerations – [Exonerations in 2025](https://exonerationregistry.org/sites/exonerationregistry.org/files/documents/2025Exonerations.pdf)
- National Registry of Exonerations – [Compensation](https://exonerationregistry.org/compensation)
- Gross, O'Brien, Hu & Kennedy – [Rate of False Conviction of Criminal Defendants Who Are Sentenced to Death](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1306417111) (PNAS, 2014)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Convicting an innocent person is treated as the state violating their rights. When a court or pardon board formally clears someone on new evidence, the prosecution pays compensation; ordinary acquittals are not compensated. Officials who caused the error through misconduct are held liable.

## Capital Trials and the Abolition of Death Row

#### Position
Capital trials are capped at a maximum of 30 days. Death row is abolished as a concept. The sentencing hearing is held on the same-day sentencing date, so the victim's choice deadline and same-day execution align; the replacement judge's 30 days is the stated exception. There is no waiting period between sentencing and execution: the sentence is carried out on the sentencing date, for effect and to increase deterrence, except where the offender is placed on the experimentation track under [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Section 10]]. Between execution and experimentation, the victim has priority in choosing. If the victim does not respond or does not care, research can proceed, with research firms requesting bodies through a registry system. A research firm's request never overrides a victim who chose execution; the victim's choice always wins. The victim is notified at conviction and must choose by the sentencing hearing; no response by then counts as not responding. When multiple victims disagree, when the victim has died, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim. Where the victim has died, as in murder, the judge chooses between execution and experimentation in the victim's place; a dead victim does not count as not responding, so the case does not go to the registry. A judge is not permitted to refuse this choice: choosing is the judge's duty, and a judge who refuses is no longer a judge; a refusal sends the case neither to the registry nor to execution by default. The refusing judge is removed through the judicial conduct process, and a replacement judge decides the case within 30 days, even if that pushes sentencing past the same-day date; this is an exception to same-day sentencing and execution. Nobody is held for years or decades under a death sentence, except on the experimentation track, and nobody is held idle for life as a substitute for one.

> [!question]- "Doesn't removing the appeals process just mean executing people before their mistakes can be found?"
> Appeals exist mainly because the trial is not trusted to reach the right answer. In the current system, the gap between sentence and execution often runs to decades. That gap brings cost and uncertainty without a reliable gain in accuracy, since most of the time is procedural. This framework addresses accuracy at the trial stage instead: only cases meeting the clear-cut evidentiary standard reach a capital verdict. If the evidence needs years of review to settle, the case does not belong on the capital track. The tradeoff is explicit: a much narrower set of capital cases in exchange for finality within those cases.

#### Key Elements
- Capital trials last no longer than 30 days.
- Death row does not exist.
- Execution takes place on the sentencing date, except under the experimentation track in [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Section 10]], which also sets out the evidence that experimentation on living subjects remains necessary.
- Organs may be taken at same-day execution or from offenders on the experimentation track.
- Between execution and experimentation, the victim has priority in choosing. If the victim does not respond or does not care, research can proceed, with research firms requesting bodies through a registry system. A research firm's request never overrides a victim who chose execution; the victim's choice always wins. The victim is notified at conviction and must choose by the sentencing hearing; no response by then counts as not responding. When multiple victims disagree, when the victim has died, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim. Where the victim has died, as in murder, the judge chooses between execution and experimentation in the victim's place; a dead victim does not count as not responding, so the case does not go to the registry. A judge is not permitted to refuse this choice: choosing is the judge's duty, and a judge who refuses is no longer a judge; a refusal sends the case neither to the registry nor to execution by default. The refusing judge is removed through the judicial conduct process, and a replacement judge decides the case within 30 days, even if that pushes sentencing past the same-day date; this is an exception to same-day sentencing and execution.
- Only cases meeting the clear-cut evidentiary standard are eligible.

#### Rationale
Delay defeats both the retributive and the protective purpose of the sentence. A punishment carried out twenty years after the crime is disconnected from the act, burdens victims' families with decades of hearings, and costs the public far more than the sentence itself. The current system keeps the severity of the death penalty while removing its certainty and speed. That combination fails on every count: it is not swift, it is not certain, and it is enormously expensive.

A fixed trial window with immediate execution keeps the formal finding of guilt by a court, which the rule of law requires, while removing indefinite delay. Organs may be taken from offenders on either track: at execution on the sentencing date, or from offenders on the experimentation track, as set out in [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Organ Donation|Section 10]]. Combined with the evidentiary standard, it limits the death penalty to the cases where speed is justified because the facts are not in serious dispute. Capital trials under current rules have run well past 30 days (a California review cited by the state's 2008 commission put the average at 79 days), so the cap depends on the narrower capital track: only cases meeting the clear-cut standard qualify.

The cost of delay is concrete. The Federal Bureau of Prisons puts the average annual cost of holding one federal inmate at \$47,162 for fiscal year 2024. Across 45 states, Vera Institute data put the average at \$33,274 per inmate in 2015. Every year a condemned prisoner spends waiting adds that cost again, on top of the cost of the appeals themselves.

## Repeat Offenders and Forfeiture of the Right to Life

#### Position
The repeat-offender threshold is three marks. When an offender reaches three marks, the offender forfeits the right to life. The original formulation set that threshold at a third theft conviction; under the mark system each theft conviction counts as one mark, with sentences rising for each mark before the threshold. The principle is settled: someone who robs and steals again and again, and is caught each time, has shown that the community cannot be protected by ordinary sentences. Once the threshold is reached, three outcomes exist, and the victim chooses among them: execution, experimentation on the [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|experimentation track]], or life with labor, open only to offenders judged competent and well behaved. When multiple victims disagree, when the victim has died, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim. A later disqualifying act ends the labor status and results in execution.

> [!question]- "Executing someone for theft is wildly disproportionate. How can stealing ever justify death?"
> The sentence does not answer the last theft by itself. It answers a pattern. A single theft is punished with a finite sentence. The capital consequence attaches only after the offender has been convicted repeatedly, served sentences each time, and returned to violating others' rights. At that point the question is no longer what one theft deserves. It is whether the community must keep absorbing an offender who has shown, through repeated choices, that they will not respect the rights of others. Forfeiture in this framework scales with the pattern as well as the act. Repeated predation erodes the trust a free society depends on, and the state's first duty is to protect the innocent from that erosion. Experimentation and life with labor also mean execution is not the only outcome at the threshold, and the choice among the three belongs to the victim. When multiple victims disagree, when the victim has died, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim.

#### Key Elements
- The repeat-offender threshold is three marks; reaching it forfeits the right to life. The original threshold was a third theft conviction; each theft conviction now counts as one mark.
- Sentences increase with each conviction before the threshold.
- Three tiers by severity: capital (immediate execution, always public, or the experimentation track, with the victim having priority in choosing between them), above everything; egregious (three marks at once, reaching the threshold: the victim's choice of execution, experimentation, or life with labor); lesser (one or two marks by severity).
- Definitions: a mark is a recorded conviction counted toward the repeat-offender threshold, and every mark includes supervised hard labor; the fixed-term labor track is the lesser tier, one or two marks, served for a term the judge sets in proportion to the harm, with a known end date; three marks is the threshold: execution, experimentation, or life with labor; capital is immediate public execution or the experimentation track under the capital evidentiary standard.
- Every mark includes hard labor: one mark is a shorter fixed term, two marks a longer fixed term; three marks is the threshold, where the victim chooses execution, experimentation, or life with labor.
- Egregious crimes, including the four abuse acts without [[#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|lasting injury]] against a human or a higher-sentience animal, count as three marks immediately.
- Every sentence short of execution or experimentation includes hard labor, at every stage of the tracks.
- Life with labor is open only to candidates for labor, offenders judged competent and well behaved; incompetent offenders are not eligible. An offender on life with labor who commits another crime or shows poor behavior is moved to experimentation or execution.
- Multiple victims may decide together; if they remain undecided, the judge is the arbiter. Where the victim declines, does not respond, or is incapacitated, or where there is no victim, the judge chooses at three marks.
- Organs may be taken, without consent, from offenders at three marks placed on the execution or experimentation track.
- Two-mark crimes include statutory rape without force; lesser homicide, a negligent killing without extreme disregard for life; assault or robbery causing injury without lasting injury; domestic violence outside the four acts and without lasting injury, aggravated over the same act between strangers; and lesser cruelty, harm to a higher-sentience animal outside the four acts and without lasting injury, such as a single strike causing pain or abandonment.
- One-mark crimes include property crimes, cruelty to lower-sentience animals, and killing a lower-sentience animal, including fish unless the best available science places a species higher. Non-forced bestiality is one mark plus a fine of about 5 percent of the offender's own existing assets, minimum \$1,000, paid first to the animal's care, then the victim restitution fund, never the seizing agency; marks stack with distinct neglect.
- Lesser tier: one mark for property crimes (theft, fraud, and similar) and lower-sentience animal cruelty, such as to fish; two marks for cruelty to higher-sentience animals outside the four acts and short of lasting injury, such as to dogs, cats, or livestock.
- At the threshold there are three outcomes, and the victim chooses: execution, experimentation, or life with labor, open only to offenders judged competent and well behaved. When multiple victims disagree, when the victim has died, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim.
- A disqualifying act while in compelled labor results in execution.

#### Rationale
The rights-forfeiture principle holds that those who refuse to respect the basic negative rights of others cannot claim the full protection of those rights. Repetition matters to that judgment. A first offense may reflect a single bad choice. A pattern of offenses, each followed by punishment and release, shows a settled disposition to treat other people's property and safety as available for the taking. Each release under that pattern exposes new victims to a known risk.

The state exists first to protect negative rights. Continuing to release a known repeat predator into the community, or to house them indefinitely at public expense, is treated as a failure of that duty. Once the number is fixed in statute, the threshold makes the consequence predictable in advance: every offender knows where the line is, and every conviction before it is an explicit warning. Property offenders reoffend at the highest rate of any group (78.3% rearrested within five years in BJS data), and California's three-strikes law cut arrests among offenders facing a third strike by 17–20%; the statutory number should be set with that evidence.

The victim chooses among execution, experimentation, or, if they are competent and well behaved, life with labor. ## Juvenile Offenders

#### Position
There is no fixed age of criminal liability. Minors hold full negative rights and are liable for their actions, and the extent of that liability is driven by the severity of the crime. Custodial sentences for minors, like all custodial sentences, are served as compelled labor, with the vocational and educational training that applies at one or two marks. Juveniles fall under the same rules: a minor whose crime is egregious enough reaches the capital tier or three marks like an adult. There is no juvenile exception to the mandatory capital rule in [[#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty]].

> [!question]- "Current law forbids executing anyone for a crime committed under 18. Why allow it?"
> *Roper v. Simmons* (2005) bars the death penalty for offenses committed under 18, and *Miller v. Alabama* (2012) bars mandatory life without parole for juveniles. The framework departs from *Roper* as a normative position, and achieving it would require the Court to revisit that holding or a constitutional amendment. It also departs from *Miller*: minors whose crimes are egregious enough reach the capital tier or three marks under the same rules as adults.

#### Key Elements
- No fixed age of criminal liability.
- Liability scales with the severity of the crime.
- Juvenile custodial sentences are served as compelled labor with training.
- Juveniles fall under the same rules: a minor whose crime is egregious enough reaches the capital tier or three marks like an adult.

#### Rationale
Responsibility under the framework follows agency, and a fixed age line treats a fifteen-year-old who plans a murder the same as one who shoplifts. Tying liability to the severity of the act keeps the response proportional to what was done to the victim. Applying the same rules to minors whose crimes are egregious enough follows from liability scaling with the severity of the crime, as described in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]. The training provisions in [[#Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor]] apply most naturally to young offenders, whose working lives are longest.

- Miller v. Alabama (2012) – [Mandatory life without parole barred for juveniles](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/10-9646)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> There is no fixed age of criminal responsibility; how severely a minor is punished depends on how serious the crime was. Sentences are served as work with training, and a minor whose crime is egregious enough reaches the capital tier or three marks like an adult.

## Age of Consent

#### Position
The age of consent is 18, except under the close-in-age exception below. A minor cannot consent to sex with an adult, and sex with a person who cannot consent is rape. Minors cannot marry. A close-in-age exception applies only when both parties are minors, or when the older party is 18 or 19 and the younger party is 16 or 17: the gap may be no more than two years when the younger party is under 16, and no more than four years when the younger party is 16 or 17. The exception never covers anyone 20 or older. Violent rape, child molestation, and child rape are capital: where established under the capital evidentiary standard, they are punished by death, as set out in [[#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty]]. Statutory rape, meaning sex with a minor based on age alone with no force or violence, is not capital. It is still a crime and still wrong, and it goes through the regular criminal justice system with a serious non-capital sentence.

> [!question]- "Should a teenage couple close in age be treated the same as an adult preying on a child?"
> No. When both parties are minors, or the older party is 18 or 19 and the younger is 16 or 17, and they are within the allowed gap (two years if the younger is under 16, four years if the younger is 16 or 17), the close-in-age exception applies. The exception never covers anyone 20 or older.

#### Key Elements
- The age of consent is 18, except under the close-in-age exception below.
- Minors cannot consent to sex with adults.
- Close-in-age exception between minors, or between an 18- or 19-year-old and a 16- or 17-year-old: a gap of up to two years if the younger party is under 16, and up to four years if the younger party is 16 or 17. Never covers anyone 20 or older.
- Sex without valid consent is rape.
- Violent rape, child molestation, and child rape proven to the capital standard are capital offenses.
- Statutory rape (age-only, non-violent) is a serious non-capital crime handled through the regular criminal justice system.
- No marriage of minors.

#### Rationale
Consent is what separates a lawful act from a rights violation, and the framework already holds that minors cannot enter certain binding agreements: they cannot marry ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Marriage Law|Cultural & Social Issues]]) and are excluded from assisted death. The same principle applies to animals, which cannot consent to sexual activity ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Animal Cruelty Law|Animal Cruelty Law]]). Federal law currently criminalizes sexual acts with minors aged 12 to 15 by a person at least four years older (18 U.S.C. § 2243). State ages of consent range from 16 to 18.

Research on teen sexual partners finds that harm clusters at age gaps of about five to six years or more and grows stronger the younger the teen. Girls under 18 with partners six or more years older had pregnancy rates 3.7 times higher than girls with partners within two years. No study identifies a single optimal gap. The sliding scale here, tighter for younger teens, is the user's determination applying that gradient, not a directly measured threshold. Most U.S. states with a close-in-age exception use a gap of two to four years, with four years the most common.

## Insanity Defense

#### Position
Moral agency does not matter for whether a rights violation occurs: a mentally handicapped person who kills still commits murder. Whether agency was absent is decided by a panel of forensic psychiatrists and a judge, not a jury. An insane person who kills is confined. Those who are able do labor; those who cannot are confined in an asylum. All receive rehabilitation or treatment. Evaluation and progress checks begin after one to two years. If the person meets no progress checks and simply remains insane, they are executed. If they make progress, they remain in confinement and serve their sentence: a decade of confinement. When the decade is up, a person who is still deemed viable, rehabilitated, and showing great, genuine progress, as judged by a board of neutral clinicians in the asylum, may be released back into society under supervision for a period. The burden for release sits entirely on the confined person: the panel must find by clear and convincing evidence that agency is restored and that the risk of violence is minimal, and a single dissenting expert blocks release. The killing still counts as a strike toward the repeat-offender threshold in [[#Repeat Offenders and Forfeiture of the Right to Life]].

> [!question]- "Isn't it cruel to hold the mentally ill responsible?"
> A rights violation is a violation whatever the offender's mental state. An insane person who kills is confined and treated, and is given the chance to make progress. Release comes only after a decade of confinement and a finding that agency is restored and the risk of violence is minimal; a person who makes no progress is executed.

> [!question]- "Doesn't current law forbid executing the insane?"
> Yes. Under current doctrine it is barred. *Ford v. Wainwright* (1986) bars executing the currently insane and *Atkins v. Virginia* (2002) bars executing the intellectually disabled. The position here does not pretend otherwise. It is a normative claim about what the law ought to be. Achieving it would require the Court to revisit those holdings or a constitutional amendment.

#### Key Elements
- Moral agency does not matter for whether a rights violation occurs; a mentally handicapped person who kills still commits murder.
- A panel of forensic psychiatrists and a judge, not a jury, decides whether agency was absent.
- The panel decides agency only; whether the person committed the act is decided by a unanimous jury ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Role of Juries|Role of Juries]]).
- The panel is drawn from a court roster of court-certified forensic psychiatrists, and each side can strike for conflict of interest.
- An insane person who kills is confined: labor for those who are able, an asylum for those who cannot.
- Rehabilitation or treatment, with evaluation and progress checks beginning after one to two years.
- No progress, remaining insane: execution. Where agency is absent there is no forfeiture-based punishment; executing a persistently insane, violent offender who makes no progress is removal of an unchangeable threat, not forfeiture ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Free Will and Responsibility|Free Will and Responsibility]]).
- Progress: a decade of confinement.
- After the decade, release under supervision for a period, only if a board of neutral clinicians finds great, genuine progress.
- The burden for release sits entirely on the confined person: clear and convincing evidence that agency is restored and violence risk is minimal; a single dissenting expert blocks release.
- The killing counts as a strike toward the repeat-offender threshold.
- An insane offender whose act is not a killing receives no marks and is confined in secure treatment until no longer dangerous; the decade-plus confinement and panel release apply to killings.

#### Rationale
Moral agency is irrelevant to whether a violation occurred ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Free Will and Responsibility|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]). In practice the insanity defense is rare: an eight-state study found it was raised in one percent of all felony cases, and only 26 percent of those who raised it were acquitted as not guilty by reason of insanity. The Supreme Court held in *Kahler v. Kansas* (2020) that due process does not require a state to adopt any particular insanity test. Under *Ford v. Wainwright* (1986), current doctrine holds that the Eighth Amendment prohibits executing the insane.

> Mental illness does not undo a rights violation: a mentally handicapped person who kills still commits murder. Whether agency was absent is decided by a panel of forensic psychiatrists and a judge, not a jury. An insane killer is confined, with labor or an asylum, and treated, with progress checks starting after one to two years. No progress means execution; progress means a decade of confinement, then possible supervised release if neutral clinicians find genuine progress and the release standard is met. The killing counts as a strike.

## Statutes of Limitations

#### Position
There is no statute of limitations for murder, violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, or human trafficking. All people are held liable for their actions, and the passage of time does not restore rights that were forfeited by a deliberate violation. Old convictions count toward the repeat-offender threshold indefinitely; there is no time limit on the look-back. There is no time limit on restitution or civil claims: restitution is owed forever until it is paid. Theft, fraud, and other non-capital crimes have limitation periods that scale with severity and the value involved: minor offenses (under \$500) may be prosecuted within 3 years, mid-level offenses (\$500 up to \$10,000) within 6 years, and major offenses (\$10,000 and up) within 20 years. The thresholds may be adjusted each year to rise with the national average wage.

> [!question]- "Doesn't evidence degrade over time, risking wrongful convictions in old cases?"
> The protection against that risk is the evidentiary standard, not a deadline. A capital conviction still requires proof to the strict standard in [[#Evidentiary Standard]], however old the case.

#### Key Elements
- No statute of limitations for murder, violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, or human trafficking.
- Time does not restore forfeited rights.
- Old convictions count toward the repeat-offender threshold indefinitely.
- No time limit on restitution or civil claims: restitution is owed until it is fully paid.
- Non-capital crimes use tiered limitation periods by severity and value: 3 years for minor (under \$500), 6 years for mid-level (\$500 up to \$10,000), 20 years for major (\$10,000 and up); thresholds may rise yearly with the national average wage.
- The evidentiary standard, not a deadline, protects against weak old cases.

#### Rationale
Forfeiture follows from the act, not from how quickly it is prosecuted ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#General Rules of Forfeiture|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]). Federal law already sets no limitation period for offenses punishable by death (18 U.S.C. § 3281). For non-capital crimes, the federal default is 5 years (18 U.S.C. § 3282). Norway scales its limitation periods by the maximum sentence, from 2 years for the least serious offenses to 25 years for the most serious, and France sets 20 years for serious crimes and 6 years for mid-level offenses. The dollar thresholds are set below what the typical full-time worker earns in the same time: the full-time median is \$65,052 a year (about \$1,251 a week), so \$500 is under half a week's pay and \$10,000 is about eight weeks. Because the framework ranks retribution and incapacitation above rehabilitation, more serious offenses carry longer periods.

Other non-capital crimes have tiered limits of 3, 6, or 20 years: under \$500, \$500 up to \$10,000, and \$10,000 and up.

## Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor

#### Position
Idle permanent incarceration is abolished in its entirety. Life sentences served in a cell and indefinite warehousing of prisoners end; every long-term sentence, including life with labor at the repeat threshold, is served as work. Every custodial sentence is served as compelled labor, generally under supervision, on government projects such as building, cleaning, and the production of goods. The labor is hard labor: difficult, demanding work, not comfortable or easy. Sentences for first and second offenses are greatly increased in length, so that early offenses are not treated as cheap strikes before the threshold. For first and second offenders, the compelled-labor term includes vocational and educational training, and offenders may be placed in work for private employers; the offender is not paid, and the employer pays for the work into restitution and rehabilitation. Cognitive-behavioral programs may run during the sentence. None of these shortens the sentence, and none is funded by taxpayers. Offenders are not paid while in prison: costs are covered only by opt-in donations or private employers. Rehabilitation remains secondary to retribution, incapacitation, and victim protection. Rights forfeited by an offender are forfeited for the duration of the sentence, and at the repeat threshold they are forfeited for life. Sentences follow three tiers by severity. The capital tier, above everything, is immediate execution, always public ([[#Public Executions]]), or the experimentation track. The egregious tier covers grave abuse that does not cause [[#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|lasting injury]]: offenders who do grave harm through abuse, often driven by anger, without killing, such as beating without drawing blood, intentional neglect, or withholding food, water, or shelter as punishment. They sit below the capital tier of serial killers, murderers, and rapists, but the abuse is egregious, so it counts as three marks at once: execution, experimentation, or life with labor, as the victim chooses. Lesser crimes carry one or two marks by severity and are served on the fixed-term labor track: a term the judge sets at sentencing in proportion to the harm, with a known end date, shorter for one mark and longer for two. Rehabilitation is mandatory on every labor sentence and runs inside it; it never shortens it, and it is paid by opt-in donations or private employers, never by taxpayers.

> [!question]- "Isn't lifelong compelled labor just slavery? Doesn't the Thirteenth Amendment forbid it?"
> The Thirteenth Amendment prohibits slavery and involuntary servitude "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted." Compelled labor imposed on a convicted offender falls within that stated exception, so the Constitution does not forbid it. The moral objection is a separate question and deserves a direct answer. Slavery as historically practiced seized people who had violated no one's rights, on the basis of race, and held them and their children as property. Compelled labor here applies only to individuals convicted of violating others' rights, as a consequence of their own choices. Under the forfeiture principle, an offender's claim against compelled labor is lost through the violation, just as a claim to liberty is lost through imprisonment. At the threshold it is one of the victim's three options, alongside execution and experimentation, not an addition to them. Lifelong labor, whether for an egregious crime or at the repeat threshold, is still not ownership. The offender has forfeited their liberty for life through their own violations, each proven in court; at the threshold, it is one of the three outcomes that forfeiture of the right to life allows, offered only to offenders judged competent and well behaved, and only where the victim chooses it in place of execution or experimentation. Ownership is claimed over people who forfeited nothing, passes to heirs, and reaches the owned person's children. Lifelong labor reaches no one but the offender, ends with the offender's life, and leaves untouched every right the offender did not forfeit. The state holds a sentence, not a person. The offender's labor offsets the cost their crimes have imposed on the public.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the Eighth Amendment forbid hard labor, public execution, and the experimentation track?"
> It may, as courts read the ban on cruel and unusual punishment. This framework supports amending or abolishing the Eighth Amendment as needed so that hard labor, public execution, and the experimentation track are permitted.

> [!question]- "If an offender's labor is compelled, isn't the state owning them?"
> No. For lesser crimes, the offender forfeited their rights for a term the judge sets in proportion to the harm, with a known end date; when it ends, full rights return. For egregious crimes and at the repeat threshold, where the victim chooses life with labor, the sentence is for life, and it rests on the offender's own forfeiture: it reaches no one else and leaves every unforfeited right intact. Ownership is claimed over people who forfeited nothing and passes no judgment on what the person did.

> [!question]- "Doesn't adding rehabilitation contradict ranking it below punishment?"
> No. The ranking is unchanged: the sentence length, the labor requirement, and restitution are fixed at sentencing, and no program shortens them. Training and therapy take place within the term the offender already serves. They are included because they serve the framework's own aims: an offender who reoffends less creates fewer new victims.

#### Key Elements
- Idle permanent incarceration, including life without parole in a cell, is abolished.
- All custodial sentences are served as compelled labor on government projects, generally under supervision.
- Compelled labor is hard labor at all stages of the tracks: difficult and demanding, not comfortable or easy.
- Sentences for first and second offenses are greatly increased in length.
- Offenders at one or two marks receive vocational programs for training and rehabilitation inside the term, alongside hard labor; the programs never shorten it.
- Private-employer prison work is permitted; the offender is not paid, and the employer pays for the work into restitution and rehabilitation.
- Cognitive-behavioral programs may run during the sentence; no program shortens it.
- Egregious tier (grave abuse without lasting injury): three marks at once; execution, experimentation, or life with labor, as the victim chooses.
- Lesser tier: one or two marks; the fixed-term labor track, a term the judge sets in proportion to the harm, with a known end date.
- Rehabilitation programs are funded only by opt-in donations or private employers, never by taxpayers; offenders are not paid while in prison.
- Life with labor is available at the three-mark threshold only to offenders judged competent and well behaved, and only where the victim chooses it over execution or experimentation.
- Lifelong labor is not ownership: it follows only from the offender's own forfeiture, proven in court, reaches no one else, and leaves every unforfeited right intact; at the threshold it is the victim's alternative to execution or experimentation.

#### Rationale
Idle incarceration costs the public heavily while giving nothing back to victims or the community. State and local governments spent \$87 billion on corrections in 2021. The Federal Bureau of Prisons reports an average cost of \$47,162 per federal inmate per year (FY2024), and Vera Institute data put the 2015 state average at \$33,274. These averages include fixed costs such as facilities and administration, so removing one prisoner saves less than the average figure. Even so, a life sentence commits taxpayers to housing, feeding, and guarding an offender for decades, which is a burden on people who did nothing wrong.

Compelled labor turns the sentence into productive work that offsets part of that cost. It is not claimed to pay for incarceration in full. The federal model, Federal Prison Industries, receives no annual appropriation and runs as a self-funding revolving fund. In fiscal year 2025 it employed 19,560 inmates and reported \$14.98 million in net income, though it posted a \$37.4 million loss from operations, made up by interest and other income. Its output is real but small relative to the cost of the prison system. The stronger case for structured prison work lies in outcomes: the Washington State Institute for Public Policy estimates that correctional-industries programs return about \$4,975 in benefits per participant, largely through reduced reoffending.

Longer sentences for early offenses address a gap the threshold would otherwise create. If only the threshold carried serious consequences, the first and second offenses would look cheap by comparison. Raising their cost means every offense carries weight, and the threshold is not the only deterrent in the system. Longer sentences also serve incapacitation and desert, not only deterrence, and they operate alongside the certainty measures in this section: expanded policing, swift trials, and execution on the sentencing date.

Training within the labor term serves victims and public safety. A RAND meta-analysis found that incarcerated adults who took part in correctional education had 43% lower odds of returning to prison, a 13-percentage-point reduction in three-year reincarceration risk, and vocational training raised the odds of post-release employment by 28%. Cognitive-behavioral programs reduced 12-month reoffending from 40% to 30% on average across 58 studies, with the largest effects among higher-risk offenders. Lower reoffending means fewer new victims.

> [!info]- What is compelled labor?
> Required, generally supervised hard labor, difficult and not comfortable or easy, on government projects (construction, cleaning, production of goods, and similar tasks) performed by convicted offenders as their sentence. It replaces idle time in a cell. For lesser crimes (one or two marks) it lasts for a finite term with a known end date; egregious crimes count as three marks, where life with labor is one of the victim's three options. Past the threshold, for offenders judged competent and well behaved, it lasts for life.

## Restorative Justice Secondary to Justice

#### Position
Restorative justice is secondary to justice. Reconciliation between offender and victim, victim-offender mediation, and community reintegration may have a place, but they are not what punishment is for, and they never come ahead of it. The criminal process exists first to protect the innocent, give offenders what their violations deserve, and remove ongoing threats. Restorative practices run only inside a sentence already matched to the violation; they never reduce or replace the sentence, the labor, or restitution. Victims are owed restitution from the offender first, not a process designed around the offender's restoration. A victim may choose to meet the offender, for example to ask questions or hear an apology; the meeting is the victim's option and does not alter the sentence. Reduced reoffending that follows from compelled labor is a welcome side effect, not a purpose of punishment.

> [!question]- "Doesn't restorative justice reduce reoffending and give victims a voice that punishment ignores?"
> Victims are given a direct material remedy through restitution paid from the offender's own assets, and the protection of the community is placed at the center of the process. Restorative programs that make the offender's reintegration the central purpose of the system put it in the wrong order. This framework places the victim's rights and the safety of everyone else first; restoration comes second, inside the sentence, and the mandatory rehabilitation on every [[#Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor|labor sentence]] is part of that second place. The state's criminal process is organized around justice, not around mediation.

#### Key Elements
- Restorative justice is secondary to justice: reconciliation, mediation, and reintegration come after protection, desert, and incapacitation, never ahead of them.
- Restorative practices never reduce or replace the sentence, labor, or restitution.
- Punishment serves protection, desert, and incapacitation.
- Restitution comes from the offender, not the public.
- Victims may choose to meet the offender; the meeting does not alter the sentence.

#### Rationale
Under the framework, a violation of negative rights calls for a response matched to the violation. Restorative approaches move the focus from what was done to the victim toward the future of the person who did it. The protective and retributive purposes of punishment are the basis of the system, so restoration can follow only inside a sentence already matched to the violation, never in place of it. A meeting chosen by the victim is a different matter: in randomized trials where victims chose to meet offenders, more victims were satisfied with how their cases were handled (60% versus 46%), and they reported fewer post-traumatic stress symptoms and less desire for revenge.

> The justice system is first about protecting victims and the public and giving offenders what they deserve; reconciling offenders with victims or reintegrating them comes second and never changes the sentence. Victims are repaid by the offender. A victim may choose to meet the offender, but the meeting never changes the sentence.

## Public Executions

#### Position
Executions are carried out in public or recorded and made openly available, including by livestream. The state's use of lethal force is visible to the citizens in whose name it is carried out, not hidden behind closed doors or presented as a quiet medical procedure. Public execution changes only the manner and visibility of the sentence. It does not lower the evidentiary standard, the trial process, or any other requirement.

> [!question]- "Isn't public execution barbaric spectacle? Shouldn't the state at least keep it out of sight?"
> Keeping executions out of sight does not make them less real. It makes them less accountable. When the state takes a life, the public that authorized it has a claim to see what is done in its name. Secrecy allows both sides to avoid the reality: supporters avoid confronting what they support, and officials avoid scrutiny of how it is carried out. Transparency serves accountability first. It also serves the deterrent purpose that only works if potential offenders see that the sentence is real and certain.

#### Key Elements
- Executions are public, or recorded and openly available, including by livestream.
- Visibility applies to the method of execution only; evidentiary and trial standards are unchanged.
- The purposes are accountability to the public and visible certainty of punishment.

#### Rationale
A government exercising its gravest power should do so in the open. Transparency is consistent with the broader demand in this framework for accountability of officials, who are held to higher standards than ordinary citizens. It also connects the punishment to its purpose: a sentence intended to show that grave violations bring certain consequences cannot show anything if it is hidden.

Britain ended public executions with the Capital Punishment Amendment Act 1868, which moved them inside prison walls; the framework reverses that move toward secrecy.

## Deterrence and the Justification for Capital Punishment

#### Position
The empirical research on whether capital punishment deters murder is, at best, insufficient to support a conclusion either way. That is not evidence that the death penalty fails to deter. It is evidence that the studies cannot tell. The weakness of the findings largely reflects the system being studied: slow trials, decades of appeals, and uncertain outcomes. The reforms set out above (swift trials, no death row, execution on the sentencing date, and public visibility) are designed to make the punishment certain and swift, which are the conditions under which deterrence is most expected to operate. The justification for the death penalty does not depend on a deterrence claim. It rests on retribution, incapacitation, and the forfeiture of rights by those who commit the gravest violations.

> [!question]- "If the research can't show that the death penalty deters, isn't the case for it gone?"
> No. The National Research Council's 2012 review found that the existing studies are "not informative" about whether capital punishment increases, decreases, or has no effect on homicide rates, and recommended they not be used to inform policy in either direction. A finding that the research is uninformative is not a finding of no effect. The case for capital punishment here was never built on deterrence statistics. It rests on desert and protection: a murderer has forfeited the right to life, and an executed offender cannot harm anyone again. Deterrence is a possible additional benefit, not the foundation.

#### Key Elements
- The deterrence evidence is insufficient to support a conclusion in either direction.
- Insufficient evidence is not evidence against deterrence.
- The current system's delays and uncertainty undercut any deterrent effect it might have.
- The reforms aim for certainty and swiftness, which the deterrence literature identifies as the factors that matter most.
- The justification rests on retribution, incapacitation, and rights forfeiture.

#### Rationale
The National Research Council panel identified recurring flaws in the deterrence literature. Studies failed to account for the effect of non-capital alternatives such as life imprisonment. They relied on untested assumptions about how potential offenders perceive the risk of execution. Their results shifted with small changes in statistical specification. The panel concluded that this body of work cannot settle the question.

The broader deterrence research points in a consistent direction. Daniel Nagin's reviews conclude that the certainty of apprehension and punishment has far more consistent support as a deterrent than the severity of punishment. The American death penalty as currently practiced is severe but neither certain nor swift. Execution is rare, comes decades after the crime, and is surrounded by uncertainty about whether it will happen at all. Under those conditions weak deterrence findings are what the theory would predict. Historical evidence also suggests visibility matters: Phillips (1980) found that homicides in London dropped in the weeks following heavily publicized executions, though the effect was short-lived.

None of this proves that a swift, certain, public death penalty would deter. It shows that the existing studies did not test one. The case stands on other grounds. Retribution gives the offender what the violation deserves. Incapacitation ensures the offender never violates anyone's rights again. Reciprocity holds that those who reject the basic framework of non-aggression cannot claim its full protections. These justifications hold regardless of what future deterrence research shows.

> [!info]- What is the difference between certainty and severity of punishment?
> Certainty is the likelihood that an offender will actually be caught and punished. Severity is how harsh the punishment is if it happens. Deterrence research generally finds that certainty has a stronger and more reliable effect on behavior than severity. A harsh punishment that is unlikely or delayed for decades deters less than people expect.

## Police Power and Accountability

#### Position
Police are heavily funded and expanded, and officers are well paid and well respected in proportion to the danger and responsibility of the work. That status carries a higher standard: officers who break the law face greater criminal liability and punishment than ordinary citizens. Misconduct is investigated by nonpartisan, independent third parties that operate like compliance audit firms, not by the department under review. Body cameras are federally mandated for every officer who deals with the public. Qualified immunity as currently formulated is abolished. In its place, a plaintiff must show that the officer's conduct was objectively unreasonable given what the officer knew at the time. A good-faith defense applies when the officer, in a split-second decision, relied on training, a supervisor's order, or a reasonable reading of unclear law. The defense fails if the officer ignored clear precedent or obvious warning signs. Current use-of-force standards are a starting point for reform, not the final word. Graham v. Connor and Tennessee v. Garner set the constitutional floor, not the ceiling. Force is used only when necessary to stop a rights violation or an imminent threat, and only in proportion to it. Deadly force is used only when necessary to protect life from an imminent threat of death or serious injury. Officers use de-escalation and less harmful means when safe and feasible, departments plan operations to minimize the need for force, and every use and display of a firearm is reported. Patrol requires one high bar of physical fitness and strength for all officers. Officers who do not meet that bar are not assigned to patrol; they are assigned to verbal confrontations in the field and administrative work, and female officers in this group also handle interactions with women.

> [!question]- "Does higher liability for officers conflict with heavy funding and support for police?"
> No. Pay, respect, and power are paired with stricter accountability, not shielded from it. Qualified immunity as currently applied under the "clearly established law" test lets officials escape personal consequences for rights violations, so it is abolished and replaced with an objective-reasonableness standard and a narrow good-faith defense. Strong policing and strict liability are treated as parts of the same commitment: enforcement exists to protect sentient rights, and an officer who violates them is held to account more severely, not less.

#### Key Elements
- Heavy funding and expansion of police forces, with high pay and public respect.
- Higher criminal liability and punishment for officers who break the law than for ordinary citizens.
- Independent, nonpartisan third-party investigation of misconduct.
- Federal body-camera mandate for all public-facing officers.
- Qualified immunity abolished; plaintiffs must show conduct was objectively unreasonable given what the officer knew, with a good-faith defense for split-second reliance on training, orders, or a reasonable reading of unclear law, which fails if clear precedent or obvious warning signs were ignored.
- Use of force must be necessary and proportionate to stopping a rights violation or imminent threat; current standards are a starting point for reform, backed by de-escalation, operational planning, and mandatory reporting of every firearm use and display.
- One high physical standard for all patrol officers; officers who do not meet it are assigned to verbal confrontations and administrative roles, with female officers also handling interactions with women.

#### Rationale
Policing is the main instrument for protecting sentient and property rights in daily life, and an understaffed or poorly paid force cannot provide that protection. The authority to use force is also the authority most open to abuse, so accountability must grow with power. Independent investigation removes the conflict of interest in departments judging their own officers. Body cameras create a record that protects both the public and officers acting lawfully; a randomized trial in Rialto, California found that shifts with cameras saw fewer use-of-force incidents and fewer complaints than shifts without them. The Supreme Court's test in Graham v. Connor asks only whether force was objectively reasonable to an officer on the scene, and that test alone is insufficient. State force must stop a rights violation and be necessary and proportionate, in line with the European Court of Human Rights' "absolutely necessary" standard and the UN Basic Principles, which allow lethal force only when strictly unavoidable to protect life. Federal records have missed more than half of police killings, so the current standards cannot be shown to be adequate. Wording alone does not change outcomes: California's 2019 "necessary" standard showed no measurable reduction in deadly force. Practice does: in a randomized trial in Louisville, de-escalation training cut use of force by 28%, and requiring officers to report every time they point a gun was linked to fewer deaths. Patrol work can require physically overpowering resisting suspects, so physical standards are set by the demands of the job.

> [!info]- What is qualified immunity?
> Qualified immunity is a judge-made doctrine that shields government officials from personal civil liability under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 unless their conduct violated "clearly established" statutory or constitutional rights that a reasonable person would have known. It grew from Pierson v. Ray (1967) and took its modern form in Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982). It concerns civil suits, not criminal prosecution.

## Drug Policy

#### Position
Marijuana is legal, and use of other drugs is decriminalized, but drug use in public areas is prohibited. Full prohibition would be ideal in a perfect society, but it does not work in practice and is not pursued. Cartels are designated as terroristic national-security threats and destroyed with military force. Enforcement resources move away from private users and toward the organizations that traffic drugs through violence.

> [!question]- "Does decriminalization contradict a hardline stance on crime?"
> No. Private drug use by an adult does not by itself violate the sentient rights of others, and prohibition has failed to stop it while feeding the black market that funds cartels. The hardline stance is aimed at rights violators: cartels that murder, traffic, and corrupt governments are treated as terrorist enemies, and public use that imposes on others remains banned. Decriminalization narrows enforcement to where harm to others actually occurs.

#### Key Elements
- Marijuana legal; decriminalization of other drug use and possession for personal use.
- Ban on drug use in public areas.
- Full prohibition acknowledged as ideal but unrealistic.
- Cartels designated as terrorist national-security threats.
- Military force used to destroy cartel organizations.

#### Rationale
The framework limits state coercion to protecting the rights of others, and private consumption falls outside that line while public use spills onto others. Portugal decriminalized the personal possession of all drugs in 2001, replacing criminal penalties with administrative responses, and the European drug agency's country reports track that model. On cartels, the United States designated several cartels and transnational gangs, including the Sinaloa Cartel and Cartel de Jalisco Nueva Generación, as Foreign Terrorist Organizations in February 2025, following a January 2025 executive order. That designation treats cartels as security threats rather than ordinary criminal enterprises, which matches the framework's position.

> [!info]- What is the difference between decriminalization and legalization?
> Decriminalization removes criminal penalties for using or possessing drugs, though use can still be restricted or handled through civil or administrative measures. Legalization makes production and sale lawful and regulated. The position here is that marijuana is legal and other drugs are decriminalized for use, without a legal commercial market for them.

## Domestic Violence

#### Position
Violence by an aggressor toward their own spouse or family is worse than violence between strangers, because it betrays the people the aggressor is supposed to protect. Murder, attempted murder, violent rape, and kidnapping inside a household are capital as anywhere else, and so are the four cruelty acts, extreme violence, intentional neglect, beating without drawing blood, and withholding food, water, or shelter as deliberate punishment, when they cause [[#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|lasting injury]]. Without lasting injury, these acts, and other grave abuse that does serious harm without killing, often driven by anger, are egregious, the [[#Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor|egregious tier]]: three marks at once, reaching the threshold: execution, experimentation, or life with labor, as the victim chooses. Beating a spouse without drawing blood is capital if it causes lasting injury and otherwise egregious. Beating means force beyond ordinary discipline: a closed fist, an object, or repeated strikes; a swat on the rear as ordinary discipline is not beating. These offenders sit below the capital tier of serial killers, murderers, and rapists; their lifelong labor rests on their own forfeiture and is not ownership. Other domestic violence is an aggravated offense, carrying an extra penalty on top of the penalty for the same act against a stranger. Protective orders are issued by a judge on evidence, and firearms are removed only through the red flag rule: a court finding that the person directly imposes a severe, non-consensual risk of serious harm on others ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Gun Rights|Gun Rights]]). Domestic violence convictions count toward the repeat-offender threshold.

> [!question]- "Why punish violence against family more harshly than violence against strangers?"
> The aggressor holds a position of trust and often power over the victim, who may depend on them and cannot easily leave. Abusing that trust is a further betrayal on top of the act itself. The same act gets the same base penalty, and the household setting adds to it.

#### Key Elements
- The four cruelty acts (extreme violence, intentional neglect, beating without drawing blood, withholding food, water, or shelter as punishment) against a spouse or family member are capital if they cause lasting injury, physical or psychological.
- Without lasting injury, those acts and other grave abuse are egregious: three marks at once: execution, experimentation, or life with labor.
- Other domestic violence is aggravated with an extra penalty.
- Egregious domestic abuse reaches the three-mark threshold at once; the victim chooses among execution, experimentation, or life with labor. When multiple victims disagree, when the victim has died, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim.
- Capital rules apply unchanged inside a household.
- Protective orders by a judge on evidence; firearm removal only through the red flag court finding.
- Convictions count toward the repeat-offender threshold.

#### Rationale
In preliminary federal data for fiscal year 2024, domestic violence was recorded as a circumstance in 9% (16,101) of children's entries into foster care.

Offenders forfeit their rights when they violate the rights of others. At the three-mark threshold, the victim chooses among execution, experimentation, or, if the offender is competent and well behaved, life with labor ([[#Repeat Offenders and Forfeiture of the Right to Life]]). When multiple victims disagree, when the victim has died, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim. Restitution to the victim is paid by the offender. The offender serves a fixed compelled-labor term, set at sentencing in proportion to the harm; the offender is not paid for that labor; what private employers pay for the work, together with opt-in donations, is applied to restitution, and any unpaid balance does not extend custody. Where the offender is executed or serving a long sentence, restitution is drawn from the offender's own pre-existing bank accounts and assets, and forfeited property and assets likewise go to restitution, victims first. This is not payment for labor; it is the offender's own existing resources. Uninvolved taxpayers fund no restitution beyond opt-in donations.

> [!question]- "What happens when an offender cannot pay?"
> The sentence is a fixed labor term set at sentencing. The offender is not paid; what private employers pay for the work, together with opt-in donations, goes toward the debt. An unpaid balance does not extend custody. Where the offender is serving a longer sentence or is executed, the offender's own pre-existing bank accounts and assets are used, and forfeited property and assets go to restitution, victims first; this is not payment for labor. The cost never shifts to taxpayers who had no part in the crime.

#### Key Elements
- Rights forfeited upon violating the rights of others.
- At the three-mark threshold, the victim chooses among execution, experimentation, or life with labor; life with labor is open only to offenders judged competent and well behaved. When multiple victims disagree, when the victim has died, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim.
- Restitution paid by the offender.
- A fixed, proportional compelled-labor term set at sentencing; the offender is not paid; private-employer payments for the work and opt-in donations go to restitution, and an unpaid balance does not extend custody.
- Restitution drawn from the offender's own pre-existing bank accounts and assets when the offender is executed or serving a long sentence; forfeited property and assets also go to restitution, victims first. These are the offender's own existing resources, not payment for labor.
- No taxpayer-funded restitution beyond voluntary opt-in donations.
- Private employers pay for prison work into restitution; the offender receives no wages.

#### Rationale
The harm belongs to the person who caused it, so the duty to repair it does too. Forfeiture theory holds that those who violate others' rights lose the claim to the protections they denied. Federal law already recognizes the principle in part: the Mandatory Victims Restitution Act requires restitution for many federal crimes of violence and property offenses, and the Crime Victims' Rights Act guarantees victims full and timely restitution as provided by law. The position extends that principle by applying the offender's own pre-existing accounts and assets, forfeited property, private-employer payments for prison work, and opt-in donations to repayment and keeping uninvolved taxpayers out of it. Most offenders have few assets (federal prosecutors classed 91% of \$110 billion in outstanding restitution as uncollectible in 2016), so private-employer payments for prison work and opt-in donations carry most of the load where assets fall short.

> [!info]- What is restitution?
> Restitution is payment ordered from an offender to a victim to cover losses caused by the crime, such as medical costs, lost income, and damaged or stolen property. It differs from a fine, which is ordinarily paid to the state.
