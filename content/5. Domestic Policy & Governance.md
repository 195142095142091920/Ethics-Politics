## **Core Principles** 

Limited government, individual responsibility, low taxation, and maximum cultural and economic freedom, with only minimal safety nets. The state prioritizes free markets, personal choice, and fiscal restraint. Opt-in social programs and basic public services are acceptable; mandatory redistribution is not, beyond the narrow exceptions named in Welfare and Entitlements. Minimal necessary taxes fund core services: K-12 education, police, fire, emergency services, infrastructure upkeep, and the bare-minimum safety floor defined in Welfare and Entitlements. K-12 is funded by each state from its general revenue at about \$10,000 per child, as set out in [[#Education|Education]].

## **Economic System** 

>[!tldr] In short
> **Markets work better than the alternatives for making people richer over time.**  
> Countries and periods that protected private property and let prices and competition guide most decisions produced higher living standards, less extreme poverty, and more innovation than systems that put the state in charge of most production and prices. The big historical examples (Soviet bloc vs. the West, North vs. South Korea, Venezuela’s collapse, China’s growth after it allowed more markets) all point in the same direction.
> 
> **Government still has a limited, important job.** 
> It needs to handle defense, basic law and order, courts, and core infrastructure. It also needs a few clear rules against force, fraud, and serious safety dangers. Beyond that, when government tries to heavily manage the economy or pick winners with subsidies, the results are usually wasteful and favor the politically connected.
> 
> **Corporate subsidies are mostly a bad deal.** 
> Taxpayers currently spend large amounts supporting specific companies. The track record shows these programs rarely deliver the promised broad benefits and often just reward lobbying.
> 
> **Minimum wage is more complicated.** 
> The research on job losses is genuinely mixed. The framework does not rest on it: a minimum wage dictates the terms of a voluntary contract, so it is abolished, and hardship is handled by the safety net instead.
> 
> **Owner income.** 
> Royalties and lease payments the nation earns as an owner of federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum are not paid out as a citizen dividend. Spread across every adult they would come to only tens of dollars a year. They go first to the [[Glossary#Earnings-only top-up|earnings-only top-up]] for low earners, and any remainder pays down the debt.
> 
> **Bottom line in plain English:** 
> Let markets do most of the heavy lifting because they have the best record at creating widespread prosperity. Keep government focused on the basics and on stopping clear harm. Avoid special favors for corporations. Be careful with wage mandates. Stay open to new ideas if technology changes the game, but don’t abandon the principles that produced the gains we already have.

#### Position 
Market-based systems grounded in secure property rights and price signals have produced the largest sustained gains in material living standards, poverty reduction, and innovation in recorded history. Comparative evidence favors these systems over socialist or heavily state-directed alternatives. Historical cases of sustained socialist organization produced chronic shortages, lower productivity, and eventual stagnation or collapse relative to more market-oriented peers. Hybrid systems that preserved significant private ownership and price signals performed better than those that suppressed them.

The state’s economic role should remain limited to core public goods - national defense, domestic public safety, courts, basic infrastructure, a bare-minimum safety net, and a basic K-12 funding floor of about \$10,000 per child paid by each state from its general revenue - plus a narrow set of evidence-based rules against force, fraud, and severe safety failures. Broad economic management, industrial planning, and taxpayer-funded corporate subsidies are rejected. There is no minimum wage; wages are set by voluntary agreement, and hardship is covered by the bare-minimum safety net ([[#Labor and Unions|Labor and Unions]]). Income the nation earns as an owner, such as royalties and lease payments on federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum, goes first to funding the earnings-only top-up ([[#Welfare and Entitlements]]), and any remainder goes automatically to reducing the national debt ([[#Fiscal Policy]]), with no annual legislative division. It is not paid out as a citizen dividend.

> [!question]- "Why is there no citizen dividend?"
> Owner income is too small to matter as a payment: spread across every adult, it comes to tens of dollars a year. Put first toward the earnings-only top-up, with any remainder going to debt reduction, the same money supports work and lowers the burden on future taxpayers. Because it is income the nation earns as an owner, using it this way takes nothing from any citizen through taxation.

> [!question]- "Don't markets leave some people behind?"
> Markets do not guarantee equal outcomes, and the framework does not claim they do. A minimal floor for basic necessities is funded by minimal necessary taxes, and anyone who wants to fund more can opt in to do so. What the framework rejects is mandatory redistribution beyond that floor and state direction of the economy, because the historical record shows those approaches produce shortages and stagnation that leave far more people behind. As markets spread after 1990, the share of the world living in extreme poverty (under \$3.00 a day) fell from 43.4% in 1990 to 10.1% in 2024, according to the World Bank. In 2023, the poorest 10% in the most economically free quarter of countries had incomes 7.8 times higher than the poorest 10% in the least free quarter. Extreme poverty affected 2% of people in the freest quarter compared with 52% in the least free. These Fraser Institute figures are cross-country correlations, not measured effects of economic freedom.

#### Key Elements
- Strong presumption in favor of open markets and private property.
- Comparative superiority of market systems over socialist and heavily state-directed alternatives.
- Government confined to public goods and a narrow regulatory core.
- Rejection of corporate subsidies and most forms of industrial policy.
- No minimum wage; wages set by voluntary agreement.
- No citizen dividend: owner income, such as royalties and lease payments on federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum, funds the earnings-only top-up first; any remainder goes automatically to debt reduction, with no annual legislative division.

#### Rationale 
Comparative historical evidence shows that societies with greater economic freedom and stronger market institutions have achieved higher long-run growth, faster poverty reduction, and greater consumer abundance than those that suppressed private property and price signals. Targeted safety and anti-fraud rules address real market failures, but regulatory scope expands easily and is prone to capture; therefore the burden of proof remains on further intervention. Corporate subsidies distort capital allocation and reward political connection more reliably than economic value.

Federal owner income is real but small. In FY2024 the Office of Natural Resources Revenue disbursed \$16.45 billion from energy and mineral production on federal and Tribal lands and federal offshore areas, of which \$6.32 billion went to the U.S. Treasury and \$1.18 billion to Tribes and individual Indian mineral owners, whose property it is. Excluding the Tribal share and spread across about 267 million adults, federal owner income comes to roughly \$24 to \$57 per adult per year: too small to matter as a payment, but useful toward the top-up and the debt. The top-up costs an estimated \$23 billion a year, far more than the \$6.32 billion the Treasury received from federal energy and mineral revenue in FY2024. So at current levels all owner income goes to the top-up, general revenue covers the rest, and debt reduction receives owner income only once it exceeds the top-up's cost.

On minimum wages the evidence is genuinely contested. Some well-known studies (notably Card & Krueger) find little or no employment loss from moderate increases. Other research and several meta-analyses find negative employment effects, particularly for teenagers and the least skilled, with the size of the effect varying by the magnitude of the increase and local labor-market conditions. The weight of evidence does not support large or rapid increases as costless. The framework abolishes the minimum wage on rights grounds, not on the employment evidence alone ([[#Labor and Unions|Labor and Unions]]).

## Socialism

>[!tldr] In short
> Socialism in its core sense, social or state ownership of the means of production and planning in place of markets, is incompatible with the framework because it overrides property and voluntary exchange. I believe socialism is antithetical to sentientism and negative rights, and a terrible idea: it takes property, forces redistribution, compels labor and speech, and suppresses dissent. Social democracy is different: the Nordic countries are market economies with large welfare states. The framework shares the goal of a floor for basic needs but funds only a minimal one and leaves the rest to markets and voluntary giving.

#### Position
The word "socialism" covers very different systems, and the framework judges each by what it does to individual rights. In its core sense, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy defines socialism as a society in which "the bulk of the means of production is under social, democratic control." Variants differ in who owns and how decisions are made: state socialism places ownership in the state and plans production centrally; democratic socialism seeks social ownership through elections; market socialism keeps markets but makes firms socially or worker owned. In practice the core form has meant state ownership and central planning ([[2. Political and Social Character#Communism in Practice|Communism in Practice]]). Social democracy keeps private ownership and markets and funds a large welfare state through taxes. The Nordic countries are in this category. Denmark's prime minister said in 2015 that "Denmark is far from a socialist planned economy. Denmark is a market economy."

Three features are incompatible with the framework. The first is collective or state ownership of productive property imposed by force, including expropriation. The second is central planning that replaces prices and voluntary exchange. The third is compulsory redistribution beyond a minimal floor for basic needs. Each takes property or overrides consent from people who have harmed no one. Social democracy shares only the third, so it is judged as too large a compulsory state, not as socialism in the core sense ([[11. Modern Political Parties#Nordic Social Democracy|Nordic Social Democracy]]).

I believe socialism is antithetical to my framework. The framework protects every sentient being's negative rights, the right not to have life, liberty, or property taken by force ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Political Philosophy|Political Philosophy]]). Socialism in its core sense violates those rights by design. It seizes property from people who have harmed no one. It forces redistribution beyond any minimal floor. Where the state is the only employer and owner, it compels labor and, by controlling the press and printing, compels speech. And because ownership of everything leaves no independent base for opposition, it suppresses dissent. I believe that makes it a terrible idea, not merely an inefficient one: the economic failures follow from the rights violations, and the worst outcomes in its history were the rights violations at scale. Soviet forced collectivization from 1929 was followed by famine in 1931 to 1934 that killed at least 5 million people, including at least 3.9 million Ukrainians in the Holodomor, according to Britannica. Demographic reconstructions put the toll of China's Great Leap Forward famine at 23 to 30 million excess deaths, which Vaclav Smil called "largely a manmade catastrophe." Both are covered in [[2. Political and Social Character#Communism in Practice|Communism in Practice]]. Democratic and market variants that stay voluntary and leave exit open do not carry those harms, and I judge them on what they compel, not on the name.

**Documented accounts** (individual reported events, not statistics):
- **Venezuela, 2016:** the ENCOVI living-conditions survey by three Venezuelan universities found 74.3% of the population lost an average of 8.7 kg (19 pounds) and about 9.6 million people ate two or fewer meals a day. CNBC (2017) – [Venezuela’s crisis causes its people to cut meals and lose weight](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/02/20/venezuelas-crisis-cause-its-people-to-cut-meals-and-lose-weight.html)

**My view:** I believe the right of citizens to simply be left alone is incomprehensible to socialists who are convinced they have a right to impose on and take from citizens whatever they want.

> [!question]- "Isn't socialism just about fairness and helping people who are struggling?"
> Many socialists are motivated by exactly that, and the framework shares part of the goal. It funds a bare-minimum safety floor for basic needs through minimal necessary taxes, and anyone may give more voluntarily ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Welfare and Entitlements|Welfare and Entitlements]]). The disagreement is about means. Taking ownership of productive property, or compelling redistribution beyond that floor, uses force against people who violated no one's rights. The record of planned economies also suggests it leaves the poor worse off, not better.

> [!question]- "The Nordic countries are successful and socialist. Doesn't that disprove the critique?"
> No, because they are not socialist in the core sense. Their own leaders describe them as market economies, and the Heritage Foundation's 2025 Index of Economic Freedom ranks Denmark 7th, Norway 9th, Sweden 12th, and Finland 13th in the world. What they add is high taxes and a large welfare state. The framework rejects that compulsory scale too, but it is a different argument from the one against planning and state ownership. Their success tends to support markets with private property, which they kept.

> [!question]- "Venezuela failed because of oil prices and U.S. sanctions, not socialism."
> Both factors played a real part, and the framework concedes it. Oil prices fell sharply in 2014, and ECLAC links part of the 2020 drop to tighter U.S. sanctions on the public sector. But the IMF found that "economic mismanagement accelerated and amplified the economic collapse," and price controls were producing shortages of staples in 2007, during the oil boom. The IMF calls the result the largest collapse for a country not at war in almost half a century. The policies made the economy fragile before the shocks arrived.

> [!question]- "Isn't Cuba's poverty caused by the U.S. embargo?"
> The embargo is a real factor, and the framework concedes it. It has blocked most U.S. trade and investment since the early 1960s. Its extraterritorial provisions, such as the 1996 Helms-Burton Act, also raise costs for foreign firms and banks that deal with Cuba. But the embargo does not stop Cuba from trading with the rest of the world. The European Union describes itself as Cuba's main trade partner and largest foreign investor, and in the 1990s Spain, Canada, and China were among its leading partners. U.S. law has also allowed agricultural exports to Cuba since 2000. Through 1990 the Soviet Union provided more than \$65 billion in aid and price subsidies, and the economy still shrank by roughly a third when that aid ended. Analysts of the 1990s crisis named the "inefficiencies of centrally planned economies" as its underlying cause. The embargo makes a weak economy worse; it does not explain why the economy is weak.

> [!question]- "Doesn't Cuba have excellent health care and literacy?"
> Cuba reports high literacy, long life expectancy, and low infant mortality, and its investment in schooling and doctors is real. These figures come from a government that controls statistics and the press, so researchers treat some of them with caution. A 2017 study in *Demography* found patterns in Cuba's records of fetal and newborn deaths consistent with misclassification. It estimated Cuba's infant mortality at about 9.0 to 10.1 per 1,000 live births, against the officially reported 5.8. Even taking the official figures at face value, the framework does not accept good services as justification for taking property and forbidding exit.

> [!question]- "Couldn't modern computers solve the planning problem?"
> The argument does not depend on computing power. Hayek's point was that the knowledge an economy runs on is "dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess," much of it about local conditions and changing preferences that are never written down. Mises argued that without market prices for capital goods, planners cannot compare the costs of different uses of resources. Prices gather that information because people trade with their own resources. A computer can process data faster, but it cannot produce the prices that come from voluntary exchange.

> [!question]- "Is market socialism, with worker cooperatives competing in markets, also rejected?"
> Not if it is voluntary. Workers may form cooperatives, own firms jointly, and compete in markets under the framework's freedom of association. What is rejected is making that model compulsory, by forbidding private ownership or seizing firms to convert them.

#### Key Elements
- Socialism in the core sense (social or state ownership of the means of production, central planning) distinguished from social democracy (markets plus a large welfare state).
- Incompatible: compulsory collective or state ownership, expropriation, central planning that overrides prices and voluntary exchange, and compulsory redistribution beyond a minimal floor.
- The economic calculation problem (Mises, Hayek) as the reason planning fails even with good intentions.
- Venezuela and Cuba as recent cases: expropriation, price controls, rationing, collapse in output, inflation, and mass emigration.
- Comparisons between similar countries that took different paths (the two Germanys, the two Koreas) and countries that moved toward markets (China, Vietnam, India), with confounders conceded.
- Shared ground: a bare-minimum safety floor, voluntary cooperatives, and voluntary giving.
- I judge socialism antithetical to sentientism and negative rights: it seizes property, forces redistribution, compels labor and speech, and suppresses dissent.
- Soviet collectivization and the Great Leap Forward as the largest famines tied to the system.

#### Rationale
The framework protects property and voluntary exchange as extensions of the right not to be subjected to force ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Political Philosophy|Political Philosophy]]). Socialism in the core sense removes both by design. It also faces a problem of knowledge that holds even under good intentions. Mises argued in 1920 that without market prices for the means of production, a planning authority cannot calculate which uses of resources are more or less valuable. Hayek added in 1945 that the relevant knowledge "never exists in concentrated or integrated form," and that prices are how a market economy uses it. The record of planned economies matches this reasoning ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Economic System|Economic System]]).

Venezuela is the most recent large case. Under Hugo Chávez the state took over the Orinoco oil projects of ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips in 2007. An international arbitration tribunal later found the Conoco takeover unlawful and awarded the company about \$8.7 billion. Price controls on about 400 basic products, imposed from 2003, were followed by recurring shortages of milk, meat, and sugar. The IMF estimates that real GDP contracted by more than 75% between 2013 and 2021, which it calls "the single largest economic collapse for a non-conflict country in almost half a century." Hyperinflation began in late 2017, and ECLAC reported annualized inflation still at 1,198% in November 2021. By late 2025, UNHCR and the R4V platform counted nearly 7.9 million Venezuelan refugees and migrants worldwide.

Cuba is the longest-running case in the hemisphere. In 1960 the revolutionary government nationalized the banks, the foreign oil refineries, and the property of U.S. nationals. The U.S. Foreign Claims Settlement Commission later found 5,911 of those claims compensable, worth about \$1.9 billion in principal. The state still owns most means of production and employs a majority of the workforce, according to the Congressional Research Service. Basic food has been rationed through the *libreta* ration book for decades. When Soviet aid ended, official statistics show GDP fell 34.8% from 1989 to 1993. Recent years have brought a second crisis. In 2021 the official consumer price index rose 77%, and economists estimate true inflation at 174% to 700%. In September 2024 the government cut the subsidized daily bread ration from 80 to 60 grams for lack of flour. In October 2024 the national grid collapsed, leaving the whole island without power. U.S. Customs and Border Protection recorded 224,607 encounters with Cuban nationals in fiscal 2022 and 200,287 in fiscal 2023. Cuba's own statistics office reports that the population fell to 9,748,007 at the end of 2024, 307,961 fewer than a year earlier, with net emigration of about 251,000 that year.

The framework concedes the overlap. It agrees with social democrats that no one should lack basic necessities, and it keeps a bare-minimum floor funded by minimal necessary taxes. It differs on scale and on compulsion. Help beyond that floor comes from markets, families, and voluntary giving, not from taking property.

#### Comparisons
The clearest tests are cases where similar people were divided between systems, or where one country changed systems. None is a controlled experiment, and each has confounders that the framework concedes. The Soviet Union and Mao's China are covered in [[2. Political and Social Character#Communism in Practice|Communism in Practice]].

- **East and West Germany.** The same nation, divided after 1945, ran a planned economy in the East and a market economy in the West. Before monetary union in 1990, IMF staff put East German labor productivity at 30% to 40% of the West German level. The German government's later account puts it at only about 20% to 25% in 1988. The two states had different occupying powers and foreign support, so not every gap is due to the economic system.
- **North and South Korea.** In 2023 the Bank of Korea estimated North Korea's gross national income per person at 1.59 million won, 3.4% of South Korea's 47.25 million won. The estimate is uncertain because North Korea publishes almost no data, and both states relied heavily on foreign patrons. South Korea also grew under military rule before it democratized, so the comparison is about markets, not about democracy.
- **China after 1978.** After China began market reforms, starting with returning farmland to individual households, GDP growth averaged over 9% a year. The World Bank credits China with lifting close to 800 million people out of extreme poverty, about three-quarters of the global reduction over that period. China remains a one-party state with a large state sector, so the gains came from introducing markets, not from changing the political system.
- **Vietnam after 1986.** Before the Đổi Mới reforms, inflation reached 487% in 1986. After the country moved to market-oriented policies, growth averaged about 8% a year in the 1990s. The poverty rate fell from 58% in 1993 to 37% in 1998, and GDP per person rose from under \$500 in 1986 to \$5,066 in 2025, according to the World Bank. As in China, the ruling party kept its monopoly on power.
- **India after 1991.** After a balance-of-payments crisis, India dismantled much of the "license raj" system of industrial licensing. Average tariffs fell from 80% in 1990 to 37% in 1996, and IMF research finds the reforms produced "decidedly more stable and sustainable growth from 1992 on." Rodrik and Subramanian note that India's growth had already accelerated around 1980, before the main liberalization, so 1991 deepened a change already under way rather than starting it.
- **Tanzania under Ujamaa.** In 1973 the ruling party made it compulsory for rural Tanzanians to live in planned villages, and scholarly estimates put the number moved at about five million. Many farmers were moved far from their fields and trees. Purchases by the cashew authority fell from 140,000 tons in 1973 to 44,000 tons in 1978–79. By 1985, IMF staff report, agricultural production had been declining since the 1970s and cash-crop exports had fallen by half since 1970. Falling world crop prices and oil shocks contributed. After liberalization began in 1986, growth averaged about 7% a year from 2000.

## Labor and Unions

>[!tldr] In short
> Unions are voluntary and no one has to join or pay one to work. Licensing exists only to prevent real harm to consumers. There is no minimum wage: pay is set by agreement, fraud and forced labor stay crimes, and the safety floor covers hardship.

#### Position
Workers may form and join unions, and employers and workers may bargain freely, under freedom of association. No one is compelled to join or pay a union as a condition of work. Occupational licensing must meet the same burden of proof as any regulation: it is justified only where it prevents real harm to consumers, not to limit competition. There is no minimum wage. A minimum wage dictates the terms of a voluntary contract between a worker and an employer, and it can price people whose labor is worth less than the floor out of work, though how many jobs are lost is disputed. Neither is consistent with [[Glossary#Negative rights|negative rights]]. Wages are set by agreement, including collective agreements reached by unions that workers join voluntarily. Low pay is supplemented by the earnings-only top-up and hardship is covered by the bare-minimum safety floor, both set out in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]], not by price controls on labor. Fraud, coercion, wage theft, and forced labor remain crimes. The abolition covers state minimum wages too: rights do not change at state lines, and the top-up uses one national zero-bracket line.

> [!question]- "Doesn't banning compelled dues weaken unions?"
> Unions remain free to organize and bargain. They keep members by delivering value, as any voluntary association does.

> [!question]- "Without a minimum wage, won't workers be exploited?"
> Exploitation through force, fraud, or coercion stays illegal: wage theft, deceptive contracts, and forced labor are rights violations and are prosecuted. What ends is the state setting the price of a voluntary agreement. Workers keep the right to refuse a job, to quit, and to join a union and bargain collectively. Several of the highest-wage countries in Europe, including Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Austria, and Italy, have no national minimum wage; wages there are set by collective agreements. Those agreements rest on very strong unions, which the framework allows only as voluntary associations, so the outcome in the United States would not simply copy theirs. People who still cannot earn enough are covered by the safety floor.

> [!question]- "Why abolish the minimum wage instead of keeping a small one?"
> A floor of any size is a rule that two consenting adults may not agree on certain terms, and it hits hardest those whose skills are worth the least. The federal minimum already binds very few people: in 2024, 1.0 percent of hourly paid workers earned \$7.25 or less. The safety floor, funded by minimal necessary taxes, handles hardship directly rather than through a price control that can price people out of work. How large that job loss is remains disputed in the research, and the abolition does not depend on its size.

> [!question]- "Without a minimum wage, how do low earners get by?"
> Through pay set by agreement plus the earnings-only top-up built into the flat tax's zero bracket. A low earner's pay is topped up through the ordinary tax return, and the top-up shrinks gradually as earnings rise, reaching zero at the zero-bracket line. So taking more hours or a raise always leaves the worker better off. The support comes through the tax system rather than from a rule that the employer must pay more, so no one is priced out of a job ([[#Welfare and Entitlements]]).

#### Key Elements
- Unions are voluntary associations.
- No compelled membership or dues.
- Licensing only where it prevents real consumer harm.
- No minimum wage; wages set by voluntary agreement, individual or collective.
- Abolition covers state minimum wages too; one national zero-bracket line.
- Fraud, coercion, wage theft, and forced labor remain crimes.
- Low pay supplemented by the earnings-only top-up; hardship covered by the bare-minimum safety floor.

#### Rationale
Union membership was 9.9% of U.S. wage and salary workers in 2024 (14.3 million), with 32.2% in the public sector and 5.9% in the private sector, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. In *Janus v. AFSCME* (2018) the Supreme Court held that compelling public employees to pay agency fees violates the First Amendment. Freedom of association is set out in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Freedom of Association|Authority & Civil Liberties]].

In 2024, 82,000 U.S. workers earned exactly the federal minimum of \$7.25 an hour and 760,000 earned less, together 1.0 percent of hourly paid workers, down from 13.4 percent in 1979. Most were young, and about two-thirds worked in leisure and hospitality, where tips may supplement wages. As of July 2026, Denmark, Italy, Austria, Finland, and Sweden had no national minimum wage, and in those countries, as in Iceland, Norway, and Switzerland, minimums are set by collective agreements for specific sectors. In 2022 the share of low-wage earners was 4.1 percent in Sweden, 6.5 percent in Finland, 8.8 percent in Italy, and 9.7 percent in Denmark, against 14.7 percent across the EU. Portugal, which has a statutory minimum wage, had the lowest share, at 1.8 percent, so the absence of a minimum wage does not by itself explain low-pay rates.

The research on employment effects is contested. Neumark and Shirley's review of U.S. studies found a clear preponderance of negative employment estimates, strongest for teens, young adults, and the less educated. Cengiz, Dube, Lindner, and Zipperer, studying 138 state minimum-wage increases, found that the overall number of low-wage jobs was essentially unchanged five years later. The framework does not rest the abolition on the employment evidence: it rests on the right of workers and employers to set their own terms.

## Antitrust

>[!tldr] In short
> Big is not illegal. Collusion, fraud, and coercion are prosecuted, and genuine bottlenecks over essential services can be regulated.

#### Position
Size alone does not justify intervention. Firms that grow by serving customers better have violated no one's rights. Intervention is justified against fraud, collusion to fix prices or divide markets, and coercive conduct, and against genuine monopoly bottlenecks where refusal would leave people without reasonable alternatives for essential services. Any intervention carries the burden of proof, as all regulation does.

> [!question]- "Won't large firms abuse their power without stronger antitrust?"
> Abuse through fraud, collusion, or coercion is prosecuted. A large firm without such conduct faces competition and new entrants; breaking it up for its size punishes success rather than wrongdoing.

#### Key Elements
- No intervention for size alone.
- Collusion, fraud, and coercion prosecuted.
- Narrow monopoly-bottleneck exception.
- Burden of proof on intervention.

#### Rationale
The Sherman Act prohibits contracts and conspiracies in restraint of trade and monopolization (15 U.S.C. §§ 1–2). The bottleneck exception follows [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Freedom of Association|Authority & Civil Liberties]].

## **Taxation** 

>[!tldr] In short
> The main federal tax is simple: most people below a clear income line pay nothing, and those with low earnings receive a top-up on what they earn. There is then a short, gradual ramp-up so that crossing the line does not create a sudden penalty. Above that ramp, everyone pays the same flat percentage. Almost all special loopholes are removed; the one exception is a flat, fully refundable \$1,500 credit per child. Capital income is taxed at a flat but lower rate so saving and investment are not punished.
> 
> Other taxes stay limited. A modest retail sales tax can help raise money without heavily punishing work; a hidden VAT is avoided. Corporate taxes stay low and simple. Payroll taxes shrink as retirement and health programs are reformed. Property taxes are mainly local and kept moderate to pay for local police, fire, basic schools, and basic services. Inheritance taxes are rejected. Special taxes on specific goods or imports are used only for narrow, clear reasons.
> 
> To raise U.S. employment, employer payroll taxes are cut across the board and business investment can be written off in full right away. These let businesses keep their own money instead of handing them taxpayer funds, and they are the two broad tools with measured job gains. No study shows that any incentive reduces outsourcing itself.
> 
> The total tax take is kept low so it only pays for essential jobs - defense, basic order, core infrastructure, a limited education floor, a small safety floor, and debt. Because this system collects less than today’s system, government spending must also be reduced over time. That is intentional. A temporary extra tax may be used during the changeover so the budget remains stable while spending is brought down.

#### Position 
The tax system across all levels of government should be low, simple, and limited to funding core public functions.

At the federal level the centerpiece is a flat income tax with a large zero bracket and a short, transparent phase-in. Income below \$42,000 for single filers and \$84,000 for joint filers (adjusted for inflation) faces a zero rate. A linear phase-in then applies over the next \$10,000 for single filers (\$42,000 to \$52,000) and \$20,000 for joint filers (\$84,000 to \$104,000), so that the rate rises smoothly to the full flat rate of 20%. Income above the phase-in range faces that single flat rate. Capital gains and dividends receive preferential but still flat treatment. Most deductions, credits, and special preferences are eliminated to keep the base broad and the rules simple.

The single exception is a flat, fully refundable child tax credit of \$1,500 per child under 18 per year. It is the same for every child at every income, and it is paid in full even when no income tax is owed.

**My view:** I believe that since restricting immigration will lower the population, we want as many things as possible to help parents and raise birth rates to replacement level ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]).

> [!question]- "How far below replacement is the U.S. birth rate?"
> The U.S. total fertility rate was about 1.6 births per woman in 2024, a record low, against a replacement level of about 2.1. Source – [CDC/NCHS: Births: Final Data for 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK623381/)

The other refundable feature is the earnings-only top-up set out in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]]: within the zero bracket, earned income draws a negative income tax that phases out to zero at the zero-bracket line.

> [!example]- Examples: federal income tax under the flat tax
> Single, \$60,000: \$2,600. Single parent, \$60,000, one child: \$1,100 after the \$1,500 child credit. Married, \$120,000, two children: \$2,200 after \$3,000 in child credits. Above the phase-in, tax is 20% of income minus the zero line minus half the phase-in width (\$5,000 single, \$10,000 joint).

Other taxes are kept secondary and constrained:

- A modest, broad-based retail sales tax may serve as a supporting or transitional revenue source. A European-style VAT is rejected in favor of the more transparent sales tax.
- Corporate tax should be low and simple, ideally structured so business income is taxed only once.
- Payroll taxes should decline as Social Security and Medicare are reformed toward more contribution-linked designs and a smaller residual safety net.
- Across-the-board employer payroll tax cuts and permanent full expensing of business investment (bonus depreciation) are the preferred tools for raising U.S. employment. Both let businesses keep their own money rather than receiving taxpayer funds.
- Property taxes remain primarily local, moderate, and tied to core local services (police, fire, roads, basic schools). They are retained but constrained.
- Estate and inheritance taxes are rejected.
- Excise taxes and tariffs are acceptable only when narrow and justified by clear externalities or national-interest policy.

Permanent progressive income-tax rate schedules with rising marginal rates are rejected; a temporary surtax with an explicit end date is the sole transitional exception. The overall tax burden should remain low and matched to essential functions only: national defense, public safety, basic infrastructure, [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Education|a limited education funding floor]], debt service, and a small austere safety net. Temporary transitional measures (a high-end surtax or modest sales tax with an explicit end date) may be used while spending is brought under control.

> [!question]- "Are payroll tax cuts and full expensing just corporate subsidies by another name?"
> No. A subsidy transfers taxpayer money to a business. A payroll tax cut or full expensing takes less of the business's own money and transfers nothing. Both are consistent with the rule that taxpayer money subsidizes no business unless taxpayers opt in, and both apply across the board rather than to chosen firms or groups.

> [!question]- "Do these tax cuts stop outsourcing?"
> No study measures whether any incentive reduces outsourcing itself; the evidence measures domestic employment and investment. Across-the-board payroll tax cuts and full expensing are the two broad tools with measured gains in U.S. jobs.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the flat tax mostly cut taxes for the rich?"
> In dollar terms, the largest cuts go to the highest earners: the top federal rate is 37% today, against a flat 20% here, and capital income keeps a preferential rate. Lower earners gain through the large zero bracket and the earnings-only top-up, and parents at every income get a flat \$1,500 refundable credit per child, so most low-income workers owe no income tax at all. The framework does not claim the change is neutral by income. It rests on principle: one rate for everyone above the zero bracket, with no penalty for earning more. The distribution across income groups has not yet been modeled.

#### Key Elements
- Flat federal income tax with a large zero bracket and a short linear phase-in.
- Flat, fully refundable \$1,500 child tax credit per child under 18, the single exception to eliminating credits.
- Earnings-only top-up (negative income tax) inside the zero bracket, phasing out at the zero-bracket line: 10 percent of earnings up to \$1,500, plus a per-child add-on for working parents, phased out on household income.
- Low, simple corporate tax; business income taxed once.
- Across-the-board employer payroll tax cuts.
- Permanent full expensing of business investment.
- Modest retail sales tax instead of a VAT; local, moderate property taxes; no estate tax.
- Tax burden matched to essential functions, alongside spending restraint.

#### Rationale 
High and complex taxes distort work, saving, investment, and compliance. U.S. federal revenue as a share of the economy has remained relatively stable for decades even when top statutory rates changed dramatically; very high rates do not reliably produce proportionally higher revenue. Federal revenue ranged from 14.2% to 18.5% of GDP during the 1950s, according to OMB, when the top rate was 91% or higher, and CBO puts the 50-year average at 17.3% of GDP. That top rate reached few people. The 91% bracket of 1950 began at \$200,000 of income, about \$2 million today, and the top 0.1 percent paid an average effective income tax rate of about 21%. So the 1950s show high statutory rates, not high rates actually paid. Flat-tax reforms in other countries have shown that simplification is workable and need not collapse collections. An IMF review of flat-tax adoptions found that personal income tax revenue rose in some countries, such as Latvia, Lithuania, and Russia, and fell in others, with total revenue effects that varied by country.

A large zero bracket increases take-home pay for lower- and middle-income households and supports private spending. A short linear phase-in removes the sharp notch that would otherwise encourage people to hold income just below the threshold. Lower marginal rates above the phase-in reduce disincentives to work and invest, though the measured gains from flat-tax adoptions abroad come mainly from simplicity and compliance, not faster growth. Reviews find no evidence that the rate cuts paid for themselves, and in Russia's 2001 reform the rise in income tax revenue came from better compliance rather than more work. A visible retail sales tax is preferable to a VAT because it is more transparent and less prone to quiet rate increases. Corporate and payroll taxes should not serve as primary redistribution tools. Property tax is retained at the local level because it is a practical and accountable way to fund local order and infrastructure; eliminating it would require less accountable replacements. Estate taxes confiscate property that has already been taxed.

No study measures whether any incentive reduces outsourcing itself. The evidence instead measures domestic employment and investment, and the two broad tools with measured gains in U.S. jobs are across-the-board payroll tax cuts and full expensing. In Norway, the abolition of lower regional payroll tax rates showed that each 1-percentage-point payroll tax increase cut local employment by 1.37% while wages fell only 0.32%. So lower payroll taxes support employment where wages do not fully adjust. A Swedish payroll tax cut for young workers raised their employment by 2–3 percentage points with no change in net wages. Bonus depreciation raised eligible investment by 10.4% (2001–04) and 16.9% (2008–10), and areas more exposed to it gained employment at roughly \$20,000–\$53,000 of forgone revenue per job, though earnings per worker did not rise. Targeted hiring credits, Opportunity Zones, domestic-content procurement rules, and firm-specific deals show weaker, short-lived, or far more costly results, often above \$110,000 per job.

This structure is sustainable only alongside spending restraint. It can support strong defense, core public goods, and a minimal safety floor. It cannot support the present long-term trajectory of major entitlement programs without reforming those programs. The tax rules and the commitment to a smaller federal government are designed to fit together.

> [!info]- Funding K-12 education
> K-12 is funded by each state from its general revenue at about \$10,000 per child per year, with a disability add-on each state sets from actual need, and the money follows the child to any public school in the state. There is no separate education tax. Private and religious schooling is paid by parents, and homeschooling families get a fully refundable \$1,000 federal credit per child ([[#K-12 Funding]]).

## Fiscal Policy

>[!tldr] In short
> The budget should balance, deficits should be kept to a minimum, and the national debt should be paid down and eventually eliminated. A ten-year glide path closes the deficit with defense protected. The committed cuts come first, new entrants move to reformed retirement and health programs, and a temporary surtax and sales tax bridge the gap. Surpluses then go automatically to the debt. The flat tax alone does not balance today's budget, so spending has to come down to fit it.

#### Position
A balanced budget is the proper target. Deficit spending is minimized. Long-term policy aims at reducing and eventually eliminating the national debt. Owner income from federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum goes first to the earnings-only top-up, and any remainder goes automatically to debt reduction ([[#Economic System]]). Defense is the top spending priority; reductions come from other areas.

The debt is paid down in order. First, a statutory glide path closes the deficit over about ten years, with defense protected. Second, the cuts the framework already commits to come first. The Department of Education is abolished, business subsidies and purely charitable foreign aid become opt-in, income support is reduced to the bare floor, and the EITC is replaced by the smaller earnings-only top-up. Third, Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid move new entrants onto the reformed, opt-in or privatized track at once, while grandfathered people keep Social Security and Medicare for life. Fourth, the temporary high-end surtax and modest sales tax set out in [[#Taxation]] bridge the gap while spending comes down, and end on their stated dates. Once the budget is in surplus, a debt brake sends every surplus automatically to paying down the debt, as owner income and Federal Reserve remittances already are. The aim is first to stop the debt growing faster than the economy, then to reduce it in dollars.

> [!question]- "Is eliminating the national debt realistic?"
> It is a long-term aim, not an immediate demand. Balanced budgets stop the debt from growing, and sustained restraint reduces it over time. The direction matters as much as the timeline.

> [!question]- "Does the flat tax raise enough to balance the budget?"
> Not with today's spending. A static estimate, with no behavioural or growth effects, puts the flat income tax at about 74 percent of today's individual income tax at the 20 percent rate (67 to 80 percent across rates of 18 to 22 percent). So total federal revenue would be roughly \$4.3 trillion to \$4.7 trillion against about \$7.4 trillion of spending. No rate in that range balances the budget, and even 30 percent would not. The flat tax is designed to fund a smaller government, so the budget balances by bringing spending down to it, not by raising the rate.

> [!question]- "Where do the savings have to come from?"
> Defense and interest together cost about \$2.1 trillion a year and are not cut directly. At the 22 percent rate, that leaves about \$2.6 trillion for everything else, which now costs over \$5 trillion. The cuts already committed to, such as abolishing the Department of Education, ending farm subsidies, and replacing the EITC, save roughly \$0.2 trillion. Most of the rest has to come from reforming Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid, which together cost about \$3.7 trillion a year. Because current recipients are protected, those savings build over time, and the temporary surtax and sales tax bridge the gap until they do.

#### Key Elements
- Balanced budget as the target.
- Minimal deficit spending.
- Long-term reduction and eventual elimination of the national debt.
- Statutory glide path to balance over about ten years, defense protected.
- Committed cuts first: Department of Education abolished, subsidies and charitable foreign aid opt-in, income support reduced to the floor, EITC replaced by the earnings-only top-up.
- New entrants move to reformed Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid at once; grandfathered people keep Social Security and Medicare for life.
- Temporary high-end surtax and modest sales tax as a bridge, each with an end date.
- Debt brake: surpluses, owner income left after the top-up, and Federal Reserve remittances go automatically to debt reduction.

#### Rationale
Persistent deficits shift costs to future taxpayers and raise interest payments that crowd out other spending. Federal debt and interest costs have grown substantially. CBO projects debt held by the public to rise from 101% of GDP in 2026 to 120% in 2036, above the 1946 record of 106%, and net interest to rise from \$1.04 trillion to \$2.14 trillion over the same period. Fiscal restraint is consistent with the framework's limits on government and its preference for low taxation. Federal debt passed 100% of the economy in 2013, when both debt and GDP were about \$16.7 trillion. The debt has been eliminated before, briefly, in 1835.

On October 14, 2026, total federal debt was \$40.33 trillion, of which \$32.48 trillion was held by the public. In the first eleven months of fiscal year 2026, the government collected \$4.85 trillion and spent \$6.81 trillion, including \$1.02 trillion of net interest, \$1.53 trillion for Social Security, \$0.98 trillion for Medicare, \$0.93 trillion for other health programs, and \$0.88 trillion for defense. A static model of the flat tax, applied to IRS returns for 2023 with a \$40,000 single and \$80,000 joint zero bracket and capital income taxed at an assumed 15 percent, puts the flat income tax at about 67 percent of today's individual income tax at 18 percent, 74 percent at 20 percent, and 80 percent at 22 percent. These are estimates, not official scores, and they ignore changes in behaviour and growth. They show that the framework's tax and spending plans must move together: the rate cannot carry the budget alone, and spending has to fall by more than a third from today's level.

## Monetary Policy

>[!tldr] In short
> Balanced budgets keep money stable. The goal is price stability, and inflation is treated as a hidden tax. The Fed is reformed, not abolished: one price-stability mandate under a rule, no deficit finance, lending only to solvent banks, no bailouts, a full audit, legal competing currencies, and any profits to debt reduction.

#### Position
Stable money follows from fiscal restraint. With balanced budgets and debt reduction, the government does not rely on the central bank to finance deficits, and monetary policy aims at price stability. Inflation acts as an unlegislated tax on savings and wages, so preventing it is part of keeping the tax burden visible and matched to essential functions. The Federal Reserve is reformed, not abolished. It keeps a single mandate, price stability under a published rule, and the employment mandate is dropped. It does not finance deficits, and it does not run asset purchases that work as deficit finance. As lender of last resort it lends only to solvent banks, against good collateral, at a penalty rate, and it does not bail out insolvent firms, whose losses would otherwise fall on taxpayers who never consented. It is fully audited in public. Competing private currencies remain legal. Any profits it remits to the Treasury go to debt reduction.

> [!question]- "Isn't some inflation needed to manage the economy?"
> Price stability is the aim. Balanced budgets remove the main pressure for inflationary finance, and stable prices let people plan, save, and invest.

> [!question]- "Why not abolish the Fed?"
> Before the Fed, money held its value over the long run but swung sharply, with frequent deflation and recurring bank panics in 1873, 1893, and 1907 that destroyed depositors' savings. The Fed's worst failure, the 1929 to 1933 collapse, came from its own discretion, as Ben Bernanke acknowledged in 2002. The lesson the framework draws is to limit discretion, not to remove the institution: a single price-stability rule, no deficit finance, and lending only to solvent banks. A return to gold is not favored, since countries that left the gold standard recovered from the Depression sooner.

#### Key Elements
- Price stability as the goal.
- No deficit financing through the central bank.
- Inflation treated as a hidden tax.
- Follows from balanced budgets.
- The Federal Reserve is reformed, not abolished.
- Single mandate: price stability under a published rule; the employment mandate is dropped.
- No deficit monetization and no asset purchases that function as deficit finance.
- Lender of last resort only to solvent banks, against good collateral, at a penalty rate; no bailouts of insolvent firms.
- Full public audit.
- Competing private currencies legal.
- Any Fed profits remitted to the Treasury go to debt reduction.

#### Rationale
Consumer prices rose 9.1% over the year ended June 2022, the largest 12-month increase since November 1981, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Balanced budgets are set out in [[#Fiscal Policy]]. By the Minneapolis Fed's consumer price index, the standard deviation of annual inflation was about 5.7 points in 1801 to 1912 and about 1.5 points in 1984 to 2025. But prices rose about 32-fold from 1913 to 2025, so a 1913 dollar buys about 3 percent of what it did. NBER business-cycle dates show average contractions of 21.6 months in 1854 to 1919 and 10.3 months in 1945 to 2020, though Christina Romer found that older data overstate pre-1914 volatility. The Federal Reserve Act sets three goals: maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates. The Fed remitted \$107.4 billion to the Treasury for 2021; since then its interest costs have exceeded its income, and its deferred asset stood at \$243.5 billion at the end of 2025.

## Public-Sector Size and Civil Service

>[!tldr] In short
> Government is sized to its essential functions, with defense first. Work private firms can do is contracted out, and agencies without an essential function are abolished. Career staff get severance or placement help; political appointees' roles end with the office.

#### Position
The size of government follows from its functions. Spending and staffing are matched to essential functions, with defense the top priority, and functions that private firms can perform are contracted out, as government research already is. Agencies with no essential function, such as the federal Department of Education, are abolished. When an agency is abolished, career staff who built their lives around these jobs deserve severance or placement help: that is the commitment side, honoring people who followed the rules. Political appointees are different; their roles end with the office, and there is no transition obligation to them.

> [!question]- "Doesn't contracting out reduce accountability?"
> Contracts set measurable deliverables and can be ended. A contractor that fails loses the work, which is a stronger check than the near-permanence of a public office.

#### Key Elements
- Staffing matched to essential functions.
- Defense the top priority.
- Contracting out where private firms can perform.
- Non-essential agencies abolished.
- Career staff at abolished agencies get severance or placement help; political appointees do not.

#### Rationale
In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. The federal civilian workforce was about 2.3 million on September 30, 2024, according to the Office of Personnel Management. Government R&D through private contracts is set out in [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Intellectual Property and Innovation|Bioethics, Progress, & Technology]].

### Civil Service Protections
*Moved from [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties]].*


>[!tldr] In short
> Hiring is by merit. Policy-setting jobs answer to elected officials. Career staff cannot be fired for politics, but removal for poor performance or misconduct is fast: notice, a 30-day decision, and one appeal decided within 90 days.

#### Position
Federal hiring is by merit, through open competition and tested qualifications, never by party or loyalty. Positions that set or direct policy serve at the pleasure of elected officials, so voters can hold the people they elect responsible for what the government does. Career staff in non-policy roles keep one firm protection: no one is hired, fired, or punished for party, political speech, or refusing an unlawful order. Removal for poor performance or misconduct is fast and fair: written notice of the specific reasons, a chance to answer, a decision within 30 days, and one appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board decided within 90 days. The probationary period is two years, with an affirmative decision required before it ends.

> [!question]- "Won't weakening protections bring back the spoils system?"
> That is the strongest objection. Protections were built after the spoils era because jobs handed out for loyalty produced corruption and incompetence, and a workforce that can be fired at will can be pressured to serve a party rather than the law. The framework keeps the part of the old settlement that answers this: merit hiring, and a ban on firing or punishing anyone for party, political speech, or refusing an unlawful order, enforceable in court. What it removes is delay. Reclassifying policy roles is legitimate only for positions that genuinely set policy; using it to reach ordinary career staff would be the spoils system by another name.

> [!question]- "Is it really that hard to fire a federal employee?"
> It is slow. The Government Accountability Office found that removing a poor-performing permanent employee can take six months to a year or longer, and that an appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board averaged 243 days in 2013. Agencies dismissed about 3,500 employees for performance or conduct that year, most during the probationary period, when appeal rights are limited. The delay discourages managers from acting at all.

#### Key Elements
- Merit hiring by open competition; no party or loyalty tests.
- Policy-setting positions serve at the pleasure of elected officials.
- No one is hired, fired, or punished for party, political speech, or refusing an unlawful order.
- Removal for performance or misconduct: written reasons, a chance to answer, decision within 30 days, one appeal decided within 90 days.
- Two-year probationary period with an affirmative decision before it ends.

#### Rationale
Accountability runs through elected officials, so those who set policy answer to them ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Public-Sector Size and Civil Service|Public-Sector Size and Civil Service]]). Due process still applies to every removal ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Due Process and Rule of Law]]), but process that takes a year protects poor performance more than it protects fairness. Keeping a hard line against political firing protects both the employee and the public from a workforce bent to party ends.

## Welfare and Entitlements

>[!tldr] In short
> People already on benefits who followed the rules keep their entitlement through a phase-out period; the funding behind it may change. Grandfathered Social Security and Medicare recipients keep theirs for life. Welfare and entitlements are cut back to a bare-minimum safety net for basic needs. Nobody is forced to fund redistribution beyond that floor, and other social programs run on voluntary contributions. Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid are privatized or restructured where possible. Low earners get a top-up of 10 percent of what they earn, up to \$1,500, plus up to \$1,000 per child for working parents, through the tax return; it fades out on household income with no cliff. People who cannot work are covered first by default private disability insurance, then by charity and mutual aid, and as a last resort by a strictly means-tested allowance for severe disability and the elderly poor. The allowance pays \$575 a month (single) or \$900 (couple) plus local rent, reduced by other income, with health coverage and, where medically needed, long-term care. Shelters, hospitals, and charities refer people in need, a clinician assesses them within days, and they are placed in the least costly safe setting.

#### Position
People who followed the rules and are on benefits should not be punished when the system changes to something better, so there is a phase-out period that honors existing commitments. The obligation is to the person, not to the old funding stream: a person's status, meaning their entitlement, is honored through the transition, while the funding sources behind those benefits may change.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
>- United States, 1 Oct 2026: under the 2025 reconciliation law, refugees, asylees and many other lawfully present immigrants without green cards became ineligible for federally funded Medicaid and CHIP (emergency Medicaid remains), with ACA subsidy limits following on 1 Jan 2027. KFF – [Refugees and Asylees: Recent Changes in Access to Health Coverage and Other Assistance](https://www.kff.org/immigrant-health/refugees-and-asylees-recent-changes-in-access-to-health-coverage-and-other-assistance/)

Existing welfare and entitlement programs are aggressively reduced to bare-minimum safety nets. Mandatory redistribution beyond a bare-minimum floor is rejected; that floor is funded by minimal necessary taxes as set out in Core Principles, and an opt-in funding model for remaining social programs is the preferred long-term structure. Benefits, when provided, cover only basic necessities and do not support comfort or lifestyle expansion. Privatization and structural reform of Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, and similar programs are pursued where feasible.

The bare-minimum floor contains two income parts. Each is a narrow, named exception to the rule against compelled redistribution, alongside emergency shelter, emergency medical care, and the K-12 backstop.

**Earnings-only top-up.** Low earners receive a top-up built into the flat tax's zero bracket: a negative income tax paid only on earned income, never for not working. It phases out gradually and reaches zero at the zero-bracket line, so there is no cliff where earning one more dollar costs a benefit, and it is administered through the ordinary tax return rather than a separate program. Each worker receives 10 percent of what they earn, up to a peak of \$15,000 in earnings, for a top-up of at most \$1,500. Working parents also receive a per-child add-on of up to \$1,000 per child for up to three children, paid only on earnings: it grows with household earnings and reaches its full amount at \$15,000. The add-on is the same for married and single parents. The top-up is paid to each worker but phased out on household income, so splitting or combining a household brings no gain. The whole household amount falls in a straight line from \$15,000 of household income to zero at the \$40,000 zero-bracket line; for married couples the range runs from \$30,000 to the joint zero-bracket line of \$80,000. A single parent of two earning \$18,000 receives about \$3,080, a married one-earner couple with two children earning \$30,000 receives \$3,500, and a teenager working in a household with \$120,000 of income receives nothing. It is funded first by owner income ([[#Economic System]]), with general revenue covering any shortfall. It replaces the minimum wage as the way low pay is supplemented, and it supplements work rather than replacing it.

**People who cannot work.** Support comes in layers, in order: (1) private disability insurance, with employers enrolling workers by default and workers free to opt out. The self-employed and people between jobs get the same opt-out choice through their tax return, and the state does not run the insurance. This sits alongside the grandfathered Social Security opt-in set out in [[#Social Security and Medicare Transition]]; (2) people who can work some are covered by the top-up, which rewards every dollar earned; (3) charity and mutual aid, including respite for family caregivers; and (4) as a last resort, a strictly means-tested allowance for severe disability and for the elderly poor, paid from general revenue under the same backstop logic as grandfathered Social Security, with fraud enforcement and periodic medical reviews. The [[Glossary#Last-resort allowance|last-resort allowance]] is a separate named exception, not an opt-in program, so the rule that opt-in programs never fall back on general taxation does not apply to it.

**The last-resort allowance.** The allowance pays \$575 a month for a single person and \$900 for a couple for non-housing needs (food, phone, transport, and personal items), plus the local HUD fair market rent for a studio (single) or a one-bedroom (couple). It is reduced when housing is shared. In a typical area this comes to about \$1,300 to \$1,500 a month for a single person, an estimate. The non-housing part rises with inflation each year, and the housing part follows HUD's annual rent update, so the amount follows real local costs rather than a single national figure. Other income, including grandfathered Social Security and private disability insurance, reduces it dollar for dollar, except earnings. The first \$100 a month of earnings is ignored, and each further dollar earned reduces the allowance by 50 cents, so that any work still pays. The asset test exempts the home and one car, plus savings of up to \$10,000 for a single person or \$15,000 for a couple, so saving is not punished. In-kind help from charity, family, or mutual aid does not reduce the allowance. Recipients are enrolled automatically in the opt-in public health plan, paid under the same last-resort exception; for grandfathered Medicare members, the allowance pays the Part B premium instead. Long-term care is provided in kind, only with medical certification, with home care preferred where it costs less than an institution, and family members who provide care may be paid at a capped rate. Severely disabled children, in severe cases only, receive a narrow allowance of up to \$575 a month for the extra costs their disability causes. Parents remain responsible for the ordinary costs of raising them, so the allowance supports that responsibility rather than replacing it. Non-citizens are ineligible for the last-resort allowance ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Removal of Non-Citizen Residents and Loss of Citizenship|Removal of Non-Citizen Residents]]).

**Getting help.** Shelters, hospitals, police, and charities refer people to the allowance office, and anyone can apply at any shelter. No new outreach agency is created; charities and mutual aid do the street outreach. Within a few days, a licensed, neutral clinician from the same office that carries out the allowance's medical reviews assesses the person, and certification qualifies the placement under the allowance. The person is placed in the least costly setting that safely meets their needs, such as a studio with home care, assisted living, or a nursing home, run by private or charity providers rather than the state. Until then, they stay in emergency shelter, or in hospital rather than being discharged to the street. If they have no family and cannot manage their own affairs, a court appoints a guardian or advocate, preferably a volunteer or a charity.

> [!question]- "Would cutting entitlements abandon people who paid into the system?"
> No. People who followed the rules and are on benefits are not punished when the system changes; there is a phase-out period that honors existing commitments. The person's entitlement is honored through the transition, even as the funding behind it shifts. Privatization and restructuring are pursued where feasible, and a bare-minimum safety net covering basic necessities remains. What is rejected is mandatory redistribution beyond that floor.

> [!question]- "What happens if opt-in funding falls short?"
> The program scales to what its contributors fund; it does not fall back on general taxation. The bare-minimum safety floor for basic necessities is funded separately by minimal necessary taxes, so a shortfall in an opt-in program never removes that floor.

> [!question]- "Isn't the top-up redistribution?"
> Yes, and the framework says so. It is funded partly from owner income and otherwise from general revenue, so it is a narrow, named exception to the rule against compelled redistribution, like emergency shelter, emergency medical care, and the K-12 backstop. It is limited in three ways: it is paid only on earned income, it ends at the zero-bracket line, and it replaces the minimum wage and separate wage-subsidy credits rather than adding to them. Redistribution beyond the named exceptions remains rejected.

> [!question]- "Why does the top-up include children?"
> A worker supporting children has more to cover on the same pay, and the strongest evidence for earnings top-ups comes from working parents. The add-on is modest and paid only on earnings, so it rewards work rather than having children. It goes to all working parents, married or single, with no marriage penalty, and it stops at three children.

> [!question]- "Why per worker but phased out by household?"
> Paying each worker keeps the top-up tied to that person's own work. Phasing it out on household income keeps it for households that are actually low-income: a teenager or second earner in a well-off household gets nothing. Because the married range is double the single range, a couple neither gains nor loses by marrying, splitting, or combining households.

> [!question]- "What about people who can't work?"
> They are covered in layers. Employers enroll workers by default in private disability insurance, and workers may opt out; the self-employed and people between jobs get the same choice through their tax return. The insurance is private; the state does not run it. Those within the grandfathered Social Security system keep that protection. People who can work some draw the earnings-only top-up on what they earn. Charity and mutual aid, including respite for family caregivers, come next. As a last resort, people with severe disabilities and the elderly poor receive a strictly means-tested allowance from general revenue, under the same backstop logic as grandfathered Social Security. It is checked by fraud enforcement and periodic medical reviews, and it is a narrow exception, not a general welfare entitlement.

> [!question]- "How much does the last-resort allowance cost?"
> In 2025, SSI paid \$64.85 billion to about 7.4 million people. Restricted to severe disability and the elderly poor, the allowance's cash cost is estimated to stay close to that, because the higher standard is offset by the narrower eligibility. Health coverage for recipients is the larger cost, but it replaces Medicaid spending for the same people rather than adding to it. These are estimates, not official scores.

> [!question]- "Won't a higher allowance invite fraud?"
> It is checked by income and asset verification, fraud enforcement, and periodic medical reviews. Only severe disability qualifies, and fraud is punished as theft. Income from any source reduces the allowance, so there is nothing to gain from hiding behind it while working.

> [!question]- "Why not one flat national amount?"
> Rent is the largest cost and varies more than anything else. A studio in Los Angeles has a fair market rent of \$2,079 a month, far above what one flat amount could cover without overpaying everywhere else. Tying the housing part to local rent pays what basic housing actually costs where the person lives, no more.

> [!question]- "Why does the allowance include health coverage?"
> Severe disability and frailty mean ongoing care, not just emergencies. Emergency-only care would leave the people least able to provide for themselves without treatment for chronic conditions, which is often more expensive when it ends in the emergency room. Covering them inside the same narrow exception keeps everyone else's healthcare opt-in.

> [!question]- "Why are severely disabled children covered when parents are responsible for their children?"
> Parents remain responsible for the ordinary costs of raising a child. A severe disability can add costs far beyond what any ordinary family budget can carry. The child allowance, up to \$575 a month, covers only those extra costs, in severe cases, so that parents can keep caring for the child at home.

> [!question]- "What happens to an elderly person with no home and no family who needs care?"
> A shelter, hospital, police officer, or charity refers them to the allowance office, or they apply at any shelter. Within a few days a licensed, neutral clinician assesses them, and once certified they are placed in the least costly setting that safely meets their needs, such as a studio with home care, assisted living, or a nursing home, run by private or charity providers. Until then they stay in shelter, or in hospital rather than being discharged to the street. If they cannot manage their own affairs, a court appoints a guardian or advocate, preferably a volunteer or a charity.

#### Key Elements
- People on benefits who followed the rules are not punished; a phase-out period honors existing commitments.
- Status (the person's entitlement) is honored through the transition; funding sources may change.
- Aggressive reduction of welfare and entitlements to bare-minimum safety nets.
- No mandatory redistribution beyond a bare-minimum floor.
- Opt-in funding for remaining social programs.
- Benefits limited to basic necessities.
- Earnings-only top-up in the flat tax's zero bracket: paid only on earned income, phasing out at the zero-bracket line with no cliff, administered through the tax return; a narrow exception to no compelled redistribution.
- Top-up parameters: 10 percent of earnings up to a \$15,000 peak (at most \$1,500 per worker); a per-child add-on of up to \$1,000 per child for up to three children for all working parents, paid only on earnings; paid per worker, phased out on household income from \$15,000 to \$40,000 (married: \$30,000 to \$80,000).
- For people who cannot work: private disability insurance, with default enrollment by employers (or through the tax return for the self-employed and those between jobs) and the right to opt out, not run by the state; the grandfathered Social Security opt-in; the top-up for partial work; charity and mutual aid, including caregiver respite.
- Last resort: a strictly means-tested allowance for severe disability and the elderly poor, from general revenue under the backstop logic, with fraud enforcement and periodic medical reviews; a narrow exception.
- Allowance amount: \$575 a month (single) or \$900 (couple) for non-housing needs, plus local HUD fair market rent (studio or one-bedroom), reduced when housing is shared; non-housing part indexed to inflation, housing to HUD's annual update.
- Other income reduces the allowance dollar for dollar; the first \$100 a month of earnings is ignored and each further dollar earned reduces it by 50 cents; in-kind charity does not reduce it.
- Asset test: home and one car exempt, plus \$10,000 in savings (single) or \$15,000 (couple).
- Recipients auto-enrolled in the opt-in public health plan under the same exception; Part B premium paid for grandfathered Medicare members.
- Long-term care in kind with medical certification; home care preferred where cheaper; capped pay for family caregivers.
- A narrow allowance of up to \$575 a month for severely disabled children, for disability-related costs only, in severe cases only.
- Intake: referral by shelters, hospitals, police, or charities, or application at any shelter; assessment within days by a licensed, neutral clinician; placement in the least costly safe setting with private or charity providers; shelter or hospital until placement; a court-appointed guardian or advocate, preferably a volunteer or charity, for those with no family who cannot manage their affairs.
- Privatization and structural reform of Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, and similar programs where feasible.

#### Rationale
In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. Mandatory redistribution takes from some citizens to fund others, which the framework treats as coercion beyond the state's core role. Major entitlement programs also face long-term funding shortfalls under current law, so structural reform is needed regardless. The 2025 Trustees Reports project Social Security's combined trust funds to be depleted in 2034, with 81% of scheduled benefits then payable, and Medicare's Hospital Insurance trust fund in 2033. An opt-in model lets those who wish to fund social programs above the floor do so, while the minimal floor for basic necessities stays funded by minimal necessary taxes. Voluntary giving alone could not yet carry the floor: SNAP benefits in FY2024 (\$92.9 billion) exceeded all U.S. charitable giving to human services in 2024 (\$91.15 billion).

An earnings-only top-up is the tool with the strongest evidence for low earners. Hoynes and Patel found that a \$1,000 increase in the Earned Income Tax Credit raised single mothers' employment by 7.3 percentage points and cut the share of their families below the poverty line by 9.4 points. Paying it only on earned income matters: in the Seattle-Denver income maintenance experiment, which guaranteed income whether or not people worked, husbands cut their hours by about 9 percent and wives by about 20 percent. Delivering the top-up through the flat tax replaces the EITC's separate rules, which produce large errors. The Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration reported that 27.3 percent of EITC payments in FY2024 (\$15.9 billion of \$58.4 billion) were improper. One return with one phase-out leaves fewer rules to get wrong, though improper payments will not vanish and must still be policed. The top-up is far smaller than the EITC it replaces: the EITC paid about \$69.6 billion to about 24 million claimants for tax year 2024, with a 2026 maximum of \$7,316 for two children. An estimate modelled on the Census Bureau's 2025 Current Population Survey puts the top-up at roughly \$23 billion a year, about \$16 billion for the base and \$7 billion for the child add-on. This is an estimate, not an official score. The phase-out takes at most 18 cents of each extra dollar, for a single parent of three, against 21.06 cents under the EITC for families with two or more children. The \$1,000 add-on sits below the \$1,700 refundable portion of the 2026 child tax credit (maximum \$2,200 per child), which the flat tax also replaces. An amount above about \$1,255 per child would push a single parent of three past the EITC's 21.06 percent.

In August 2026, 7.3 million people received Supplemental Security Income, averaging \$738.73 a month, and 7.0 million disabled workers received earned Social Security Disability Insurance benefits averaging \$1,635.90. The need is real: in 2023, 24.2 percent of working-age adults with disabilities living in the community were in poverty, against 9.9 percent of those without. Means-tested programs are also error-prone: SSI's improper-payment rate rose from 9.41 percent in FY2019 to 10.62 percent (about \$6.5 billion) in FY2023, which is why the last-resort allowance carries fraud enforcement and periodic medical review. Work-focused design shrinks the group who cannot work at all. After the Netherlands required employers to attempt rehabilitation before a disability claim (2002) and replaced its disability law with one built around remaining work capacity (2006), new disability claims fell by about 25 and 21 percentage points respectively, and the caseload by more than 60 percent. Voluntary institutions once carried much of this load: by David Beito's conservative estimate, one in three adult American men belonged to a fraternal society in 1920.

The current federal SSI maximum is \$994 a month for an individual and \$1,491 for a couple in 2026. In December 2025, the average federal SSI payment was \$688.47, and 7.39 million people received payments, 1.20 million aged and 6.13 million disabled, of whom 1.02 million were children. That is well below what basic needs cost. The 2026 poverty guideline for one person is \$15,960 a year, about \$1,330 a month. The USDA's Thrifty Food Plan alone costs \$236.30 to \$281.20 a month for an adult aged 51 to 70. HUD's fair market rent for a Los Angeles studio is \$2,079. Health and care needs are larger still. Medicaid spent \$27,361 per disabled enrollee and \$20,305 per aged enrollee in FY2023. A semi-private nursing home room costs a national median of \$9,581 a month, against \$6,673 for a home caregiver 44 hours a week, which is why home care is preferred where it costs less. The Medicare Part B premium is \$202.90 a month in 2026.

> [!info]- What is an opt-in funding model?

## Child Support

>[!tldr] In short
> Both genetic parents who consented to the sex owe support. Civil orders come first; willful refusal after an order brings escalating penalties. A rape victim owes no duty from the rape; the rapist owes full support.

#### Position
Voluntary conception creates a support duty on both genetic parents who consented to the sex. Support is enforced by civil order first; willful refusal after an order is a rights violation, a fraud on the child's claim, with escalating penalties; any fines are [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Income-Scaled Fines|scaled to income and wealth]]. In cases of rape, the victim parent owes no duty created by the rape, and the rapist parent owes full support.

> [!question]- "Does a parent who was raped owe support?"
> No. Duties arise from voluntary responsibility, and rape is not voluntary for the victim. The rapist parent owes full support.

#### Key Elements
- Support duty on both genetic parents who consented to the sex.
- Civil order first.
- Willful refusal after an order is a rights violation with escalating penalties.
- Victim parent in a rape owes no duty created by the rape.
- Rapist parent owes full support.

#### Rationale
Duties arise from voluntary responsibility, matching the voluntary-procreation responsibility in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Axioms|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]. A parent who consented to the sex took on the child's claim; refusing an order to meet it defrauds the child. Rape is not voluntary for the victim, so no duty arises for the victim from it. The U.S. Census Bureau reports that in 2022 about one in four children under 21 lived with only one parent while the other parent lived outside the household.

## Social Security and Medicare Transition

>[!tldr] In short
> Social Security and Medicare become voluntary: nobody is forced to pay in. Current retirees, and people within ten years of full retirement age, are protected before the new system comes in, as an honored commitment, and keep their benefits for life. The old system fades out only as fewer people remain in it. The separate basic safety floor stays. Employers sign workers up for private retirement savings by default, and anyone can opt out.

#### Position
Social Security and Medicare become opt-in only. Participation and contributions are voluntary; no one is compelled to pay into either program or to rely on it, and those who opt out arrange their own retirement savings and coverage. Current retirees are protected before the new system comes in: their benefits are an existing commitment that is not a rights violation, and existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition to the new system. People within ten years of full retirement age get the same protection as current retirees, grandfathered on current benefit formulas, as part of the same honored commitment. Grandfathered people keep Social Security and Medicare for life. The old system phases out because fewer people remain in it, not because benefits end on a set date. This protection is part of the position, not a practical concession. The separate bare-minimum safety floor for basic necessities, defined in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]], is unaffected.

Employers enroll workers by default in a private retirement savings account, and workers may opt out. The self-employed make the same choice on their tax return. The state does not run or hold the accounts. The default contribution rate is not set by the framework. One rule ties this to the disability-insurance default in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]]: defaults apply only where a person mainly protects themselves, as with disability insurance and retirement savings; anything that funds other people stays opt-in.

> [!note]- Realistic transition
> In a realistic world, many systems — like taxes, like education, like all the systems that would be changing — would have some gradual period of change for most of these changes to happen. That practical lag is a brief aside, not the main position.

> [!question]- "Doesn't moving to opt-in break the promise made to people who paid in?"
> Current retirees are protected before the new system comes in, because existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition; people who planned around the current system are not cut off, and the basic-necessities floor remains. Grandfathered people keep their benefits for life; the old system shrinks only as fewer people remain in it. The programs are also not financed for the long term under current law, so continuing them unchanged would itself break the promise of scheduled benefits.

> [!question]- "Who pays for the protected benefits, and what about people who paid in but are more than ten years from retirement?"
> Protected benefits are funded by opt-in contributions plus a general-revenue backstop. This keeps the insurance framing, where people are repaid on what they paid in, and the backstop covers anyone who could not contribute enough, without compelled redistribution. For grandfathered Medicare, opt-in contributions pay first and general revenue covers any shortfall for grandfathered people only, the same backstop as grandfathered Social Security. People more than ten years from full retirement age keep a credit for the contributions they already paid, payable at retirement.

> [!question]- "Won't moving to opt-in push the elderly into poverty?"
> It could if the transition is handled badly, and the scale is large. Social Security is the country's largest antipoverty program: in 2024 it moved 28.7 million people above the Census Bureau's Supplemental Poverty Measure line, 20.1 million of them aged 65 or older. Benefit payments that year were about \$1.47 trillion (\$1,316.4 billion retirement and survivors, \$155.0 billion disability), and the program costs about 5.3% of GDP in 2025. That is why the transition is built around the people at risk. Current retirees and everyone within ten years of full retirement age keep their benefits for life, so no one now old or nearly old loses income. Younger workers keep a credit for what they have already paid, and default enrollment in private accounts builds their own savings. The last-resort allowance remains for the elderly poor who still fall short. The honest cost is that the protected benefits run for decades. The backstop must cover benefits on the order of today's payments at first, shrinking only as the grandfathered group shrinks, and the program is already short, with the combined trust funds projected to be depleted in 2034.

> [!question]- "Isn't default enrollment a form of coercion?"
> No. Nobody is forced to save: opting out is free and simple, and the money stays in the worker's own private account. The default only changes what happens when a person does nothing. The evidence shows how much that matters. In Madrian and Shea's study of one large U.S. employer, 401(k) participation among new hires rose from 37% under opt-in to 86% under automatic enrollment. In Great Britain, where employers have enrolled workers automatically since 2012, 89% of eligible employees were saving into a workplace pension in 2024. More people with their own savings means fewer who end up relying on the last-resort allowance in old age.

#### Key Elements
- Social Security and Medicare are opt-in only.
- No compelled contributions; participation is voluntary.
- Current retirees are protected before the new system comes in, as an honored existing commitment.
- People within ten years of full retirement age get the same protection, grandfathered on current benefit formulas.
- Protected benefits are funded by opt-in contributions plus a general-revenue backstop.
- Grandfathered people keep Social Security and Medicare for life; the old system phases out as fewer people remain in it, not on a set date.
- Grandfathered Medicare: opt-in contributions first, general revenue covers any shortfall for grandfathered people only, the same backstop as grandfathered Social Security.
- People more than ten years from full retirement age keep a credit for contributions already paid, payable at retirement.
- The bare-minimum safety floor is unaffected.
- Default enrollment by employers in a private retirement savings account, with a free opt-out; the self-employed choose on the tax return. Every adult is also enrolled by default in a low-cost private index account, with a free opt-out and no taxpayer seed ([[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#AI and Automation|AI and Automation]]). The state does not run or hold the accounts.
- Defaults only where a person mainly protects themselves (disability insurance, retirement savings); anything that funds others stays opt-in.

#### Rationale
Compelled contributions to retirement and health programs are redistribution beyond the floor, which the framework rejects. Existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition, which is why current retirees are protected before the new system comes in. The protection extends to people within ten years of full retirement age because the transition risk lands hardest on them. They have planned and paid in around the current system and have the least time left to build their own retirement savings and coverage. The programs also face shortfalls under current law. The 2025 Trustees Reports project Social Security's combined trust funds to be depleted in 2034, with 81% of scheduled benefits payable after that, and Medicare's Hospital Insurance trust fund in 2033. CBO projects Medicare spending to rise from 2.6% of GDP in 2025 to 5.2% in 2055. Structural reform is needed regardless; the framework sets its direction toward voluntary participation. Default enrollment fills the gap that opt-in leaves, because inertia is strong: Madrian and Shea found that most workers hired under automatic enrollment also kept the default contribution rate and fund. That same finding is why the framework leaves the default rate unset rather than picking a number the evidence does not single out; the rate will shape how much people save.

## Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness

>[!tldr] In short
> Housing is left mainly to property owners and the market. Basic zoning is allowed, but rules that wipe out property value must be paid for, and housing subsidies need voluntary funding. The safety floor offers emergency shelter only, not housing, and charities and private groups are free to run Housing First; camping and drug use in public are banned.

#### Position
Housing supply is governed by property rights and the market. Ordinary zoning that coordinates land use is allowed, but regulation that eliminates the economically beneficial use of property, or destroys most of its value, is a taking and requires compensation. Population growth is met through densification, development, and voluntary exchange, not political allocation of land. Housing subsidies require taxpayer opt-in. For homelessness, the safety floor provides emergency shelters only; it is not a housing program. The one exception is the rent component of the last-resort allowance for severe disability and the elderly poor ([[#Welfare and Entitlements]]). Charities and private groups are free to run Housing First programs, and opt-in funding can support them; the public rent component stays limited to the last-resort allowance. Camping on public property is not permitted, and public drug use is prohibited.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **Record homelessness, 2024:** HUD reported more than 770,000 people homeless on a single night in January 2024, an 18% increase from 2023. U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (2024) – [HUD Releases January 2024 Point-In-Time Count Report](https://archives.hud.gov/news/2024/pr24-327.cfm)

> [!question]- "If public camping is banned, where do homeless people go?"
> To emergency shelter, which the safety floor provides. The floor covers a basic necessity, not long-term housing; permanent housing comes from the market, opt-in programs, and private charity. Public spaces remain open for everyone's use rather than becoming encampments.

> [!question]- "Doesn't Housing First work better than shelters?"
> For keeping people housed, yes. A meta-analysis of randomized trials found Housing First participants about 2.46 times as likely to be stably housed, with fewer emergency-department visits and hospital stays, though no clear improvement in mental health or substance use. The framework does not dispute that evidence. Housing First is a housing subsidy, so it is left to charities, private groups, and opt-in funding, which are free to run it. The public floor stays at emergency shelter, and the only public rent support is the last-resort allowance.

#### Key Elements
- Housing supply is a market matter governed by secure property rights.
- Ordinary zoning is allowed; value-destroying regulation is a compensable taking.
- Growth is absorbed through densification and development.
- Housing subsidies require taxpayer opt-in.
- The safety floor provides emergency shelters only and is not a housing program, except the rent component of the last-resort allowance.
- Housing First run by charities, private groups, and opt-in funding.
- No camping on public property; no public drug use.

#### Rationale
Secure property and open land markets are how societies have historically absorbed population growth with rising living standards ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Property Rights|Authority & Civil Liberties]]). Homelessness has grown. HUD's 2024 point-in-time count found more than 770,000 people experiencing homelessness on a single night in January 2024, an 18% increase from 2023. HUD attributed part of the rise to steep rent increases and years of under-building, along with migration and natural disasters.[^hud-pit] Supply restrictions that hold back building bear directly on the problem. Randomized trials show Housing First keeps more people stably housed than treatment-first approaches, which is why the framework leaves charities and opt-in programs free to run it while the public floor stays at shelter. The basic-necessities floor is set out in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]], and the rule on public drug use in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Drug Policy|Criminal Justice & Security]].

## Healthcare

>[!tldr] In short
> People choose between a publicly supported plan and private insurance. No one is required to buy coverage, but the public plan has one yearly sign-up window and a modest surcharge for joining late.

#### Position
Healthcare is organized around freedom of choice. Individuals may opt into publicly supported systems or purchase private coverage. Mandates are rejected. The voluntary public plan has one annual open-enrollment window, and people who join late pay a modest surcharge, modeled on Medicare Part B's 10 percent for each full year a person was eligible but not enrolled. No one is required to join or penalized for staying out; the surcharge applies only to those who choose to join later. Emergency care stays tax-funded, and people on the last-resort allowance are enrolled automatically.

> [!question]- "Without a mandate, will healthy people avoid coverage and drive up costs?"
> Adverse selection is real: when the Massachusetts mandate took effect, healthier people entered the individual market and average costs and premiums fell. Evidence on the 2019 repeal of the federal penalty is mixed, with some studies finding no clear drop in coverage and others a modest rise in people becoming uninsured. The framework keeps choice and limits the problem without a mandate. The public plan has one annual enrollment window, and late joiners pay a modest surcharge, as in Medicare Part B, so people cannot wait until they are sick to sign up. No one is compelled to buy insurance.

> [!question]- "What about people who can't afford private coverage?"
> They can opt into a publicly supported system. Emergency medical care remains part of the safety floor, so no one in a genuine emergency is left without care ([[2. Political and Social Character#Economic Orientation|Economic Orientation]]). People on the last-resort allowance for severe disability or old-age poverty are enrolled in the public plan automatically, paid under that narrow exception ([[#Welfare and Entitlements]]).

#### Key Elements
- Freedom of choice as the organizing principle.
- Opt-in publicly supported systems available.
- Private coverage available.
- No mandates.
- Public plan: one annual open-enrollment window and a modest late-enrollment surcharge, modeled on Medicare Part B.
- Emergency care tax-funded; last-resort allowance recipients enrolled automatically.

#### Rationale
Compelling citizens to buy a product conflicts with the framework's limits on state coercion. Offering both public and private options keeps access while preserving choice. Federal health spending is a major driver of long-term budget pressure, which supports keeping public systems opt-in. CBO projects major federal health spending to rise from 5.8% of GDP in 2025 to 8.1% in 2055, with Medicare rising from 2.6% to 5.2% of GDP. Without a mandate, a voluntary plan risks adverse selection: Hackmann, Kolstad, and Kowalski found that the Massachusetts mandate brought healthier people into the individual market and lowered average costs and premiums. After the federal penalty was repealed in 2019, one study comparing states that kept their own mandates found no significant change in coverage or marketplace enrollment. Medicare Part B shows a non-mandate tool. Its late-enrollment penalty adds 10% for each full 12-month period a person was eligible but not enrolled, usually for as long as they have Part B. The framework borrows that design in modest form.

## Healthcare Funding, Emergency Care, and Drug Approval

>[!tldr] In short
> The public health option is voluntary, and taxpayers fund it only by choice. Emergency care is part of the basic services taxes pay for. Drug and insurance rules exist only to protect safety and stop consumer abuse, and nobody has to buy insurance.

#### Position
The publicly supported healthcare option is opt-in, and its funding follows the rule for all programs beyond core services: subsidies require taxpayer opt-in. Emergency services are among the core services funded by minimal necessary taxes. Drug and device regulation is legitimate for genuine safety and against anti-consumer practices, with the burden of proof resting on each regulation. Insurance regulation is limited to preventing fraud and anti-consumer practices. No one is required to buy coverage.

> [!question]- "What happens to people who have no coverage and need emergency care?"
> Emergency services are part of the core public functions funded by minimal necessary taxes. Current federal law also requires hospitals with emergency departments to screen anyone who arrives and to stabilize an emergency condition or transfer the patient appropriately, regardless of ability to pay. The framework keeps emergency care within the safety floor while leaving routine coverage to individual choice.

#### Key Elements
- The public option is opt-in, and its subsidies require taxpayer opt-in.
- Emergency services are a tax-funded core service.
- Exception: recipients of the last-resort allowance are covered in the public plan under that allowance's general-revenue exception.
- Drug and device regulation is limited to genuine safety and anti-consumer practices, with the burden of proof on the rule.
- Insurance regulation targets fraud and anti-consumer practices.
- No coverage mandate.

#### Rationale
Most Americans already arrange coverage without a mandate: 92.0% had health insurance for some or all of 2024, with private coverage (66.1%) more common than public (35.5%). Federal health spending is the main driver of long-term budget pressure; CBO projects it to rise from 5.8% of GDP in 2025 to 8.1% in 2055. Keeping the public option opt-in prevents that pressure from becoming compulsory redistribution. The Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act (1986) requires a screening examination and stabilizing treatment or appropriate transfer in hospitals with emergency departments, without delay to ask about payment. Drug regulation follows the same burden-of-proof rule as all business regulation in [[#Infrastructure and Regulation]]. Federal law now permits non-animal alternatives in drug approval, consistent with [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Bioethics, Progress, & Technology]].

## Education

### K-12 Funding

>[!tldr] In short
> I believe each state should fund about \$10,000 per child per year for basic K-12 education from its general revenue, roughly what Utah and Idaho spend, with an extra amount for students with disabilities that each state sets from actual need. The money follows the child to any public school, district or charter, anywhere in the state. Private and religious schools stay legal but get no public money; parents pay for them. Homeschooling families get a \$1,000 tax credit per child per year. Pre-K is excluded.

#### Position
I believe basic K-12 education should be funded by each state from its general revenue at about \$10,000 per child per year, close to what Utah and Idaho spend today. Students with disabilities receive an added amount that each state sets from actual need, with no fixed national figure. The money follows the child to any public school, district or charter, anywhere in the state, through statewide open enrollment; publicly funded means publicly accountable. Private and religious schools remain legal but receive no public funds, and parents pay for them themselves. Families who homeschool receive a fully refundable \$1,000 credit per homeschooled child per year against federal income tax. Pre-K is not part of the floor. The funding is regulated to avoid administrative bloat and waste.

**My view:** I believe that since restricting immigration will lower the population, we want as many things as possible to help parents and raise birth rates to replacement level ([[#Taxation|Taxation]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]).

> [!question]- "Is \$10,000 per child enough?"
> Utah spent \$10,333 per pupil and Idaho \$10,247 in FY2023, against a national average of \$16,526, and both scored at or above the national results in 2024 NAEP eighth-grade math and reading (Utah 282 and 261, Idaho 278 and 261). New York spent \$30,012 and scored 271 and 257. Across OECD countries, higher spending tracks math scores only up to about \$75,000 per student over ages 6 to 15, and the United States spends more than \$150,000.

> [!question]- "Where did the extra money go?"
> From FY1992 to FY2024, public school students rose 17.2% while all staff rose 51.5%. Teachers rose 33.4% and non-teaching staff 69.0%. Real spending per pupil rose 68% while real teacher pay fell 6%.

> [!question]- "Why include charter schools?"
> Charter schools received about 30%, or \$7,147, less per pupil than district schools across 18 cities in 2019–20. CREDO's 2023 national study found charter students gained the equivalent of 16 more days of learning in reading and 6 in math per year than matched peers in district schools.

#### Key Elements
- Each state funds about \$10,000 per child per year from state general revenue; no federal K-12 funding.
- Disability add-on set by each state from actual need; no fixed figure.
- The money follows the child to any public school, district or charter, statewide (open enrollment).
- Private and religious schools legal, with no public funds; parents pay.
- Homeschooling families get a fully refundable \$1,000 federal income-tax credit per child per year.
- Pre-K excluded; funding regulated against administrative bloat.

#### Rationale
Current spending is \$16,526 per pupil (Census, FY2023), and a large part of the growth since 1992 went to non-teaching staff rather than teachers. States near \$10,000 per pupil, such as Utah and Idaho, match or beat higher-spending states on NAEP. Funding that follows the child to any public school lets families leave schools that fail them, and charter schools have delivered gains at lower cost. Keeping public money out of private schools keeps it publicly accountable.

In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. Public K-12 schools collected about \$981.8 billion in revenue in FY2023, or \$19,871 per pupil: \$443.3 billion state, \$412.1 billion local, and \$126.4 billion federal (12.9 percent). Spending was \$18,905 per pupil in total expenditure and \$16,526 per pupil in current expenditure. The largest federal K-12 programs that block grants would replace are IDEA Part B grants to states (\$14.214 billion in FY2025) and Title I-A (\$18.4 billion in FY2025).

The evidence that local control by itself improves results is weak, while the evidence that school funding matters is strong. A 10 percent increase in per-pupil spending across all twelve years of school leads to 0.27 more completed years of education and 7.25 percent higher adult wages, with larger effects for children from low-income families. State funding of the per-child amount keeps that floor guaranteed in poorer districts.

### Governance and Curriculum

>[!tldr] In short
> The federal Department of Education is abolished and control is local first. States set minimum core-skill standards; districts pick curriculum, limited to core skills and objective facts.

#### Position
The federal Department of Education is abolished outright, and its programs are ended or returned to the states. States that structured budgets around the transitional block grants are not cut off cold; their commitments are honored through the phase-out window. Control is local first. Local districts run schools, and the state backs up the funding floor, with state and federal law limited to child protections such as security of children's personal information and training and oversight for childcare, minimum core-skill standards, and fraud and safety rules for public funds. Charter schools set their own curriculum and remain publicly accountable; each private school sets its own curriculum. Public-school curriculum standards exist and carve away biases of any sort; private schools set their own. For public schools, states set minimum standards for core skills, and local districts pick curriculum and materials. Teaching focuses on core skills (literacy, numeracy, science, and civic knowledge) and is entirely objective about facts and subject knowledge, not ideological, with no bias against frameworks such as critical race theory or toward forced patriotism.

> [!note]- Realistic transition
> In a realistic world, many systems — like taxes, like education, like all the systems that would be changing — would have some gradual period of change for most of these changes to happen. Here that means transitional block grants that phase out. That practical lag is a brief aside, not the main position.

#### Key Elements
- Federal Department of Education abolished outright; its programs ended or returned to states, with any block-grant run-off as a practical aside.
- Local-first control; the state backs up the funding floor; state and federal law limited to child protections, minimum core-skill standards, and fraud and safety rules for public funds.
- Charter schools set their own curriculum and remain publicly accountable; private schools set their own curriculum.
- Public schools: states set minimum core-skill standards; local districts pick curriculum and materials.
- Curriculum limited to core skills and objective facts, without ideological bias in either direction.

#### Rationale
Education does not require a central ministry. Canada, Belgium, and Bosnia and Herzegovina have no national education ministry; Switzerland's cantons run its schools; Germany and Australia have national ministries but leave schools to their states. On PISA 2022, all five wealthy decentralized countries outscored the U.S. in math (Switzerland 508, Canada 497, Belgium 489, Australia 487, Germany 475, U.S. 465), while the U.S. scored 504 in reading, trailing only Canada (507) among them. Bosnia and Herzegovina did not take part in PISA 2022; in PISA 2018 it scored 406 in math and 403 in reading, far below the others. The comparison cannot show that decentralization causes these results, and Bosnia's scores show it does not guarantee them.

Local control is chosen on grounds of accountability and closeness to the families affected, not on outcome evidence. Restricting curriculum to objective knowledge and skills keeps public schools from becoming instruments of ideology. Curriculum is already set locally in most of the country: local entities develop curriculum in 43 states, the state alone in one (Rhode Island), and both in five. Common Core has no official tracker; roughly 30 states still use it in original, renamed, or revised form, and about 15 adopted and then repealed or replaced it.

### Homeschooling

>[!tldr] In short
> Homeschooling is fully allowed, with no testing requirement. Homeschooling families receive a fully refundable \$1,000 federal income-tax credit per child per year ([[#K-12 Funding]]).

#### Position
Homeschooling is fully allowed, with no testing requirement. Homeschooling families receive a fully refundable \$1,000 federal income-tax credit per child per year ([[#K-12 Funding]]).

#### Key Elements
- Homeschooling fully allowed with no testing requirement.
- Fully refundable \$1,000 federal income-tax credit per homeschooled child per year.

#### Rationale
Homeschooling carries no testing requirement because no study shows that state testing or progress reviews improve homeschool outcomes. The most comprehensive review of the research (Kunzman and Gaither, 2020) finds the literature cannot isolate the effect of homeschooling, let alone the effect of regulation. Where data cannot show either way, the less regulatory option applies. Private schools publish yearly results because parents choose among schools and need comparable information; a homeschooling family is not choosing among providers, so the same reason does not apply.

### Private Education Oversight

>[!tldr] In short
> Private and religious schools have the full right to exist and are privately funded. They meet background-check, audit, and anti-fraud rules and publish a yearly norm-referenced test of their choice. Homeschooling keeps its testing exemption.

#### Position
Private and religious schools have the full right to exist. They are privately funded, with no public vouchers or education savings accounts. Private schools, religious schools, and any other privately funded education provider meet safety and fraud rules (background checks, financial audits, no fraud) and give a yearly nationally norm-referenced test of the school's choice, with results published. Homeschooling families are covered by the no-fraud rule and keep the testing exemption in the Homeschooling topic.

#### Key Elements
- Full right of private and religious schools to exist; privately funded, with no public vouchers or education savings accounts.
- Private schools, religious schools, and other privately funded providers meet safety and fraud rules: background checks, financial audits, no fraud.
- Yearly nationally norm-referenced test of the school's choice, with results published.
- Homeschooling keeps its testing exemption.

#### Rationale
State law on education is limited to child protections, minimum core-skill standards, and fraud and safety rules; these rules apply them to privately funded schools without public funds attached. A test of the school's choice gives parents comparable results without imposing the state curriculum. Florida's tax-credit scholarship, where schools choose a nationally norm-referenced test, raised college enrollment to 57 percent from 51 percent, while Louisiana's state-test model left students 0.41 standard deviations lower in math after one year with no significant effect on college entrance. The two programs differ in many ways, so the comparison cannot isolate testing rules as the cause.

### Higher Education and Student Loans

>[!tldr] In short
> Higher education is outside the public funding floor. Pell Grants become opt-in, state subsidies end, and new federal student lending stops; existing loans and contracts are honored.

#### Position
Higher education sits outside the public funding floor. Pell Grants are converted to opt-in, taxpayer-designated funding through a voluntary checkoff. State subsidies above the K-12 floor end. Existing commitments to higher-education subsidy recipients are honored through the transition window; they are not cut off cold. Student loans for college are personal loans, not federal loans from taxpayers; anyone who pursues higher education does so on a personal loan, not on the taxpayer's dime. New federal lending stops, and existing federal loans are collected on their current terms. Existing loan contracts, including forgiveness terms in force when they were signed, are honored, and no new forgiveness programs are created.

> [!question]- "Won't ending federal student loans shut people out of college?"
> College remains open to anyone who finances it through a personal loan. Ending federal lending and phasing out state subsidies removes the taxpayer from the risk of a borrower's degree choice, and the cost and value of each degree are settled between the student, the lender, and the college.

#### Key Elements
- Pell Grants converted to opt-in, taxpayer-designated funding (voluntary checkoff).
- State subsidies for higher education above the K-12 floor end.
- No new federal student loans; existing loans collected on current terms; existing contracts honored, no new forgiveness.

#### Rationale
In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. Pell Grants reached 7.3 million students in 2024-25 at a cost of \$38.6 billion, with an average grant of \$5,320 and a maximum of \$7,395. State and local support for higher education totaled \$149.2 billion in FY2025. Public Service Loan Forgiveness and related programs had discharged \$93.4 billion for about 1.25 million borrowers through April 2026.

Federal student loans place the cost of a borrower's degree choice on taxpayers while any benefit stays private, which conflicts with the framework's limits on redistribution and its emphasis on personal responsibility. The federal government is currently the main lender to U.S. college students through the Direct Loan program.

## Infrastructure and Regulation

>[!tldr] In short
> Taxes pay to keep core infrastructure safe and working. Businesses are regulated only for safety, serious environmental harm, worker exploitation, and consumer protection. Everything else is left to the market.

#### Position
Core infrastructure is maintained to safe and functional standards with tax revenue. Business regulation is legitimate only for genuine safety, prevention of serious environmental harm (as defined in [[9. Energy & Environment#Regulation and Conservation|Energy & Environment]]), prevention of clear worker exploitation, and protection against anti-consumer practices. Outside these domains, the market operates freely.

> [!question]- "Who decides what counts as genuine safety or clear exploitation?"
> The burden of proof rests on the regulation. A rule must show a real safety, environmental, worker-exploitation, or consumer-protection harm it addresses. Rules that cannot meet that test are removed, and the market governs everything else.

#### Key Elements
- Tax-funded core infrastructure maintained to safe and functional standards.
- Regulation limited to safety, serious environmental harm, worker exploitation, and anti-consumer practices.
- Free market outside those domains.

#### Rationale
Roads, bridges, and water systems are public goods that markets do not reliably provide, and their condition directly affects safety. Regulation beyond clear harms raises costs and is prone to capture by established firms. Dawson and Seater find that federal regulation accumulated since 1949 has significantly reduced U.S. output growth. Djankov and colleagues find that heavier entry regulation across 85 countries goes with more corruption and larger unofficial economies rather than better goods. Limiting regulation to defined harms keeps government effective where it is needed and out of the way elsewhere.
