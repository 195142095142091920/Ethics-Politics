## **Core Principles** 

Limited government, individual responsibility, low taxation, and maximum cultural and economic freedom, with only minimal safety nets. The state prioritizes free markets, personal choice, and fiscal restraint. Opt-in social programs and basic public services are acceptable; mandatory redistribution is not.

## **Economic System** 

#### Position 
Market-based systems grounded in secure property rights and price signals have produced the largest sustained gains in material living standards, poverty reduction, and innovation in recorded history. Comparative evidence favors these systems over socialist or heavily state-directed alternatives. Historical cases of sustained socialist organization produced chronic shortages, lower productivity, and eventual stagnation or collapse relative to more market-oriented peers. Hybrid systems that preserved significant private ownership and price signals performed better than those that suppressed them.

The state’s economic role should remain limited to core public goods - national defense, domestic public safety, courts, and basic infrastructure - plus a narrow set of evidence-based rules against force, fraud, and severe safety failures. Broad economic management, industrial planning, and taxpayer-funded corporate subsidies are rejected. A modest, slowly adjusted minimum-wage floor can be tolerated as a practical compromise; large or rapid increases are opposed on employment and cost grounds. In an age of advancing automation, a future modest citizen dividend framed as a share of national output remains conceivable, but only if it stays universal, limited, and does not expand mandatory redistribution or undermine work.

#### Key Elements
- Strong presumption in favor of open markets and private property.
- Comparative superiority of market systems over socialist and heavily state-directed alternatives.
- Government confined to public goods and a narrow regulatory core.
- Rejection of corporate subsidies and most forms of industrial policy.
- Acceptance of a low, inflation-adjusted wage floor; opposition to aggressive minimum-wage policy.
- Conditional openness to a future productivity-linked citizen dividend under strict constraints.

#### Rationale 
Comparative historical evidence shows that societies with greater economic freedom and stronger market institutions have achieved higher long-run growth, faster poverty reduction, and greater consumer abundance than those that suppressed private property and price signals. Targeted safety and anti-fraud rules address real market failures, but regulatory scope expands easily and is prone to capture; therefore the burden of proof remains on further intervention. Corporate subsidies distort capital allocation and reward political connection more reliably than economic value.

On minimum wages the evidence is genuinely contested. Some well-known studies (notably Card & Krueger) find little or no employment loss from moderate increases. Other research and several meta-analyses find negative employment effects, particularly for teenagers and the least skilled, with the size of the effect varying by the magnitude of the increase and local labor-market conditions. The weight of evidence does not support large or rapid increases as costless. A modest floor remains compatible with the overall framework; aggressive minimum-wage policy does not.

> **Markets work better than the alternatives for making people richer over time.**  
> Countries and periods that protected private property and let prices and competition guide most decisions produced higher living standards, less extreme poverty, and more innovation than systems that put the state in charge of most production and prices. The big historical examples (Soviet bloc vs. the West, North vs. South Korea, Venezuela’s collapse, China’s growth after it allowed more markets) all point in the same direction.
> 
> **Government still has a limited, important job.** 
> It needs to handle defense, basic law and order, courts, and core infrastructure. It also needs a few clear rules against force, fraud, and serious safety dangers. Beyond that, when government tries to heavily manage the economy or pick winners with subsidies, the results are usually wasteful and favor the politically connected.
> 
> **Corporate subsidies are mostly a bad deal.** 
> Taxpayers currently spend large amounts supporting specific companies. The track record shows these programs rarely deliver the promised broad benefits and often just reward lobbying.
>

> 
> **Looking ahead to automation.** 
> If machines eventually produce a lot more wealth with less human labor, a small universal payment tied to overall economic output *might* make sense someday. But it has to stay small and universal, not turn into another big redistributive welfare system that discourages work.
> 
> **Bottom line in plain English:** 
> Let markets do most of the heavy lifting because they have the best record at creating widespread prosperity. Keep government focused on the basics and on stopping clear harm. Avoid special favors for corporations. Be careful with wage mandates. Stay open to new ideas if technology changes the game, but don’t abandon the principles that produced the gains we already have.

## **Taxation** 

#### Position 
The tax system across all levels of government should be low, simple, and limited to funding core public functions.

At the federal level the centerpiece is a flat income tax with a large zero bracket and a short, transparent phase-in. Income below roughly \$40,000-\$45,000 for single filers and \$80,000-\$90,000 for joint filers (adjusted over time) faces a zero rate. A brief linear phase-in range then applies so that the rate rises smoothly to the full flat rate of 18-22%. Income above the phase-in range faces that single flat rate. Capital gains and dividends receive preferential but still flat treatment. Most deductions, credits, and special preferences are eliminated to keep the base broad and the rules simple.

Other taxes are kept secondary and constrained:

- A modest, broad-based retail sales tax may serve as a supporting or transitional revenue source. A European-style VAT is rejected in favor of the more transparent sales tax.
- Corporate tax should be low and simple, ideally structured so business income is taxed only once.
- Payroll taxes should decline as Social Security and Medicare are reformed toward more contribution-linked designs and a smaller residual safety net.
- Across-the-board employer payroll tax cuts and permanent full expensing of business investment (bonus depreciation) are the preferred tools for raising U.S. employment. Both let businesses keep their own money rather than receiving taxpayer funds.
- Property taxes remain primarily local, moderate, and tied to core local services (police, fire, roads, basic schools). They are retained but constrained.
- Estate and inheritance taxes are rejected.
- Excise taxes and tariffs are acceptable only when narrow and justified by clear externalities or national-interest policy.

Progressive income-tax rate schedules with rising marginal rates are rejected. The overall tax burden should remain low and matched to essential functions only: national defense, public safety, basic infrastructure, [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Education|a limited education funding floor]], debt service, and a small austere safety net. Temporary transitional measures (a high-end surtax or modest sales tax with an explicit end date) may be used while spending is brought under control.

> [!question]- "Are payroll tax cuts and full expensing just corporate subsidies by another name?"
> No. A subsidy transfers taxpayer money to a business. A payroll tax cut or full expensing takes less of the business's own money and transfers nothing. Both are consistent with the rule that taxpayer money subsidizes no business unless taxpayers opt in, and both apply across the board rather than to chosen firms or groups.

> [!question]- "Do these tax cuts stop outsourcing?"
> No study measures whether any incentive reduces outsourcing itself; the evidence measures domestic employment and investment. Across-the-board payroll tax cuts and full expensing are the two broad tools with measured gains in U.S. jobs.

#### Key Elements
- Flat federal income tax with a large zero bracket and a short linear phase-in.
- Low, simple corporate tax; business income taxed once.
- Across-the-board employer payroll tax cuts.
- Permanent full expensing of business investment.
- Modest retail sales tax instead of a VAT; local, moderate property taxes; no estate tax.
- Tax burden matched to essential functions, alongside spending restraint.

#### Rationale 
High and complex taxes distort work, saving, investment, and compliance. U.S. federal revenue as a share of the economy has remained relatively stable for decades even when top statutory rates changed dramatically; very high rates do not reliably produce proportionally higher revenue. Flat-tax reforms in other countries have shown that simplification is workable and need not collapse collections.

A large zero bracket increases take-home pay for lower- and middle-income households and supports private spending. A short linear phase-in removes the sharp notch that would otherwise encourage people to hold income just below the threshold. Lower marginal rates above the phase-in reduce disincentives to work and invest. A visible retail sales tax is preferable to a VAT because it is more transparent and less prone to quiet rate increases. Corporate and payroll taxes should not serve as primary redistribution tools. Property tax is retained at the local level because it is a practical and accountable way to fund local order and infrastructure; eliminating it would require less accountable replacements. Estate taxes confiscate property that has already been taxed.

No study measures whether any incentive reduces outsourcing itself. The evidence instead measures domestic employment and investment, and the two broad tools with measured gains in U.S. jobs are across-the-board payroll tax cuts and full expensing. In Norway, the abolition of lower regional payroll tax rates showed that each 1-percentage-point payroll tax increase cut local employment by 1.37% while wages fell only 0.32%, so lower payroll taxes support employment where wages do not fully adjust. A Swedish payroll tax cut for young workers raised their employment by 2–3 percentage points with no change in net wages. Bonus depreciation raised eligible investment by 10.4% (2001–04) and 16.9% (2008–10), and areas more exposed to it gained employment at roughly \$20,000–\$53,000 of forgone revenue per job, though earnings per worker did not rise. Targeted hiring credits, Opportunity Zones, domestic-content procurement rules, and firm-specific deals show weaker, short-lived, or far more costly results, often above \$110,000 per job.

This structure is sustainable only alongside spending restraint. It can support strong defense, core public goods, and a minimal safety floor. It cannot support the present long-term trajectory of major entitlement programs without reforming those programs. The tax rules and the commitment to a smaller federal government are designed to fit together.

## Welfare and Entitlements

#### Position
Existing welfare and entitlement programs are aggressively reduced to bare-minimum safety nets. Mandatory redistribution is rejected, and an opt-in funding model for remaining social programs is the preferred long-term structure. Benefits, when provided, cover only basic necessities and do not support comfort or lifestyle expansion. Privatization and structural reform of Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, and similar programs are pursued where feasible.

> [!question]- "Would cutting entitlements abandon people who paid into the system?"
> Reform is structural and gradual, not a sudden cutoff. Privatization and restructuring are pursued where feasible, and a bare-minimum safety net covering basic necessities remains. What is rejected is mandatory redistribution beyond that floor.

#### Key Elements
- Aggressive reduction of welfare and entitlements to bare-minimum safety nets.
- No mandatory redistribution.
- Opt-in funding for remaining social programs.
- Benefits limited to basic necessities.
- Privatization and structural reform of Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, and similar programs where feasible.

#### Rationale
Mandatory redistribution takes from some citizens to fund others, which the framework treats as coercion beyond the state's core role. Major entitlement programs also face long-term funding shortfalls under current law, so structural reform is needed regardless. An opt-in model lets those who wish to fund social programs do so while keeping a minimal floor for basic necessities.

> [!info]- What is an opt-in funding model?
> An opt-in model funds a program through voluntary contributions from those who choose to support it, rather than through mandatory taxes on everyone.

##### Sources
- U.S. Department of Agriculture – [Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP)](https://www.fns.usda.gov/snap/supplemental-nutrition-assistance-program)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Welfare and entitlements are cut back to a bare-minimum safety net for basic needs. Nobody is forced to fund redistribution, and other social programs run on voluntary contributions. Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid are privatized or restructured where possible.

## Healthcare

#### Position
Healthcare is organized around freedom of choice. Individuals may opt into publicly supported systems or purchase private coverage. Mandates are rejected.

> [!question]- "Without a mandate, will healthy people avoid coverage and drive up costs?"
> The framework accepts that trade-off in favor of individual choice. People choose between a publicly supported option and private coverage, and no one is compelled to buy insurance. Personal responsibility for that choice is part of the design.

#### Key Elements
- Freedom of choice as the organizing principle.
- Opt-in publicly supported systems available.
- Private coverage available.
- No mandates.

#### Rationale
Compelling citizens to buy a product conflicts with the framework's limits on state coercion. Offering both public and private options keeps access while preserving choice. Federal health spending is a major driver of long-term budget pressure, which supports keeping public systems opt-in.

>[!tldr]- Summary
> People choose between a publicly supported plan and private insurance. No one is required to buy coverage.

## Education

#### Position
Some level of basic K-12 education is publicly funded, regulated to avoid administrative bloat and waste. Pre-K is not part of the floor. Special education is included in the floor through a disability weight added to the per-pupil amount. The public funding obligation is portable: the floor is a fixed per-pupil dollar amount that follows the child through vouchers or education savings accounts, and parents may direct it to public, charter, or private schools or to homeschooling. Private schools that accept vouchers or education savings account funds meet safety and fraud rules (background checks, financial audits, no fraud) and give a yearly nationally norm-referenced test of the school's choice, with results published. Religious schools that accept vouchers are allowed, because the money belongs to the family's choice, not the school. Private schools have the full right to exist, and homeschooling is fully allowed, with no testing requirement, whether publicly funded or not. The federal Department of Education is abolished, and its programs are returned to the states as block grants or ended. Control is local first: local districts run schools, and the state only backs up the funding floor, with state and federal law limited to child protections such as security of children's personal information and training and oversight for childcare. With funding following the student, each school sets its own curriculum and parents choose among schools. Curriculum standards exist but carve away biases of any sort. For public schools, states set minimum standards for core skills, and local districts pick curriculum and materials. Teaching focuses on core skills (literacy, numeracy, science, and civic knowledge) and is entirely objective about facts and subject knowledge, not ideological, with no bias against frameworks such as critical race theory or toward forced patriotism.

Higher education sits outside the public funding floor. Pell Grants are converted to opt-in, taxpayer-designated funding, such as a checkoff or a donation-matched fund. State subsidies above the K-12 floor are phased out. Student loans for college are personal loans, not federal loans from taxpayers; anyone who pursues higher education does so on a personal loan, not on the taxpayer's dime. New federal lending stops, and existing federal loans are collected on their current terms. Existing loan contracts, including forgiveness terms in force when they were signed, are honored, and no new forgiveness programs are created. While federal loans are being phased out, colleges share the risk by repaying a portion of their students' unpaid balances. Trade schools, apprenticeships, and certificate programs are equal pathways under the same rules.

> [!question]- "Do vouchers drain funding from public schools?"
> Funding follows the student rather than the institution. Public schools keep the students and money of families who choose them, and families who choose otherwise take their share with them. Public education is funded to serve children, not to protect a particular system.

> [!question]- "Can a limited funding floor still produce a highly educated population?"
> Yes. The floor guarantees core skills for every child, portable funding lets families move to schools that teach well, and high-ability students can accelerate into demanding options. Families, employers, philanthropy, and students themselves continue to invest above the floor.

> [!question]- "Won't ending federal student loans shut people out of college?"
> College remains open to anyone who finances it through a personal loan. Ending federal lending removes the taxpayer from the risk of a borrower's degree choice, and holding colleges responsible for the results of their programs makes degrees with no market value harder to sell.

#### Key Elements
- Publicly funded basic K-12 education, kept free of administrative bloat and waste.
- A fixed per-pupil dollar amount that follows the child (vouchers or education savings accounts).
- Religious schools may accept vouchers; the money follows the family's choice, not the school.
- Pre-K excluded from the floor; special education included through a disability weight.
- Private schools taking public funds meet safety and fraud rules and give a yearly norm-referenced test of their choice, with results published.
- Full right of private schools to exist; homeschooling fully allowed with no testing requirement, whether publicly funded or not.
- Federal Department of Education abolished; its programs returned to states as block grants or ended.
- Local-first control; the state only backs up the funding floor; state and federal law limited to child protections.
- Each school sets its own curriculum, and parents choose.
- Public schools: states set minimum core-skill standards; local districts pick curriculum and materials.
- Curriculum limited to core skills and objective facts, without ideological bias in either direction.
- Pell Grants converted to opt-in, taxpayer-designated funding (checkoff or donation-matched fund).
- State subsidies for higher education above the K-12 floor phased out.
- No new federal student loans; existing loans collected on current terms; existing contracts honored, no new forgiveness.
- Colleges share risk on unpaid federal loans during the phase-out.
- Trade schools, apprenticeships, and certificates named as equal pathways under the same rules.

#### Rationale
Parents are best placed to choose their children's education, and competition among schools rewards quality. Schools that fail to educate lose students and funding, and schools that succeed attract them. Education does not require a central ministry. Canada, Belgium, and Bosnia and Herzegovina have no national education ministry; Switzerland's cantons run its schools; Germany and Australia have national ministries but leave schools to their states. On PISA 2022, all five wealthy decentralized countries outscored the U.S. in math (Switzerland 508, Canada 497, Belgium 489, Australia 487, Germany 475, U.S. 465), while the U.S. scored 504 in reading, trailing only Canada (507) among them. Bosnia and Herzegovina did not take part in PISA 2022; in PISA 2018 it scored 406 in math and 403 in reading, far below the others. The comparison cannot show that decentralization causes these results, and Bosnia's scores show it does not guarantee them.

Voucher rules follow the evidence: Florida's lighter-rules tax-credit scholarship, where schools choose a norm-referenced test, raised college enrollment to 57 percent from 51 percent and bachelor's completion by 1 to 2 points, while Louisiana's heavier state-test model left voucher students 0.41 standard deviations lower in math after one year with no significant effect on college entrance. Florida and Louisiana differ in many ways besides regulation, so this comparison cannot isolate regulation as the cause.

Homeschooling carries no testing requirement because no study shows that state testing or progress reviews improve homeschool outcomes; the most comprehensive review of the research (Kunzman and Gaither, 2020) finds the literature cannot isolate the effect of homeschooling, let alone the effect of regulation. Where data cannot show either way, the less regulatory option applies.

Local control keeps decisions close to the families affected. The evidence that local control by itself improves results is weak, while the evidence that school funding matters is strong: a 10 percent increase in per-pupil spending across all twelve years of school leads to 0.27 more completed years of education and 7.25 percent higher adult wages, with larger effects for children from low-income families. The state backstop on the funding floor keeps that floor guaranteed in poorer districts. Restricting curriculum to objective knowledge and skills keeps public schools from becoming instruments of ideology. School choice programs now operate in many states.

Federal student loans place the cost of a borrower's degree choice on taxpayers while any benefit stays private, which conflicts with the framework's limits on redistribution and its emphasis on personal responsibility. The federal government is currently the main lender to U.S. college students through the Direct Loan program.

> [!info]- What is a school voucher?
> A voucher is public funding that a family can direct to a school of its choice, including private schools, instead of the money going automatically to an assigned public school.

> [!info]- What is an education savings account?
> An education savings account (ESA) places a student's public education funding in a parent-controlled account that can pay for tuition, tutoring, curriculum materials, homeschool costs, and other approved education expenses.

## Infrastructure and Regulation

#### Position
Core infrastructure is maintained to safe and functional standards with tax revenue. Business regulation is legitimate only for genuine safety, prevention of serious environmental harm, prevention of clear worker exploitation, and protection against anti-consumer practices. Outside these domains, the market operates freely.

> [!question]- "Who decides what counts as genuine safety or clear exploitation?"
> The burden of proof rests on the regulation. A rule must show a real safety, environmental, worker-exploitation, or consumer-protection harm it addresses. Rules that cannot meet that test are removed, and the market governs everything else.

#### Key Elements
- Tax-funded core infrastructure maintained to safe and functional standards.
- Regulation limited to safety, serious environmental harm, worker exploitation, and anti-consumer practices.
- Free market outside those domains.

#### Rationale
Roads, bridges, and water systems are public goods that markets do not reliably provide, and their condition directly affects safety. Regulation beyond clear harms raises costs and is prone to capture by established firms. Limiting regulation to defined harms keeps government effective where it is needed and out of the way elsewhere.

> Taxes pay to keep core infrastructure safe and working. Businesses are regulated only for safety, serious environmental harm, worker exploitation, and consumer protection. Everything else is left to the market.

## Fiscal Policy

#### Position
A balanced budget is the proper target. Deficit spending is minimized. Long-term policy aims at reducing and eventually eliminating the national debt.

> [!question]- "Is eliminating the national debt realistic?"
> It is a long-term aim, not an immediate demand. Balanced budgets stop the debt from growing, and sustained restraint reduces it over time. The direction matters as much as the timeline.

#### Key Elements
- Balanced budget as the target.
- Minimal deficit spending.
- Long-term reduction and eventual elimination of the national debt.

#### Rationale
Persistent deficits shift costs to future taxpayers and raise interest payments that crowd out other spending. Federal debt and interest costs have grown substantially. Fiscal restraint is consistent with the framework's limits on government and its preference for low taxation.

##### Sources
- U.S. Treasury Fiscal Data – [Understanding the National Debt](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/americas-finance-guide/national-debt/)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> The budget should balance, deficits should be kept to a minimum, and the national debt should be paid down and eventually eliminated.

## Energy Policy

#### Position
A gradual shift toward cleaner energy sources is accepted. Nuclear power is incentivized, first through deregulation such as streamlined licensing and permitting. Any public financial support for nuclear is drawn only from taxpayers who opt in to fund it; no taxpayer is compelled to subsidize nuclear or any other energy industry. Renewables may be encouraged but are not mandated. Energy markets remain competitive and consumer-oriented, without taxpayer subsidies (absent opt-in) or coercive industrial policy.

> [!question]- "Does incentivizing nuclear contradict opposition to subsidies?"
> No. Nuclear is incentivized first by removing the regulatory and permitting obstacles that hold it back, and any public money comes only from taxpayers who opt in, consistent with [[9. Energy & Environment#Subsidies and Mandates|Energy & Environment]]. Nuclear is singled out because of its safety record and reliability. Energy policy is set out further in [[9. Energy & Environment|Energy & Environment]].

#### Key Elements
- Gradual transition to cleaner energy.
- Nuclear power incentivized: deregulation first; any public money only from taxpayers who opt in.
- Renewables encouraged, not mandated.
- Competitive, consumer-oriented energy markets.
- No taxpayer subsidies (absent opt-in) or coercive industrial policy.

#### Rationale
A reliable, affordable energy supply underpins economic growth, so transition must not disrupt it. Nuclear power produces large amounts of low-carbon electricity with one of the lowest death rates per unit of energy of any source. Competitive markets drive innovation more efficiently than mandates.
