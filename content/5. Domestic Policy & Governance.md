## Core Principles

Limited government, individual responsibility, low taxation, and maximum cultural and economic freedom, with only minimal safety nets. The state prioritizes free markets, personal choice, and fiscal restraint. Opt-in social programs and basic public services are acceptable; mandatory redistribution is not, beyond the narrow exceptions named in Welfare and Entitlements. Minimal necessary taxes fund core services: K-12 education, police, fire, emergency services, infrastructure upkeep, and the bare-minimum safety floor defined in Welfare and Entitlements. K-12 is funded by each state from its general revenue at about \$10,000 per child, as set out in [[#Education|Education]].

## Economic System

>[!tldr] In short
> **I believe markets work better than the alternatives for making people richer over time.**
> Countries and periods that protected private property and let prices and competition guide most decisions produced higher living standards, less extreme poverty, and more innovation than systems that put the state in charge of most production and prices. The big historical examples (Soviet bloc vs. the West, North vs. South Korea, Venezuela’s collapse, China’s growth after it allowed more markets) all point in the same direction.
>
> **Government still has a limited, important job.**
> It should handle defense, basic law and order, courts, and core infrastructure, and keep a few clear rules against force, fraud, and serious safety dangers. Beyond that, when government tries to heavily manage the economy or pick winners with subsidies, the results are usually wasteful and favor the politically connected.
>
> **Corporate subsidies are mostly a bad deal.**
> Taxpayers currently spend large amounts supporting specific companies. The track record shows these programs rarely deliver the promised broad benefits and often just reward lobbying. I would end them.
>
> **No minimum wage.**
> The research on job losses is genuinely mixed, but my position does not rest on it: a minimum wage dictates the terms of a voluntary contract, so I would abolish it. Low pay should be topped up through the tax return, and hardship handled through the safety net.
>
> **Owner income.**
> Royalties and lease payments the nation earns as an owner of federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum should not be paid out as a citizen dividend. Spread across every adult they would come to only tens of dollars a year. I would send them first to the [[Glossary#Earnings-only top-up|earnings-only top-up]] for low earners, with any remainder paying down the debt.
>
> **Bottom line:**
> Markets should do most of the heavy lifting - they have the best record at creating widespread prosperity. Government ought to stay focused on the basics and on stopping clear harm. I'd avoid special favors for corporations and set no wage floor, topping up low pay through the tax return instead.

#### Position 
Market-based systems grounded in secure property rights and price signals have produced the largest sustained gains in material living standards, poverty reduction, and innovation in recorded history. Comparative evidence favors these systems over socialist or heavily state-directed alternatives. Historical cases of sustained socialist organization produced chronic shortages, lower productivity, and eventual stagnation or collapse relative to more market-oriented peers. Hybrid systems that preserved significant private ownership and price signals performed better than those that suppressed them.

The state’s economic role should remain limited to core public goods - national defense, domestic public safety, courts, basic infrastructure, a bare-minimum safety net, and basic K-12 funding averaging about \$10,000 per child, paid by each state from its general revenue - plus a narrow set of evidence-based rules against force, fraud, and severe safety failures. Broad economic management, industrial planning, and taxpayer-funded corporate subsidies are rejected. There is no minimum wage; wages are set by voluntary agreement, and hardship is covered by the bare-minimum safety net ([[#Labor and Unions|Labor and Unions]]). Income the nation earns as an owner, such as royalties and lease payments on federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum, goes first to funding the earnings-only top-up ([[#Welfare and Entitlements]]), and any remainder goes automatically to reducing the national debt ([[#Fiscal Policy]]), with no annual legislative division. It is not paid out as a citizen dividend.

> [!question]- "Why is there no citizen dividend?"
> Owner income is too small to matter as a payment: spread across every adult, it comes to tens of dollars a year. Put first toward the earnings-only top-up, with any remainder going to debt reduction, the same money supports work and lowers the burden on future taxpayers. Because it is income the nation earns as an owner, using it this way takes nothing from any citizen through taxation.

> [!question]- "Don't markets leave some people behind?"
> Markets do not guarantee equal outcomes, and I do not claim they do. A minimal floor for basic necessities is funded by minimal necessary taxes, and anyone who wants to fund more can opt in to do so. What I reject is mandatory redistribution beyond that floor and state direction of the economy, because the historical record shows those approaches produce shortages and stagnation that leave far more people behind. As markets spread after 1990, the share of the world living in extreme poverty (under \$3.00 a day) fell from 43.4% in 1990 to 10.1% in 2024, according to the World Bank. Hunger has fallen too but has not ended: the UN estimated that 8.2% of the world, about 673 million people, faced hunger in 2024, with the share above 20% in Africa. In 2023, the poorest 10% in the most economically free quarter of countries had incomes 7.8 times higher than the poorest 10% in the least free quarter. Extreme poverty affected 2% of people in the freest quarter compared with 52% in the least free. These Fraser Institute figures are cross-country correlations, not measured effects of economic freedom.


#### Rationale 
Comparative historical evidence shows that societies with greater economic freedom and stronger market institutions have achieved higher long-run growth, faster poverty reduction, and greater consumer abundance than those that suppressed private property and price signals. Targeted safety and anti-fraud rules address real market failures, but regulatory scope expands easily and is prone to capture; therefore the burden of proof remains on further intervention. Corporate subsidies distort capital allocation and reward political connection more reliably than economic value.

Federal owner income is real but small. In FY2024 the Office of Natural Resources Revenue disbursed \$16.45 billion from energy and mineral production on federal and Tribal lands and federal offshore areas, of which \$6.32 billion went to the U.S. Treasury and \$1.18 billion to Tribes and individual Indian mineral owners, whose property it is. Excluding the Tribal share and spread across about 267 million adults, federal owner income comes to roughly \$24 to \$57 per adult per year: too small to matter as a payment, but useful toward the top-up and the debt. The top-up costs about \$26 billion a year on my estimate ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#^topup-cost|Welfare and Entitlements]]), far more than the \$6.32 billion the Treasury received from federal energy and mineral revenue in FY2024. So at current levels all owner income goes to the top-up, general revenue covers the rest, and debt reduction receives owner income only once it exceeds the top-up's cost.

Defenders of the minimum wage cite well-known studies, notably Card & Krueger, that find little or no employment loss from moderate increases. Other research and several meta-analyses find negative employment effects, particularly for teenagers and the least skilled, with the size of the effect varying by the magnitude of the increase and local labor-market conditions. The weight of evidence does not support large or rapid increases as costless, and the government ought to abolish the minimum wage on rights grounds, not on the employment evidence alone ([[#Labor and Unions|Labor and Unions]]).

## Socialism

>[!tldr] In short
> Socialism in its core sense, social or state ownership of the means of production and planning in place of markets, is antithetical to sentientism and negative rights, and a terrible idea: it takes property, forces redistribution, compels labor and speech, and suppresses dissent. Social democracy is different: the Nordic countries are market economies with large welfare states. I share the goal of a floor for basic needs but fund only a minimal one and leave the rest to markets and voluntary giving.

#### Position
The word "socialism" covers very different systems, and I judge each by what it does to individual rights. In its core sense — social or state ownership of the means of production and planning in place of markets (Stanford Encyclopedia: "the bulk of the means of production is under social, democratic control") — three features are incompatible with my framework: (1) collective or state ownership of productive property imposed by force, including expropriation; (2) central planning that replaces prices and voluntary exchange; (3) compulsory redistribution beyond a minimal floor for basic needs. Each takes property or overrides consent from people who have harmed no one.

Social democracy keeps private ownership and markets and funds a large welfare state through taxes. The Nordic countries are in this category (Denmark's PM, 2015: "Denmark is far from a socialist planned economy. Denmark is a market economy."). Social democracy shares only the third feature, so it is judged as too large a compulsory state, not as socialism in the core sense ([[11. Modern Political Parties#Nordic Social Democracy|Nordic Social Democracy]]).

Socialism in the core sense is antithetical to sentientism and negative rights: it seizes property, forces redistribution, compels labor and speech, and suppresses dissent. Cases, calculation-problem argument, and country comparisons live in the Q&As, Rationale, and Comparisons — not repeated here. Shared ground I keep: a bare-minimum safety floor, voluntary cooperatives, and voluntary giving. Flat tax, named minimal welfare/top-up floor, and the time-limited Sanctuary Levy are **not** excluded socialism ([[Glossary#Socialism (excluded)|Socialism (excluded)]]).

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **Venezuela, 2016:** the ENCOVI living-conditions survey by three Venezuelan universities found 74.3% of the population lost an average of 8.7 kg (19 pounds) and about 9.6 million people ate two or fewer meals a day. CNBC (2017) – [Venezuela’s crisis causes its people to cut meals and lose weight](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/02/20/venezuelas-crisis-cause-its-people-to-cut-meals-and-lose-weight.html)

**My view:** The right of citizens to simply be left alone is incomprehensible to socialists who are convinced they have a mandate to rearrange other people's lives.

> [!question]- "Isn't socialism just about fairness and helping people who are struggling?"
> The government ought to fund a bare-minimum safety floor for basic needs through minimal necessary taxes, and anyone may give more voluntarily ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Welfare and Entitlements|Welfare and Entitlements]]). The disagreement is about means. Taking ownership of productive property, or compelling redistribution beyond that floor, uses force against people who violated no one's rights. The record of planned economies also suggests it leaves the poor worse off, not better.

> [!question]- "The Nordic countries are successful and socialist. Doesn't that disprove the critique?"
> No, because they are not socialist in the core sense. Their own leaders describe them as market economies, and the Heritage Foundation's 2025 Index of Economic Freedom ranks Denmark 7th, Norway 9th, Sweden 12th, and Finland 13th in the world. What they add is high taxes and a large welfare state. I reject that compulsory scale too, but it's a different argument from the one against planning and state ownership. Their success tends to support markets with private property, which they kept.

> [!question]- "Venezuela failed because of oil prices and U.S. sanctions, not socialism."
> They were shocks, not the cause. Oil prices fell sharply in 2014, and ECLAC links part of the 2020 drop to tighter U.S. sanctions on the public sector. But the IMF found that "economic mismanagement accelerated and amplified the economic collapse," and price controls were producing shortages of staples in 2007, during the oil boom. The IMF calls the result the largest collapse for a country not at war in almost half a century. The policies made the economy fragile before the shocks arrived.

> [!question]- "Isn't Cuba's poverty caused by the U.S. embargo?"
> The embargo raises costs, but it is not the cause of Cuba's poverty. It has blocked most U.S. trade and investment since the early 1960s. Its extraterritorial provisions, such as the 1996 Helms-Burton Act, also raise costs for foreign firms and banks that deal with Cuba. But the embargo does not stop Cuba from trading with the rest of the world. The European Union describes itself as Cuba's main trade partner and largest foreign investor, and in the 1990s Spain, Canada, and China were among its leading partners. U.S. law has also allowed agricultural exports to Cuba since 2000. Through 1990 the Soviet Union provided more than \$65 billion in aid and price subsidies, and the economy still shrank by roughly a third when that aid ended. Analysts of the 1990s crisis named the "inefficiencies of centrally planned economies" as its underlying cause. The embargo makes a weak economy worse; it does not explain why the economy is weak.

> [!question]- "Doesn't Cuba have excellent health care and literacy?"
> Cuba reports high literacy, long life expectancy, and low infant mortality. These figures come from a government that controls statistics and the press, so researchers treat some of them with caution. A 2017 study in *Demography* found patterns in Cuba's records of fetal and newborn deaths consistent with misclassification. It estimated Cuba's infant mortality at about 9.0 to 10.1 per 1,000 live births, against the officially reported 5.8. Even taking the official figures at face value, I do not accept good services as justification for taking property and forbidding exit.

> [!question]- "Couldn't modern computers solve the planning problem?"
> The argument does not depend on computing power. Hayek's point was that the knowledge an economy runs on is "dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess," much of it about local conditions and changing preferences that are never written down. Mises argued that without market prices for capital goods, planners cannot compare the costs of different uses of resources. Prices gather that information because people trade with their own resources. A computer can process data faster, but it cannot produce the prices that come from voluntary exchange.

> [!question]- "Is market socialism, with worker cooperatives competing in markets, also rejected?"
> Not if it is voluntary. Workers may form cooperatives, own firms jointly, and compete in markets under my freedom of association. What is rejected is making that model compulsory, by forbidding private ownership or seizing firms to convert them.


#### Rationale
I protect property and voluntary exchange as extensions of the right not to be subjected to force ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Political Philosophy|Political Philosophy]]). Socialism in the core sense removes both by design. It also faces a problem of knowledge that holds even under good intentions. Mises argued in 1920 that without market prices for the means of production, a planning authority cannot calculate which uses of resources are more or less valuable. Hayek added in 1945 that the relevant knowledge "never exists in concentrated or integrated form," and that prices are how a market economy uses it. The record of planned economies matches this reasoning ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Economic System|Economic System]]).

Venezuela is the most recent large case. Under Hugo Chávez the state took over the Orinoco oil projects of ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips in 2007. An international arbitration tribunal later found the Conoco takeover unlawful and awarded the company about \$8.7 billion. Price controls on about 400 basic products, imposed from 2003, were followed by recurring shortages of milk, meat, and sugar. The IMF estimates that real GDP contracted by more than 75% between 2013 and 2021, which it calls "the single largest economic collapse for a non-conflict country in almost half a century." Hyperinflation began in late 2017, and ECLAC reported annualized inflation still at 1,198% in November 2021. By late 2025, UNHCR and the R4V platform counted nearly 7.9 million Venezuelan refugees and migrants worldwide.

Cuba is the longest-running case in the hemisphere. In 1960 the revolutionary government nationalized the banks, the foreign oil refineries, and the property of U.S. nationals. The U.S. Foreign Claims Settlement Commission later found 5,911 of those claims compensable, worth about \$1.9 billion in principal. The state still owns most means of production and employs a majority of the workforce, according to the Congressional Research Service. Basic food has been rationed through the *libreta* ration book for decades. When Soviet aid ended, official statistics show GDP fell 34.8% from 1989 to 1993. Recent years have brought a second crisis. In 2021 the official consumer price index rose 77%, and economists estimate true inflation at 174% to 700%. In September 2024 the government cut the subsidized daily bread ration from 80 to 60 grams for lack of flour. In October 2024 the national grid collapsed, leaving the whole island without power. U.S. Customs and Border Protection recorded 224,607 encounters with Cuban nationals in fiscal 2022 and 200,287 in fiscal 2023. Cuba's own statistics office reports that the population fell to 9,748,007 at the end of 2024, 307,961 fewer than a year earlier, with net emigration of about 251,000 that year.

Social democrats share one premise with it: no one should lack basic necessities. The overlap ends there. It keeps only a bare-minimum floor funded by minimal necessary taxes, and it differs on scale and on compulsion. Help beyond that floor comes from markets, families, and voluntary giving, not from taking property.

#### Comparisons
The clearest tests are cases where similar people were divided between systems, or where one country changed systems. Critics note that none is a controlled experiment and that each has confounders, set out below; in every case the more market-oriented system or period outperformed the planned one. The Soviet Union and Mao's China are covered in [[2. Political and Social Character#Communism|Communism]].

- **East and West Germany.** The same nation, divided after 1945, ran a planned economy in the East and a market economy in the West. Before monetary union in 1990, IMF staff put East German labor productivity at 30% to 40% of the West German level. The German government's later account puts it at only about 20% to 25% in 1988. The two states had different occupying powers and foreign support, so not every gap is due to the economic system.
- **North and South Korea.** In 2023 the Bank of Korea estimated North Korea's gross national income per person at 1.59 million won, 3.4% of South Korea's 47.25 million won. The estimate is uncertain because North Korea publishes almost no data, and both states relied heavily on foreign patrons. South Korea also grew under military rule before it democratized, so the comparison is about markets, not about democracy.
- **China after 1978.** After China began market reforms, starting with returning farmland to individual households, GDP growth averaged over 9% a year. The World Bank credits China with lifting close to 800 million people out of extreme poverty, about three-quarters of the global reduction over that period. China remains a one-party state with a large state sector, so the gains came from introducing markets, not from changing the political system.
- **Vietnam after 1986.** Before the Đổi Mới reforms, inflation reached 487% in 1986. After the country moved to market-oriented policies, growth averaged about 8% a year in the 1990s. The poverty rate fell from 58% in 1993 to 37% in 1998, and GDP per person rose from under \$500 in 1986 to \$5,066 in 2025, according to the World Bank. As in China, the ruling party kept its monopoly on power.
- **India after 1991.** After a balance-of-payments crisis, India dismantled much of the "license raj" system of industrial licensing. Average tariffs fell from 80% in 1990 to 37% in 1996, and IMF research finds the reforms produced "decidedly more stable and sustainable growth from 1992 on." Rodrik and Subramanian note that India's growth had already accelerated around 1980, before the main liberalization, so 1991 deepened a change already under way rather than starting it.
- **Tanzania under Ujamaa.** In 1973 the ruling party made it compulsory for rural Tanzanians to live in planned villages, and scholarly estimates put the number moved at about five million. Many farmers were moved far from their fields and trees. Purchases by the cashew authority fell from 140,000 tons in 1973 to 44,000 tons in 1978–79. By 1985, IMF staff report, agricultural production had been declining since the 1970s and cash-crop exports had fallen by half since 1970. Falling world crop prices and oil shocks contributed. After liberalization began in 1986, growth averaged about 7% a year from 2000.
- **Across countries.** Single cases have confounders, so I also look at the cross-country record. Grier and Grier studied 141 countries from 1970 to 2015 and identified 49 sustained jumps in economic freedom. Compared with matched countries that did not reform, average annual growth ran about 2.1 to 2.9 percentage points higher over the next five years and about 1.0 to 1.9 points higher over ten; on their illustration, that leaves a reforming country roughly 16% richer after a decade. Critics note that reforms are not randomly assigned, and that the result is an average, not a guarantee for every country; the authors match on investment, institutions, human capital and other covariates, and the effect holds across their different thresholds and estimators.

I see the same pattern inside the United States. Prices have risen fastest where government pays, subsidizes or regulates most heavily, in hospital care, college, child care and rent, and have fallen or stayed far below inflation where firms compete for customers, in televisions, toys, software, phones and clothing (data below). As the commentator Brivael Le Pogam put it, "Capitalism is the force that drove down the price of almost everything it touched. The state is the force that blew up the price of almost everything it captured." X – [Brivael Le Pogam, 17 Oct 2026](https://x.com/brivael/status/2111420678914085225)

Televisions fell 98%, toys 72%, computer software 68% and wireless phone service 41%, while apparel rose 10% and new vehicles 26%, far below inflation. ## Labor and Unions

>[!tldr] In short
> Unions should be voluntary, and no one should have to join or pay one to work. Licensing ought to exist only to prevent real harm to consumers. I oppose a minimum wage: pay is set by agreement, fraud and forced labor stay crimes, and the safety floor covers hardship.

#### Position
Workers may form and join unions, and employers and workers may bargain freely, under freedom of association. No one is compelled to join or pay a union as a condition of work. Occupational licensing must meet the same burden of proof as any regulation: it is justified only where it prevents real harm to consumers, not to limit competition. There is no minimum wage. A minimum wage dictates the terms of a voluntary contract between a worker and an employer, and it can price people whose labor is worth less than the floor out of work, though how many jobs are lost is disputed. Neither is consistent with [[Glossary#Negative rights|negative rights]]. Wages are set by agreement, including collective agreements reached by unions that workers join voluntarily. Low pay is supplemented by the earnings-only top-up and hardship is covered by the bare-minimum safety floor, both set out in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]], not by price controls on labor. Fraud, coercion, wage theft, and forced labor remain crimes. The abolition covers state minimum wages too: rights do not change at state lines, and the top-up uses one national zero-bracket line.

> [!question]- "Doesn't banning compelled dues weaken unions?"
> Unions remain free to organize and bargain. They keep members by delivering value, as any voluntary association does.

> [!question]- "Without a minimum wage, won't workers be exploited?"
> Exploitation through force, fraud, or coercion stays illegal: wage theft, deceptive contracts, and forced labor are rights violations and are prosecuted. What ends is the state setting the price of a voluntary agreement. Workers keep the right to refuse a job, to quit, and to join a union and bargain collectively. Several of the highest-wage countries in Europe, including Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Austria, and Italy, have no national minimum wage; wages there are set by collective agreements. Those agreements rest on very strong unions, which the law ought to allow only as voluntary associations, so the outcome in the United States would not simply copy theirs. People who still cannot earn enough are covered by the safety floor.

> [!question]- "Why abolish the minimum wage instead of keeping a small one?"
> A floor of any size is a rule that two consenting adults may not agree on certain terms, and it hits hardest those whose skills are worth the least. The federal minimum already binds very few people: in 2024, 1.0 percent of hourly paid workers earned \$7.25 or less. The safety floor, funded by minimal necessary taxes, handles hardship directly rather than through a price control that can price people out of work. How large that job loss is remains disputed in the research, and the abolition does not depend on its size.

> [!question]- "Isn't the minimum wage just a wage floor, not a ban on work?"
> In price terms it is a floor: the law forbids a voluntary wage below a set price. When a worker's productivity is worth less than that floor, the job often does not exist at the legal wage. Employment effects are debated in size; the definition is not. I abolish the floor because negative rights forbid the state from dictating the terms of a peaceful contract, and I cover hardship through the earnings-only top-up and the safety floor instead ([[#Welfare and Entitlements|Welfare and Entitlements]]).

> [!question]- "Without a minimum wage, how do low earners get by?"
> Through pay set by agreement plus the earnings-only top-up built into the flat tax's zero bracket. A low earner's pay is topped up through the ordinary tax return, and the top-up shrinks gradually as earnings rise, reaching zero at the zero-bracket line. So taking more hours or a raise always leaves the worker better off. The support comes through the tax system rather than from a rule that the employer must pay more, so no one is priced out of a job ([[#Welfare and Entitlements]]).


#### Rationale
Union membership was 9.9% of U.S. wage and salary workers in 2024 (14.3 million), with 32.2% in the public sector and 5.9% in the private sector, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. In *Janus v. AFSCME* (2018) the Supreme Court held that compelling public employees to pay agency fees violates the First Amendment. Freedom of association is set out in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Freedom of Association|Authority & Civil Liberties]].

In 2024, 82,000 U.S. workers earned exactly the federal minimum of \$7.25 an hour and 760,000 earned less, together 1.0 percent of hourly paid workers, down from 13.4 percent in 1979. Most were young, and about two-thirds worked in leisure and hospitality, where tips may supplement wages. As of July 2026, Denmark, Italy, Austria, Finland, and Sweden had no national minimum wage, and in those countries, as in Iceland, Norway, and Switzerland, minimums are set by collective agreements for specific sectors. In 2022 the share of low-wage earners was 4.1 percent in Sweden, 6.5 percent in Finland, 8.8 percent in Italy, and 9.7 percent in Denmark, against 14.7 percent across the EU. Portugal, which has a statutory minimum wage, had the lowest share, at 1.8 percent, so the absence of a minimum wage does not by itself explain low-pay rates.

Defenders cite Cengiz, Dube, Lindner, and Zipperer, who studied 138 state minimum-wage increases and found that the overall number of low-wage jobs was essentially unchanged five years later. Neumark and Shirley's review of U.S. studies found the opposite in most of the literature: a clear preponderance of negative employment estimates, strongest for teens, young adults, and the less educated. The abolition does not rest on the employment evidence: I rest it on the right of workers and employers to set their own terms.

## Antitrust

>[!tldr] In short
> Big is not illegal in my view. Collusion, fraud, and coercion should be prosecuted, and genuine bottlenecks over essential services can be regulated.

#### Position
Size alone does not justify intervention. Firms that grow by serving customers better have violated no one's rights. Intervention is justified against fraud, collusion to fix prices or divide markets, and coercive conduct, and against genuine monopoly bottlenecks where refusal would leave people without reasonable alternatives for essential services. Any intervention carries the burden of proof, as all regulation does.

> [!question]- "Won't large firms abuse their power without stronger antitrust?"
> Abuse through fraud, collusion, or coercion is prosecuted. A large firm without such conduct faces competition and new entrants; breaking it up for its size punishes success rather than wrongdoing.


#### Rationale
The Sherman Act prohibits contracts and conspiracies in restraint of trade and monopolization (15 U.S.C. §§ 1–2). The bottleneck exception follows [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Freedom of Association|Authority & Civil Liberties]].

## Taxation

>[!tldr] In short
> I want a federal income tax with a large zero bracket, a short phase-in, then a single flat 20% (22% through 2041 with the transition surcharge), and capital income at a preferential flat 15% (17% through 2041). The base should be broad; family measures are the named exceptions ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#^family-credits|Births]]). I'd have no sales tax or VAT, no estate tax, and local moderate property taxes. A transition levy (≤16% of wages) should replace FICA for grandfathered SS/Medicare and debt, and a Sanctuary Levy ≤3% on income above \$75,000/\$150,000 is time-limited ([[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]). I support full expensing now and across-the-board employer payroll cuts as the levy steps down. The tax take should match essential functions only.

#### Position
The tax system across all levels of government should be low, simple, and limited to funding core public functions.

At the federal level the centerpiece is a flat income tax with a large zero bracket and a short, transparent phase-in. Income below \$42,000 for single filers and \$84,000 for joint filers (adjusted for inflation) faces a zero rate. A linear phase-in then applies over the next \$10,000 for single filers (\$42,000 to \$52,000) and \$20,000 for joint filers (\$84,000 to \$104,000), so that the rate rises smoothly to the full flat rate of 20%. Income above the phase-in range faces that single flat rate. Capital gains and dividends receive a preferential but still flat 15% rate, which rises to the full 20% rate only if the automation trigger in [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#AI and Automation|AI and Automation]] is met. Through 2041 the 2-point transition surcharge applies to both rates, so the top rates are 22% on ordinary income and 17% on capital gains and dividends (22% if the trigger is met); permanent progressive rate schedules are rejected, and the surcharge goes to the debt. ^surcharge

Most deductions, credits, and special preferences are eliminated to keep the base broad and the rules simple. ^rate-schedule

Inherited assets keep the original owner's cost basis (carryover basis) rather than a stepped-up basis. Today an heir's cost basis is "stepped up" to the value at death, so gains built up during the owner's life are never taxed; the Joint Committee on Taxation estimates this exclusion at \$336 billion in forgone revenue for fiscal years 2024 to 2028. Under carryover basis the heir keeps the original cost basis and pays the flat tax only when the asset is sold. Nothing is taxed at death, so this is not an estate or inheritance tax, which I reject. JCT estimated that an earlier version of this change would raise about \$105 billion over ten years. Joint Committee on Taxation – [Estimates of Federal Tax Expenditures for Fiscal Years 2024-2028 (JCX-48-24)](https://www.jct.gov/publications/2024/jcx-48-24/); Congressional Budget Office – [Change the Tax Treatment of Capital Gains From Sales of Inherited Assets](https://www.cbo.gov/budget-options/54792). Carryover basis is also how capital income stays taxed as AI ownership grows ([[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#AI and Automation|AI and Automation]]). ^carryover

The exceptions are the family measures set out in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#^family-credits|Births and Family Formation]] and the earnings-only top-up in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]]. How the family package counts under the franchise is fixed in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#^net-fiscal-vote|net fiscal voting bar]].

**My view:** Since restricting immigration will lower the population, we want as many things as possible to help parents and raise birth rates to replacement level ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]).

Secondary taxes (no VAT/sales; low corporate tax so business income is taxed once; transition levy; surcharge; Sanctuary Levy; permanent full expensing; local moderate property taxes; no estate tax; narrow excise and tariffs only), the two named time-limited exceptions to the single flat rate (the transition surcharge through 2041 and the Sanctuary Levy), and the essential-functions tax-take rule — including sanctuary care for the animals freed by the shutdown — are set out in In short and the example callouts above, not re-bulleted here.

**My view:** The honest way to keep promises to people who already paid in is one visible levy with a hard ceiling and a written end, not a hidden mix of taxes.

> [!question]- "How far below replacement is the U.S. birth rate?"
> The U.S. total fertility rate was about 1.6 births per woman in 2024, a record low, against a replacement level of about 2.1. CDC/NCHS – [Births: Final Data for 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK623381/)

> [!example]- Examples: federal income tax under the flat tax
> Single, \$60,000: \$2,600. Single parent, \$60,000, one child: \$1,100 after the \$1,500 child credit. Married, \$120,000, two children: \$2,200 after \$3,000 in child credits. Above the phase-in, tax is 20% of income minus the zero line minus half the phase-in width (\$5,000 single, \$10,000 joint). These use the permanent 20% rate; through 2041 the 2-point transition surcharge also applies above the phase-in. While the Sanctuary Levy runs, it adds about 1.71% on my middle case, but only on income above \$75,000 single or \$150,000 joint, so the single \$60,000 filer pays none of it.

> [!question]- "Are payroll tax cuts and full expensing just corporate subsidies by another name?"
> No. A subsidy transfers taxpayer money to a business. A payroll tax cut or full expensing takes less of the business's own money and transfers nothing. Both are consistent with the rule that taxpayer money subsidizes no business unless taxpayers opt in, and both apply across the board rather than to chosen firms or groups. Full expensing applies now; payroll tax cuts are a long-run goal as the transition levy steps down.

> [!question]- "Do these tax cuts stop outsourcing?"
> No study measures whether any incentive reduces outsourcing itself; the evidence measures domestic employment and investment. Across-the-board payroll tax cuts and full expensing are the two broad tools with measured gains in U.S. jobs. Full expensing applies now; payroll tax cuts are a long-run goal as the transition levy steps down.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the flat tax mostly cut taxes for the rich?"
> In dollar terms, the largest cuts go to the highest earners: the top federal rate is 37% today, against a flat 20% here (22% through 2041 with the transition surcharge), and capital income keeps a preferential rate of 15% (17% through 2041). Lower earners gain through the large zero bracket and the earnings-only top-up, and parents at every income get a refundable credit per child ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#^family-credits|Births and Family Formation]]), so most low-income workers owe no income tax at all. I do not claim the change is neutral by income. It rests on principle: one rate for everyone above the zero bracket, with no penalty for earning more. My static model's household table in [[#Fiscal Policy]] shows every household tested paying less federal tax than today; those are my estimates, not official scores.

> [!question]- "Couldn't taxing billionaires pay for the government?"
> Not for long. While \$8.4 trillion is more than a full year of federal spending, and most wealth-tax proposals are yearly levies rather than one-time seizures, the yearly version raises little: Saez and Zucman estimated that Senator Warren's 2019 plan (2% a year above \$50 million, 1% more above \$1 billion) would raise about \$2.75 trillion over ten years, about 1% of GDP a year, under a twentieth of federal spending at about 21% of GDP. But the fortunes themselves run out fast. Forbes counted 989 U.S. billionaires worth a combined \$8.4 trillion on March 1, 2026. Federal outlays were \$7.01 trillion in fiscal 2025, so taking every billionaire's entire fortune would pay for about 14 months of federal spending, once. Counting state and local governments, U.S. government spending ran at about \$11.1 trillion a year in 2025 (BEA), which the same fortunes would cover for about nine months. Spending recurs every year; a fortune can be taken only once. That is one more reason I reject wealth taxes at every level, including California's Proposition 40 ([[#State and Local Taxes]]).

#### State and Local Taxes
States set their own taxes ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Federalism|Federalism]]), and the same principles ought to apply at every level: flat, low and broad taxes, with no wealth tax, no graduated rates, no retroactive taxes, and moderate local property taxes tied to core services. A tax base should treat residents alike. If I were voting:
- California: **no** on Proposition 3 (it makes the higher top income-tax rates permanent) and **no** on Proposition 40 (a one-time tax of up to 5% on covered assets over \$1 billion, mostly for health care). SEIU-UHW is a primary backer; calling the measure leverage or "blackmail" is opinion, but a union-backed wealth tax aimed at Medi-Cal funding is still a wealth tax, which I reject at every level. A Stanford/NBER analysis finds many billionaires would leave and that the net present value is often negative if the measure passes. **Yes** on Proposition 41 (audits of special-tax programs) and **yes** on Proposition 42 (it bars new taxes on owning financial assets and limits retroactive taxes). **No** on Proposition 43 (it requires two-thirds voter approval for voter-proposed local special taxes, while I hold that measures pass by a majority).
- Colorado: **no** on Amendment 87 (graduated income tax) and **yes** on Proposition 136 (a 4.4% cap on income-tax rates).
- Washington: **yes** on IP26-645 (it repeals the 9.9% tax on income above \$1 million and bars state and local income taxes).
- Florida: **no** on Amendment 3 (a \$150,000 non-school homestead exemption in 2027 and \$250,000 in 2028). It narrows the base, shifts the load onto renters, businesses and second homes, and gives new residents a smaller exemption for their first years.


#### Rationale 
High and complex taxes distort work, saving, investment, and compliance. U.S. federal revenue as a share of the economy has remained relatively stable for decades even when top statutory rates changed dramatically; very high rates do not reliably produce proportionally higher revenue. Federal revenue ranged from 14.2% to 18.5% of GDP during the 1950s, according to OMB, when the top rate was 91% or higher, and CBO puts the 50-year average at 17.3% of GDP. That top rate reached few people. The 91% bracket of 1950 began at \$200,000 of income, about \$2 million today, and the top 0.1 percent paid an average effective income tax rate of about 21%. So the 1950s show high statutory rates, not high rates actually paid. Flat-tax reforms in other countries have shown that simplification is workable and need not collapse collections. An IMF review of flat-tax adoptions found that personal income tax revenue rose in some countries, such as Latvia, Lithuania, and Russia, and fell in others, with total revenue effects that varied by country.

A large zero bracket increases take-home pay for lower- and middle-income households and supports private spending. A short linear phase-in removes the sharp notch that would otherwise encourage people to hold income just below the threshold. Lower marginal rates above the phase-in reduce disincentives to work and invest, though the measured gains from flat-tax adoptions abroad come mainly from simplicity and compliance, not faster growth. Reviews find no evidence that the rate cuts paid for themselves, and in Russia's 2001 reform the rise in income tax revenue came from better compliance rather than more work. Neither a sales tax nor a VAT is used, so no new consumption tax can grow quietly. Corporate and payroll taxes should not serve as primary redistribution tools. Property tax is retained at the local level because it is a practical and accountable way to fund local order and infrastructure; eliminating it would require less accountable replacements. Estate taxes confiscate property that has already been taxed.

No study measures whether any incentive reduces outsourcing itself. The evidence instead measures domestic employment and investment, and the two broad tools with measured gains in U.S. jobs are across-the-board payroll tax cuts and full expensing. The government ought to apply full expensing now and treat payroll tax cuts as a long-run goal as the transition levy steps down. In Norway, the abolition of lower regional payroll tax rates showed that each 1-percentage-point payroll tax increase cut local employment by 1.37% while wages fell only 0.32%. So lower payroll taxes support employment where wages do not fully adjust. A Swedish payroll tax cut for young workers raised their employment by 2–3 percentage points with no change in net wages. Bonus depreciation raised eligible investment by 10.4% (2001–04) and 16.9% (2008–10), and areas more exposed to it gained employment at roughly \$20,000–\$53,000 of forgone revenue per job, though earnings per worker did not rise. Targeted hiring credits, Opportunity Zones, domestic-content procurement rules, and firm-specific deals show weaker, short-lived, or far more costly results, often above \$110,000 per job.

This structure is sustainable only alongside spending restraint. It can support strong defense, core public goods, and a minimal safety floor. It cannot support the present long-term trajectory of major entitlement programs without reforming those programs. The tax rules and the commitment to a smaller federal government are designed to fit together.

## Fiscal Policy

>[!tldr] In short
> I believe we should balance the budget, minimize deficits, and pay down and eventually eliminate the national debt. Defense stays protected; committed cuts come first; new entrants go on reformed tracks; grandfathered SS/Medicare is kept. A capped transition levy plus a 2-point surcharge (ends 2041) fund promises and debt, and a debt brake auto-corrects. Sanctuary bonds are the one stated deficit exception, repaid by the Sanctuary Levy ([[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]). The flat tax alone does not balance today's budget - spending must fall to fit it. Numbers: Data callout (my static model, not official scores).

#### Position
A balanced budget is the proper target. Deficit spending is minimized; the one stated exception is sanctuary bonds, which cover the early years of sanctuary care that liquidation proceeds do not and are repaid in full by the Sanctuary Levy ([[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]). Long-term policy aims at reducing and eventually eliminating the national debt. Owner income from federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum goes first to the earnings-only top-up, and any remainder goes automatically to debt reduction ([[#Economic System]]). Defense is the top spending priority; reductions come from other areas.

The paydown order is committed cuts first, then the 30% nondefense cut, reformed tracks for new entrants with grandfathered SS/Medicare kept, CBO-scored Medicare savings, transition levy + surcharge, and the debt brake — scored in the Data callout. The aim is first to stop the debt growing faster than the economy, then to reduce it in dollars.

> [!question]- "Is eliminating the national debt realistic?"
> It is a long-term aim, not an immediate demand. Balanced budgets stop the debt from growing, and sustained restraint reduces it over time. The direction matters as much as the timeline.

> [!question]- "Does the flat tax raise enough to balance the budget?"
> Not on its own. Applied to IRS returns for 2023 and grown to 2026, my static estimate puts the 20% flat tax at about \$1.9 trillion a year, or about 6.0% of GDP. That is roughly 71% of today's individual income tax. Heads of household use the single line, and capital gains and dividends are taxed at their permanent 15% rate; the 2-point surcharge through 2041 is counted separately below. The range is about 5.6–6.3% of GDP depending on income growth and capital-income treatment. That is why spending has to fall and why the transition levy exists. IRS SOI – [Table 1.2, Tax Year 2023](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-soi/23in12ms.xls); IRS SOI – [Table 1.4, Tax Year 2023](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-soi/23in14ar.xls)

> [!question]- "Does the flat tax at least cover defense and interest?"
> Only just, on my estimate. For fiscal 2026, CBO projects defense outlays of \$885 billion (2.8% of GDP) and net interest of \$1,039 billion (3.3%), together \$1.92 trillion, or 6.1% of GDP. My static estimate of the 20% flat tax is about \$1.9 trillion, or 6.0% of GDP, within a range of 5.6–6.3%. So the income tax roughly pays for defense and interest and nothing else, and at the low end of the range it falls about \$0.15 trillion short.

> The rest closes through measures already on this page, set out in the answers below. The transition levy carries Social Security and Medicare for the protected generation, so they do not draw on the income tax. The committed cuts end SNAP, farm payments, and the Department of Education, and wind non-emergency Medicaid down to a residual. Other nondefense spending falls 30% over three years; applied to CBO's \$996 billion, that is about \$300 billion a year once fully phased in. Interest is the item that shrinks only as the debt does, which is why the surcharge, the levy floor, and the debt brake all go to the debt first. > [!question]- "Who pays for the benefits already promised?"
> The transition levy, which replaces today's payroll taxes. It is a temporary tax that pays off existing promises. In 2026 Social Security costs about \$1.67 trillion and Medicare about \$1.06 trillion. The levy pays only for people who are protected and for credits earned before the change. It applies to all wages because 17.4% of earnings sit above today's Social Security cap, and a wider base lets the rate stay lower for everyone under it. Nobody earns new benefits from it, and nobody is enrolled in anything by paying it: it is a debt to the people already promised, and it shrinks as they pass. SSA – [2026 COLA Fact Sheet](https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/cola/factsheets/2026.html); SSA – [Fast Facts & Figures 2025](https://www.ssa.gov/policy/docs/chartbooks/fast_facts/2025/fast_facts25.html)

> [!question]- "Why does the levy stay at 12% after the protected costs fall?"
> Because the debt still has to be paid. On my model, letting the levy fall as soon as the protected costs fall leaves deficits in the 2050s. Holding it at 12% until debt is below 60% of GDP, then stepping it down half a point a year, balances the budget in 2050 and keeps it in surplus after. The levy is never higher than in year one.

> [!question]- "Where do the savings come from?"
> Mostly from programs that end or shrink: non-emergency Medicaid, SNAP, farm payments, the Department of Education, and a 30% cut to other nondefense spending phased over three years. Spending lags appropriations: CBO estimates a one-third cut to transportation and education grants saves only \$13 billion in its first year against \$41 billion of funding cut. Medicare savings are counted only where CBO has scored them. Paying hospital outpatient departments site-neutral rates saves \$156.9 billion over 2025–2034. CBO – [Reduce Selected Nondefense Discretionary Spending](https://www.cbo.gov/budget-options/60934); CBO – [Site-neutral payments](https://www.cbo.gov/budget-options/60908); CBO – [Options for Reducing the Deficit: 2025 to 2034](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2024-12/60557-budget-options.pdf)

> [!question]- "What stops the plan from drifting off course?"
> A debt brake. Each year debt is compared with the legislated path, leaving out sanctuary bonds while the Sanctuary Levy repays them on schedule. For each point of overshoot, the next year's income surcharge rises half a point, up to two points. The brake is suspended in a recession. If it stays on for three years in a row, Congress must pass spending cuts to close the gap, and the surcharge then ends. Surpluses, owner income left after the top-up, and Federal Reserve remittances go straight to the debt.

> [!question]- "Does anyone pay more than today?"
> Not in any household I tested, from the first year on (see the table below). The closest case is a self-employed worker earning \$70,000, who pays about \$77 less in the early years, because today's tax code gives them a 20% business-income deduction.

> [!question]- "What could go wrong?"
> The biggest risks are the flat tax raising less than estimated, interest rates rising, and removals costing more than assumed. I assume about \$15 billion a year above current law, which is about 876,000 removals at ICE's own \$17,121 average. Penn Wharton's estimate of \$70,236 per removal would make that cost about four times higher. ## Monetary Policy

>[!tldr] In short
> Balanced budgets keep money stable. My goal is price stability, and I treat inflation as a hidden tax. The Fed should be reformed, not abolished: one price-stability mandate under a rule, no deficit finance, lending only to solvent banks, no bailouts, a full audit, legal competing currencies, and any profits to debt reduction.

#### Position
Stable money follows from fiscal restraint. With balanced budgets and debt reduction, the government does not rely on the central bank to finance deficits, and monetary policy aims at price stability. Inflation acts as an unlegislated tax on savings and wages, so preventing it is part of keeping the tax burden visible and matched to essential functions. The Federal Reserve is reformed, not abolished. It keeps a single mandate, price stability under a published rule, and the employment mandate is dropped. It does not finance deficits, and it does not run asset purchases that work as deficit finance. As lender of last resort it lends only to solvent banks, against good collateral, at a penalty rate, and it does not bail out insolvent firms, whose losses would otherwise fall on taxpayers who never consented. It is fully audited in public. Competing private currencies remain legal. Any profits it remits to the Treasury go to debt reduction. Fed governors should be removable only for cause, such as misconduct or breaking the price-stability rule, proven with notice, the evidence and a hearing, and never for disagreeing with the President on interest rates. A Fed bound by one published rule needs no political steering, and that insulation keeps it from being pushed to finance deficits.

> [!question]- "Isn't some inflation needed to manage the economy?"
> Price stability is the aim. Balanced budgets remove the main pressure for inflationary finance, and stable prices let people plan, save, and invest.

> [!question]- "Why not abolish the Fed?"
> Before the Fed, money held its value over the long run but swung sharply, with frequent deflation and recurring bank panics in 1873, 1893, and 1907 that destroyed depositors' savings. The Fed's worst failure, the 1929 to 1933 collapse, came from its own discretion, as Ben Bernanke acknowledged in 2002. The lesson I draw is to limit discretion, not to remove the institution: a single price-stability rule, no deficit finance, and lending only to solvent banks. A return to gold is not favored, since countries that left the gold standard recovered from the Depression sooner.


#### Rationale
Consumer prices rose 9.1% over the year ended June 2022, the largest 12-month increase since November 1981, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Balanced budgets are set out in [[#Fiscal Policy]]. By the Minneapolis Fed's consumer price index, the standard deviation of annual inflation was about 5.7 points in 1801 to 1912 and about 1.5 points in 1984 to 2025. But prices rose about 32-fold from 1913 to 2025, so a 1913 dollar buys about 3 percent of what it did. NBER business-cycle dates show average contractions of 21.6 months in 1854 to 1919 and 10.3 months in 1945 to 2020, though Christina Romer found that older data overstate pre-1914 volatility. The Federal Reserve Act sets three goals: maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates. The Fed remitted \$107.4 billion to the Treasury for 2021; since then its interest costs have exceeded its income, and its deferred asset stood at \$243.5 billion at the end of 2025.

## Public-Sector Size and Civil Service

>[!tldr] In short
> Government should be sized to its essential functions, with defense first. Work private firms can do ought to be contracted out, and I would abolish agencies without an essential function. Career staff get severance or placement help; political appointees' roles end with the office.

#### Position
The size of government follows from its functions. Spending and staffing are matched to essential functions, with defense the top priority, and functions that private firms can perform are contracted out, as government research already is. Agencies with no essential function, such as the federal Department of Education, are abolished. When an agency is abolished, career staff who built their lives around these jobs deserve severance or placement help: that is the commitment side, honoring people who followed the rules. Political appointees are different; their roles end with the office, and there is no transition obligation to them.

> [!question]- "Doesn't contracting out reduce accountability?"
> Contracts set measurable deliverables and can be ended. A contractor that fails loses the work, which is a stronger check than the near-permanence of a public office.


#### Rationale
In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. The federal civilian workforce was about 2.3 million on September 30, 2024, according to the Office of Personnel Management. Government R&D through private contracts is set out in [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Intellectual Property and Innovation|Bioethics, Progress, & Technology]].

### Public Funding of NGOs

>[!tldr] In short
> I would defund NGOs that run on public money and end grants to nongovernmental organizations. A nonprofit should be paid only under a contract for an essential public service, with measurable deliverables, verified results and the right to end it for failure. No public money should pay for advocacy, lobbying, litigation or political campaigns, and no nonprofit is paid to pass public money on to others.

#### Position
The government ought to defund NGOs that run on public money. A nonprofit that lives on taxpayer grants is a government program with the accountability taken out: it spends public money without answering to voters and without the deliverables a contract would impose. The United States ought to end every federal grant to nongovernmental organizations, at home and abroad, and the states ought to do the same.

The rule has four parts. (1) No grants: no grant, cooperative agreement or other transfer without deliverables goes to a nongovernmental organization. (2) Contracts only: where a nonprofit performs an essential public function, it is paid under a contract that names the service, sets measurable deliverables, checks them independently, pays only for what was delivered, and can be ended for failure, as with sanctuary operators ([[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]); where the work is put out to bid, nonprofits and firms compete on the same terms. Money that follows a person to the provider they use, such as per-child school funding or the last-resort allowance paying for a placement, is payment for a service, not a grant. (3) No advocacy money: no public dollar, under a contract or otherwise, pays for lobbying, advocacy campaigns, litigation against the government, or political organizing. (4) No pass-through: no nonprofit is paid to hand public money on to other organizations; the agency pays the provider that does the work. Causes with no essential function can still be funded through opt-in, taxpayer-designated funds, as charitable foreign aid is ([[#Fiscal Policy]]). Tax exemption for charities is a separate question and is not changed here. ^ngo-funding

> [!question]- "Don't NGOs deliver services cheaper and better than government?"
> Supporters of NGO funding point to exactly that. If it is true, the contract rule costs them nothing: an NGO that really delivers more for less wins and keeps the contract on measured results, against public agencies and private firms alike. What ends is money handed over without competition or checked deliverables. Feeding Our Future, a Minnesota nonprofit that sponsored sites in the federal child nutrition program, went from receiving and disbursing about \$3.4 million in federal funds in 2019 to nearly \$200 million in 2021. It did so by opening more than 250 sites that filed fake meal counts, and it fraudulently obtained and disbursed more than \$240 million, collecting more than \$18 million in administrative fees for sponsoring them. Its founder was sentenced in May 2026 to 500 months in prison. A pass-through sponsor paid on self-reported claims is the model this rule ends.

> [!question]- "Won't cutting grants close charities that people depend on?"
> Critics cite the Urban Institute's finding that, in every state, 60 to 86 percent of the nonprofits that received government grants would have run at a loss without them from 2021 to 2023. That measures dependence, not value; Urban itself describes it as a measure of grant dependence, not a prediction. A charity that cannot cover its costs without taxpayers is already a public program, so where its service is essential it moves to a contract, and the rest compete for voluntary giving, which reached \$592.50 billion in 2024, and for opt-in funds. Grants end over the phase-out window, not overnight.

> [!question]- "Doesn't federal law already stop grants from paying for lobbying?"
> Only narrowly. 31 U.S.C. § 1352 bars a recipient of a federal grant, contract, loan or cooperative agreement from using those appropriated funds to pay anyone to influence federal officials or Congress on a federal award. It restricts only the federal money itself and only lobbying on awards, so a grant still frees an organization's own money for advocacy, campaigns and lawsuits. Ending grants and barring advocacy spending under contracts closes that gap.


#### Rationale
In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. The scale is large. The Urban Institute counts more than 80,000 501(c)(3) charities, about a third of those that electronically filed a full Form 990, reporting government grants each year from 2021 to 2023, worth \$304 billion in 2021, \$294 billion in 2022 and \$240 billion in 2023, more than double foundation giving. That total leaves out Medicare and Medicaid payments, so it understates public funding.

France shows how far the model can go. In 2024 the French state made 114,778 payments to associations worth €9.5 billion. Subsidies were €7.3 billion of it, 76.7%, while payment for services was €923 million, 9.7%. The largest subsidy streams ran through school education (€1,162 million), territorial cohesion (€983 million), solidarity and equal opportunity (€973 million), special social and pension schemes (€875 million) and labor and employment (€797 million).

Accountability runs through elected officials ([[#Civil Service Protections]]). A grant moves public money beyond that line; a contract keeps it there, because the agency that signs it answers for the results and can end it. Paying NGOs to lobby, campaign or sue also makes every taxpayer fund political speech they may oppose, the same coercion I reject in mandatory redistribution ([[#Welfare and Entitlements]]).

### Civil Service Protections

>[!tldr] In short
> Hiring should be by merit, and policy-setting jobs ought to answer to elected officials. Career staff cannot be fired for politics, but I want removal for poor performance or misconduct to be fast: notice, a 30-day decision, and one appeal decided within 90 days.

#### Position
Federal hiring is by merit, through open competition and tested qualifications, never by party or loyalty. Positions that set or direct policy serve at the pleasure of elected officials, so voters can hold the people they elect responsible for what the government does. Career staff in non-policy roles keep one firm protection: no one is hired, fired, or punished for party, political speech, or refusing an unlawful order. Removal for poor performance or misconduct is fast and fair: written notice of the specific reasons, a chance to answer, a decision within 30 days, and one appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board decided within 90 days. The probationary period is two years, with an affirmative decision required before it ends.

> [!question]- "Won't weakening protections bring back the spoils system?"
> That is the strongest objection. Protections were built after the spoils era because jobs handed out for loyalty produced corruption and incompetence, and a workforce that can be fired at will can be pressured to serve a party rather than the law. The government ought to keep the part of the old settlement that answers this: merit hiring, and a ban on firing or punishing anyone for party, political speech, or refusing an unlawful order, enforceable in court. What it removes is delay. Reclassifying policy roles is legitimate only for positions that genuinely set policy; using it to reach ordinary career staff would be the spoils system by another name.

> [!question]- "Is it really that hard to fire a federal employee?"
> It is slow. The Government Accountability Office found that removing a poor-performing permanent employee can take six months to a year or longer, and that an appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board averaged 243 days in 2013. Agencies dismissed about 3,500 employees for performance or conduct that year, most during the probationary period, when appeal rights are limited. The delay discourages managers from acting at all.


#### Rationale
Accountability runs through elected officials, so those who set policy answer to them ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Public-Sector Size and Civil Service|Public-Sector Size and Civil Service]]). Due process still applies to every removal ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Due Process and Rule of Law]]), but process that takes a year protects poor performance more than it protects fairness. Keeping a hard line against political firing protects both the employee and the public from a workforce bent to party ends.

## Welfare and Entitlements

>[!tldr] In short
> I would phase out existing benefits for people who followed the rules, with grandfathered Social Security and Medicare for life. Welfare should be cut to a bare-minimum floor for basic needs, with no compelled redistribution beyond that floor and other social programs opt-in. I support an earnings-only tax-return top-up: 10% of earnings up to a \$15,000 peak (at most \$1,500 per worker); a per-child add-on of up to \$1,000 per child for up to three children for all working parents, paid only on earnings; paid per worker, phased out on household income from \$15,000 to \$42,000 (married: \$30,000 to \$84,000); legal residents and lawfully present non-citizens who pay taxes receive it on the same terms as citizens. ^topup-params
> For people who cannot work, the order is private disability default → charity → last-resort means-tested allowance (\$575/\$900 + local rent). Franchise fails under the net-taxpayer bar ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#^net-fiscal-vote|net fiscal voting bar]]).

#### Position
People who followed the rules and are on benefits should not be punished when the system changes to something better, so there is a phase-out period that honors existing commitments. The obligation is to the person, not to the old funding stream: a person's status, meaning their entitlement, is honored through the transition, while the funding sources behind those benefits may change.

Failing the net-taxpayer test suspends the franchise. A person whose personally directed transfers — cash, food, housing, or non-emergency medical support funded by general taxation, including the last-resort allowance, the earnings-only top-up, and refundable credit cash-outs (only the amount paid when liability would otherwise be ≤ \$0) — meet or exceed the taxes they pay, or whose pre-exemption income-tax liability is zero, cannot vote on the taxes and programs that move that money — a massive conflict of interest ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Voting and Elections|Voting and Elections]] · [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#^net-fiscal-vote|net fiscal voting bar]]). Non-refundable relief, including the [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#^family-credits|mothers' income-tax exemption]], is not received, and that exemption does not by itself fail the positive-liability gate when pre-exemption liability is positive. Grandfathered Social Security and Medicare, unemployment insurance, veterans' benefits, private insurance, charity, mutual aid, emergency shelter, emergency medical care, K-12, roads, defense, and other shared public goods do not count on the received side. Excluding grandfathered Social Security and Medicare from the received side does not waive the positive-liability gate: an SS-only retiree with \$0 pre-exemption income-tax liability cannot vote until a later year shows positive liability. The benefit remains available; failing the net-taxpayer test suspends the vote until a completed tax year shows otherwise. Joint filers share one status: a stay-at-home spouse on a joint return that is a net taxpayer with positive liability keeps the vote.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - United States, 1 Oct 2026: under the 2025 reconciliation law, refugees, asylees and many other lawfully present immigrants without green cards became ineligible for federally funded Medicaid and CHIP (emergency Medicaid remains), with ACA subsidy limits following on 1 Jan 2027. KFF – [Refugees and Asylees: Recent Changes in Access to Health Coverage and Other Assistance](https://www.kff.org/immigrant-health/refugees-and-asylees-recent-changes-in-access-to-health-coverage-and-other-assistance/)

Existing welfare and entitlement programs are aggressively reduced to bare-minimum safety nets. Mandatory redistribution beyond a bare-minimum floor is rejected; that floor is funded by minimal necessary taxes as set out in Core Principles, and an opt-in funding model for remaining social programs is the preferred long-term structure. Benefits, when provided, cover only basic necessities and do not support comfort or lifestyle expansion. Privatization and structural reform of Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, and similar programs are pursued where feasible.

The bare-minimum floor has two income parts — the earnings-only top-up and the last-resort allowance for people who cannot work — each a narrow, named exception to the rule against compelled redistribution, alongside emergency shelter, emergency medical care, and the K-12 backstop. Layers run private disability → charity → last resort. The last-resort allowance is \$575 a month (single) or \$900 (couple) for non-housing needs, plus local HUD fair market rent (studio or one-bedroom), reduced when housing is shared; non-housing indexed to inflation, housing to HUD's annual update. Other income reduces the allowance dollar for dollar; the first \$100/month of earnings is ignored, then 50¢ per further dollar; in-kind charity does not reduce it. Asset test: home and one car exempt, plus \$10,000 savings (single) or \$15,000 (couple). Recipients are auto-enrolled in the opt-in public health plan under the same exception; Part B premium paid for grandfathered Medicare members; long-term care in kind with medical certification (home care preferred where cheaper; capped pay for family caregivers). A narrow allowance of up to \$575 a month covers severely disabled children for disability-related costs only. Intake is by referral from shelters, hospitals, police, or charities, or by application at any shelter; assessment within days by a licensed, neutral clinician; placement in the least costly safe setting with private or charity providers; shelter or hospital until placement; court-appointed guardian/advocate (preferably volunteer or charity) when needed. Non-citizens are ineligible for the last-resort allowance ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Removal of Non-Citizen Residents and Loss of Citizenship|Removal of Non-Citizen Residents]]). Parameters and dollar amounts are also answered in the Q&As.

> [!question]- "Would cutting entitlements abandon people who paid into the system?"
> No. People who followed the rules and are on benefits are not punished when the system changes; there is a phase-out period that honors existing commitments. The person's entitlement is honored through the transition, even as the funding behind it shifts. Privatization and restructuring are pursued where feasible, and a bare-minimum safety net covering basic necessities remains. What is rejected is mandatory redistribution beyond that floor.

> [!question]- "Doesn't 'food insecurity' mean people are starving?"
> No. USDA "food insecurity" is a survey measure of constrained or anxious access to food, not chronic starvation. NHANES patterns show food-insecure adults in the United States, especially women, have higher obesity rates than food-secure adults, not lower body weight. The bare-minimum floor covers basic food need; it is not sized as if the typical insecure household were wasting away. Nature Scientific Reports – [food insecurity and obesity, NHANES-based analyses](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-74108-x)

> [!question]- "What happens if opt-in funding falls short?"
> The program scales to what its contributors fund; it does not fall back on general taxation. The bare-minimum safety floor for basic necessities is funded separately by minimal necessary taxes, so a shortfall in an opt-in program never removes that floor.

> [!question]- "Isn't the top-up redistribution?"
> Yes. It is funded partly from owner income and otherwise from general revenue, so it is a narrow, named exception to the rule against compelled redistribution, like emergency shelter, emergency medical care, and the K-12 backstop. It is limited in three ways: it is paid only on earned income, it ends at the zero-bracket line, and it replaces the minimum wage and separate wage-subsidy credits rather than adding to them. Redistribution beyond the named exceptions remains rejected.

> [!question]- "Why does the top-up include children?"
> A worker supporting children has more to cover on the same pay, and the strongest evidence for earnings top-ups comes from working parents. The add-on is modest and paid only on earnings, so it rewards work rather than having children. It goes to all working parents, married or single, with no marriage penalty, and it stops at three children.

> [!question]- "Why per worker but phased out by household?"
> Paying each worker keeps the top-up tied to that person's own work. Phasing it out on household income keeps it for households that are actually low-income: a teenager or second earner in a well-off household gets nothing. Because the married range is double the single range, a couple neither gains nor loses by marrying, splitting, or combining households.

> [!question]- "What about people who can't work?"
> They are covered in layers. Employers enroll workers by default in private disability insurance, and workers may opt out; the self-employed and people between jobs get the same choice through their tax return. The insurance is private; the state does not run it. Those within the grandfathered Social Security system keep that protection. People who can work some draw the earnings-only top-up on what they earn. Charity and mutual aid, including respite for family caregivers, come next. As a last resort, people with severe disabilities and the elderly poor receive a strictly means-tested allowance from general revenue, under the same backstop logic as grandfathered Social Security. It is checked by fraud enforcement and periodic medical reviews, and it is a narrow exception, not a general welfare entitlement.

> [!question]- "How much does the last-resort allowance cost?"
> In 2025, SSI paid \$64.85 billion to about 7.4 million people. Restricted to severe disability and the elderly poor, the allowance's cash cost is estimated to stay close to that, because the higher standard is offset by the narrower eligibility. Health coverage for recipients is the larger cost, but it replaces Medicaid spending for the same people rather than adding to it. These are estimates, not official scores.

> [!question]- "Won't a higher allowance invite fraud?"
> It is checked by income and asset verification, fraud enforcement, and periodic medical reviews. Only severe disability qualifies, and fraud is punished as theft. Income from any source reduces the allowance, so there is nothing to gain from hiding behind it while working.

> [!question]- "Why not one flat national amount?"
> Rent is the largest cost and varies more than anything else. A studio in Los Angeles has a fair market rent of \$2,079 a month, far above what one flat amount could cover without overpaying everywhere else. Tying the housing part to local rent pays what basic housing actually costs where the person lives, no more.

> [!question]- "Why does the allowance include health coverage?"
> Severe disability and frailty mean ongoing care, not just emergencies. Emergency-only care would leave the people least able to provide for themselves without treatment for chronic conditions, which is often more expensive when it ends in the emergency room. Covering them inside the same narrow exception keeps everyone else's healthcare opt-in.

> [!question]- "Why are severely disabled children covered when parents are responsible for their children?"
> Parents remain responsible for the ordinary costs of raising a child. A severe disability can add costs far beyond what any ordinary family budget can carry. The child allowance, up to \$575 a month, covers only those extra costs, in severe cases, so that parents can keep caring for the child at home.

> [!question]- "What happens to an elderly person with no home and no family who needs care?"
> A shelter, hospital, police officer, or charity refers them to the allowance office, or they apply at any shelter. Within a few days a licensed, neutral clinician assesses them, and once certified they are placed in the least costly setting that safely meets their needs, such as a studio with home care, assisted living, or a nursing home, run by private or charity providers. Until then they stay in shelter, or in hospital rather than being discharged to the street. If they cannot manage their own affairs, a court appoints a guardian or advocate, preferably a volunteer or a charity.


#### Rationale

In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. Mandatory redistribution takes from some citizens to fund others, which I treat as coercion beyond the state's core role. Major entitlement programs also face long-term funding shortfalls under current law, so structural reform is needed regardless. The 2025 Trustees Reports project Social Security's combined trust funds to be depleted in 2034, with 81% of scheduled benefits then payable, and Medicare's Hospital Insurance trust fund in 2033. An opt-in model lets those who wish to fund social programs above the floor do so, while the minimal floor for basic necessities stays funded by minimal necessary taxes. Voluntary giving alone could not yet carry the floor: SNAP benefits in FY2024 (\$92.9 billion) exceeded all U.S. charitable giving to human services in 2024 (\$91.15 billion).

An earnings-only top-up is the tool with the strongest evidence for low earners. Hoynes and Patel found that a \$1,000 increase in the Earned Income Tax Credit raised single mothers' employment by 7.3 percentage points and cut the share of their families below the poverty line by 9.4 points. Paying it only on earned income matters: in the Seattle-Denver income maintenance experiment, which guaranteed income whether or not people worked, husbands cut their hours by about 9 percent and wives by about 20 percent. Delivering the top-up through the flat tax replaces the EITC's separate rules, which produce large errors. The Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration reported that 27.3 percent of EITC payments in FY2024 (\$15.9 billion of \$58.4 billion) were improper. One return with one phase-out leaves fewer rules to get wrong, though improper payments will not vanish and must still be policed. The top-up is far smaller than the EITC it replaces: the EITC paid about \$69.6 billion to about 24 million claimants for tax year 2024, with a 2026 maximum of \$7,316 for two children. I modelled the top-up on the Census Bureau's 2025 Current Population Survey microdata, applying the rules exactly as set out here to every household, with 2024 incomes carried forward to 2026 by wage and price growth. On my middle case it costs about \$26 billion a year, about \$17.5 billion for the base and \$8 billion for the child add-on, paid to about 20 million households. Granted, survey data understate what the IRS pays out: the same data and method put the 2024 EITC at about \$45 billion, against the \$69.6 billion the IRS paid. My middle case scales the top-up up by the same ratio as the EITC's proper payments, the IRS total less the 27.3 percent paid improperly, bear to the survey figure, and it allows a small earnings response to the top-up's own rates. If the top-up instead runs over its survey estimate by the full EITC gap, improper claims included, and earnings respond more strongly, it costs about \$36 billion. If the survey is right and only 85% of those eligible claim it, slightly more than the 81% who claim the EITC, it costs about \$19 billion. The model counts every worker because the survey cannot separate workers without legal status, who would not receive it; about 9.7 million of them were in the workforce in 2023, so these figures overstate the cost by about \$2 billion. Even at the high end it costs about half the EITC it replaces, which runs about \$70 billion a year by 2026, so the replacement saves about \$44 billion a year on my middle case (\$34 billion to \$51 billion). This is my own estimate, not an official score. Granted, by the same evidence a smaller credit gives weaker work incentives: the top-up pays a single parent of two at most \$3,500, against \$7,316 under the EITC. I accept that trade: the top-up keeps support tied to work and ends at the zero-bracket line, at a third to half the cost. The phase-out takes at most about 16.7 cents of each extra dollar, for a single parent of three, against 21.06 cents under the EITC for families with two or more children. The \$1,000 add-on sits below the \$1,700 refundable portion of the 2026 child tax credit (maximum \$2,200 per child), which the flat tax also replaces. An amount above about \$1,395 per child would push a single parent of three past the EITC's 21.06 percent. ^topup-cost

In August 2026, 7.3 million people received Supplemental Security Income, averaging \$738.73 a month, and 7.0 million disabled workers received earned Social Security Disability Insurance benefits averaging \$1,635.90. The need is real: in 2023, 24.2 percent of working-age adults with disabilities living in the community were in poverty, against 9.9 percent of those without. Means-tested programs are also error-prone: SSI's improper-payment rate rose from 9.41 percent in FY2019 to 10.62 percent (about \$6.5 billion) in FY2023, which is why the last-resort allowance carries fraud enforcement and periodic medical review. Work-focused design shrinks the group who cannot work at all. After the Netherlands required employers to attempt rehabilitation before a disability claim (2002) and replaced its disability law with one built around remaining work capacity (2006), new disability claims fell by about 25 and 21 percentage points respectively, and the caseload by more than 60 percent. Voluntary institutions once carried much of this load: by David Beito's conservative estimate, one in three adult American men belonged to a fraternal society in 1920.

The current federal SSI maximum is \$994 a month for an individual and \$1,491 for a couple in 2026. In December 2025, the average federal SSI payment was \$688.47, and 7.39 million people received payments, 1.20 million aged and 6.13 million disabled, of whom 1.02 million were children. That is well below what basic needs cost. The 2026 poverty guideline for one person is \$15,960 a year, about \$1,330 a month. The USDA's Thrifty Food Plan alone costs \$236.30 to \$281.20 a month for an adult aged 51 to 70. HUD's fair market rent for a Los Angeles studio is \$2,079. Health and care needs are larger still. Medicaid spent \$27,361 per disabled enrollee and \$20,305 per aged enrollee in FY2023. A semi-private nursing home room costs a national median of \$9,581 a month, against \$6,673 for a home caregiver 44 hours a week, which is why home care is preferred where it costs less. The Medicare Part B premium is \$202.90 a month in 2026.

> [!info]- What is an opt-in funding model?
> An opt-in model funds a program through voluntary contributions from those who choose to support it, rather than through mandatory taxes on everyone. A voluntary checkoff, where a taxpayer chooses to direct a contribution to a program, counts as opt-in.

## Social Security and Medicare Transition

>[!tldr] In short
> Social Security and Medicare should become voluntary for new benefits: nobody is forced into the new system. A temporary transition levy, which replaces today's payroll taxes, pays off the benefits already promised and ends when they end. I would protect current retirees, and people within ten years of full retirement age, before the new system comes in, as an honored commitment, and they keep their benefits for life. The old system fades out only as fewer people remain in it. The separate basic safety floor stays. Employers should sign workers up for private retirement savings by default, and anyone can opt out.

#### Position
Social Security and Medicare become opt-in only. Participation and contributions for new benefits are voluntary; no one is compelled to join either program or to rely on it, and those who opt out arrange their own retirement savings and coverage. The transition levy is a temporary tax that pays off existing promises; it buys no new benefits and ends when they end ([[#Taxation]]). Current retirees are protected before the new system comes in: their benefits are an existing commitment that is not a rights violation, and existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition to the new system. People within ten years of full retirement age get the same protection as current retirees, grandfathered on current benefit formulas, as part of the same honored commitment. Grandfathered people keep Social Security and Medicare for life. The old system phases out because fewer people remain in it, not because benefits end on a set date. This protection is part of the position, not a practical concession. The separate bare-minimum safety floor for basic necessities, defined in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]], is unaffected.

Employers enroll workers by default in a private retirement savings account, and workers may opt out. The self-employed make the same choice on their tax return. The state does not run or hold the accounts. I don't set the default contribution rate. One rule ties this to the disability-insurance default in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]]: defaults apply only where a person mainly protects themselves, as with disability insurance and retirement savings; anything that funds other people stays opt-in.

> [!note]- Realistic transition
> In a realistic world, many systems — like taxes, like education, like all the systems that would be changing — would have some gradual period of change for most of these changes to happen. That practical lag is a brief aside, not the main position.

> [!question]- "Doesn't moving to opt-in break the promise made to people who paid in?"
> Current retirees are protected before the new system comes in, because existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition; people who planned around the current system are not cut off, and the basic-necessities floor remains. Grandfathered people keep their benefits for life; the old system shrinks only as fewer people remain in it. The programs are also not financed for the long term under current law, so continuing them unchanged would itself break the promise of scheduled benefits.

> [!question]- "Who pays for the protected benefits, and what about people who paid in but are more than ten years from retirement?"
> Protected benefits are paid first by opt-in contributions and then by the transition levy, which replaces today's payroll taxes and ends when the protected benefits end. The government ought to pay for the rest of the transition with spending cuts, not new debt. The retirement age rises two years, phased in, for people not yet 67, and protected benefits for people now aged 57–66 are reduced 15% above a means-test threshold. That is the price of keeping everyone else's promise without new debt. People more than ten years from full retirement age keep a credit for the contributions they already paid, payable at retirement.

> [!question]- "Won't moving to opt-in push the elderly into poverty?"
> Critics warn that ending Social Security would push millions of older people into poverty, and the scale they point to is large. Social Security is the country's largest antipoverty program: in 2024 it moved 28.7 million people above the Census Bureau's Supplemental Poverty Measure line, 20.1 million of them aged 65 or older. Even with it, 15.0% of people 65 and older were poor under that measure in 2024; without it, the Census Bureau estimates their rate would have been 32.7 points higher, about 47.7%. Benefit payments that year were about \$1.47 trillion (\$1,316.4 billion retirement and survivors, \$155.0 billion disability), and the program costs about 5.3% of GDP in 2025. That is why the transition is built around the people at risk. Current retirees and everyone within ten years of full retirement age keep their benefits for life, so no one now old or nearly old loses income. Younger workers keep a credit for what they have already paid, and default enrollment in private accounts builds their own savings. The last-resort allowance remains for the elderly poor who still fall short. The price of that protection is that the benefits run for decades. The backstop must cover benefits on the order of today's payments at first, shrinking only as the grandfathered group shrinks, and the program is already short, with the combined trust funds projected to be depleted in 2034. For people who reach old age after the transition with too little saved, the allowance is thinner than a Social Security check by design: it is a bare-minimum floor, not a pension, and it still covers the elderly poor, because it is means-tested rather than earned. Any eligible elderly person below the line qualifies, whatever they paid in, and it pays for food, phone, transport, and personal needs plus local rent, rising each year with prices and rents ([[#Welfare and Entitlements|last-resort allowance]]).

> [!question]- "Isn't default enrollment a form of coercion?"
> No. Nobody is forced to save: opting out is free and simple, and the money stays in the worker's own private account. The default only changes what happens when a person does nothing. The evidence shows how much that matters. In Madrian and Shea's study of one large U.S. employer, 401(k) participation among new hires rose from 37% under opt-in to 86% under automatic enrollment. In Great Britain, where employers have enrolled workers automatically since 2012, 89% of eligible employees were saving into a workplace pension in 2024. More people with their own savings means fewer who end up relying on the last-resort allowance in old age.


#### Rationale
Compelled contributions to retirement and health programs are redistribution beyond the floor, which I reject. The transition levy is different: it is a temporary tax that pays off promises already made, buys no new benefits, and ends when those promises end. Existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition, which is why current retirees are protected before the new system comes in. The protection extends to people within ten years of full retirement age because the transition risk lands hardest on them. They have planned and paid in around the current system and have the least time left to build their own retirement savings and coverage. The programs also face shortfalls under current law. The 2025 Trustees Reports project Social Security's combined trust funds to be depleted in 2034, with 81% of scheduled benefits payable after that, and Medicare's Hospital Insurance trust fund in 2033. CBO projects Medicare spending to rise from 2.6% of GDP in 2025 to 5.2% in 2055. Structural reform is needed regardless; the government ought to set its direction toward voluntary participation. Default enrollment fills the gap that opt-in leaves, because inertia is strong: Madrian and Shea found that most workers hired under automatic enrollment also kept the default contribution rate and fund. That same finding is why I leave the default rate unset rather than picking a number the evidence does not single out; the rate will shape how much people save.

## Births and Family Formation

>[!tldr] In short
> America should get back above replacement, 2.1 children per woman. I would do it with support that grows with family size, cheaper housing through freed-up building, and a higher-trust culture that honors marriage and children (phone-free schools, childhood-independence laws, car-seat mandate review), not with mandates or a large welfare state.

#### Position
The U.S. fertility rate hit a record low of about 1.6 in 2024 and has been below replacement since 2008. Americans say the ideal family has 2.7 children, so most of the gap comes from obstacles, not from what people want. The government's job is to remove the obstacles it controls: taxes on parents, housing scarcity, and school costs. Culture and faith, which matter most, are carried by families, churches, and communities.

The fertility collapse is mostly a marriage collapse. Age-controlled marital fertility has held up or risen in some cells while marriage rates cratered; fewer and later marriages drive the drop (data below). Policy that only writes checks without making early pairing easier will underperform. Money is a lever, not the whole cause. I treat higher trust — built through assimilation, values-tested immigration, and national cohesion argued elsewhere — as a natalist prior, not a measured policy lever with a published effect size on this page.

The package, evidenced in the Data callouts: refundable child credit of \$1,500 for the first and second child and \$3,000 for the third and each later child; one-time refundable \$5,000 birth credit for third and later births; no federal income tax on a mother's earned income for life after her fourth child, half after her third, on up to \$30,000 of earnings a year; franchise carve for the family package — non-refundable relief not received, refundable cash-outs only when liability would otherwise be ≤ \$0, mothers' exemption does not alone zero the liability gate when pre-exemption liability > \$0, and SNAP / TANF / last-resort / top-up stay hard ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#^net-fiscal-vote|net fiscal voting bar]]). ^family-credits

By-right starter homes and duplexes, including family-size units (3+ bedroom apartments, single-stair buildings, no bedroom-count caps), and repeal of minimum lot sizes and parking minimums; childcare deregulation within safety limits, with home daycare by right; growth and rising earnings for young adults through the flat tax and job growth; no federal paid-leave mandate on employers; no marriage penalty anywhere in the tax code; IVF legal and unrestricted, with no insurance mandate and honest age-and-fertility information in existing health education; phone-free schools set by states and districts; Reasonable Childhood Independence laws on the Utah model; state review of car-seat age mandates; leaving-home, meeting-place, and career-path levers in the notes below. Culture, faith, and marriage are carried by families and churches, not the state. The United States ought not to use immigration to fill the gap; the nation should renew itself through its own families ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]).

> [!note]- Leaving home earlier
> I want young adults to be able to afford a place of their own in their early twenties, because people who move out tend to be the ones who marry and start families. The fix is supply, not subsidy: legalize small starter apartments, accessory dwelling units, shared and roommate housing, and by-right starter homes, so a first lease costs less.

>[!example]- Data: leaving home
>- In 2023, 57% of Americans 18–24 lived in a parent's home (53% in 1993), along with 21% of those 25–29 and 11% of those 30–34; far fewer young adults are married or have kids than in 1993. Pew Research Center – [Parents, Young Adult Children and the Transition to Adulthood (2024)](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2024/01/ST_2024.01.25_Parents-Young-Adults_Report.pdf)
>- Worsening rent- and price-to-income ratios explain up to a quarter of the 9-point rise in young adults living with parents from 2000 to 2021. Real Estate Economics – [Why do young adults coreside with their parents? (2024)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1540-6229.12467)
>- Moving out is strongly associated with marriage and childbearing, but a causal effect of living at home on family formation was not established. Jacinda Sze, University of Delaware – [The Dynamics of Co-Residence, Wealth, and Family Formation (2025)](https://lerner.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/sze-JMP.pdf)
>- Supply that only makes living alone cheaper does less for births than family-size housing; large-unit supply produced 2.3 times the births of equal-cost one-bedroom supply. Couillard – [Build, Baby, Build](https://ti.org/pdfs/BuildBabyBuild.pdf)
>- Evidence strength: moderate on housing costs leading to living at home; weak on living at home leading to fewer births.

> [!note]- Places to meet in person
> I want young people to meet each other face to face again. Government shouldn't run dating programs, but local rules shouldn't make it hard to open a café, bar, gym, club, or event space either. I support allowing mixed-use neighborhoods by right, simpler business and liquor licensing, and fewer permits for small venues and events.

>[!example]- Data: how couples meet

Rosenfeld et al., PNAS – [Disintermediating your friends (2019)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1908630116)

>- The drop in in-person social time is in the phones box above. Evidence strength: the decline in in-person meeting is well documented; no causal study was found showing that more venues raise marriage or births.

> [!note]- Shorter paths into careers
> I want young people to be able to start a career, and a family, without spending their twenties collecting credentials. I support dropping degree requirements in federal hiring wherever a degree isn't truly needed, recognizing apprenticeships and skills tests as equal paths, and trimming occupational licensing hours. The government ought not to mandate anything on private employers. Earlier starts give couples more time to reach the family size they want, and this is good policy on its own.

>[!example]- Data: careers and timing

>[!example]- Data: what the new measures cost (estimates)

>- \$3,000 credit for third and later children: about 1.05 million such births a year, over 18 years, is roughly 19 million children. The extra \$1,500 each costs about \$28 billion a year.

>- Zoning reform, no leave mandate, no marriage penalty, and legal IVF cost the budget nothing.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the net-taxpayer voting bar cancel the family credits?"
> No. The bar targets conflict of interest over other people's money, not parenthood. Non-refundable relief is not received. Refundable credits count only as cash-outs when liability would otherwise be ≤ \$0. The mothers' exemption does not alone fail the liability gate when pre-exemption liability is positive. A household that draws SNAP, the last-resort allowance, or the earnings-only top-up, or that takes large cash-outs with little tax paid, still fails. Working parents whose family package is tax reduction against positive liability keep the vote under [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#^net-fiscal-vote|net fiscal voting bar]].


#### Rationale
Americans still want 2.7 children and are having 1.6, so the problem is obstacles, not desire. The evidence points the same way across countries: cash raises births but costs a lot per extra child, the strongest response comes at the third child and later, and higher housing costs push births down. Spending on its own has failed in South Korea, Poland, and the Nordics. The countries that stay high, Israel and Georgia, do it through faith and a culture that prizes large families. So the state stays small: a low flat tax, support that grows with family size, cheaper housing through freed supply, and no mandates. The rest belongs to families and churches.

## Child Support

>[!tldr] In short
> I believe both genetic parents who consented to the sex owe support. Civil orders should come first; willful refusal after an order brings escalating penalties. A rape victim owes no duty from the rape; the rapist owes full support.

#### Position
Voluntary conception creates a support duty on both genetic parents who consented to the sex. Support is enforced by civil order first; willful refusal after an order is a rights violation, a fraud on the child's claim, with escalating penalties; any fines are [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Income-Scaled Fines|scaled to income and wealth]]. In cases of rape, the victim parent owes no duty created by the rape, and the rapist parent owes full support.

> [!question]- "Does a parent who was raped owe support?"
> No. Duties arise from voluntary responsibility, and rape is not voluntary for the victim. The rapist parent owes full support.


#### Rationale
Duties arise from voluntary responsibility, matching the voluntary-procreation responsibility in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Axioms|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]. A parent who consented to the sex took on the child's claim; refusing an order to meet it defrauds the child. Rape is not voluntary for the victim, so no duty arises for the victim from it. The U.S. Census Bureau reports that in 2022 about one in four children under 21 lived with only one parent while the other parent lived outside the household.

## Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness

>[!tldr] In short
> I'd leave housing mainly to property owners and the market. Basic zoning is allowed, but rules that wipe out property value must be paid for, and housing subsidies need voluntary funding. The safety floor should offer emergency shelter only, not housing, and charities and private groups are free to run Housing First; camping and drug use in public ought to be banned.

#### Position
Housing supply is governed by property rights and the market. Ordinary zoning that coordinates land use is allowed, but regulation that eliminates the economically beneficial use of property, or destroys most of its value, is a taking and requires compensation. Population growth is met through densification, development, and voluntary exchange, not political allocation of land. Housing subsidies require taxpayer opt-in. For homelessness, the safety floor provides emergency shelters only; it is not a housing program. The one exception is the rent component of the last-resort allowance for severe disability and the elderly poor ([[#Welfare and Entitlements]]). Charities and private groups are free to run Housing First programs, and opt-in funding can support them; the public rent component stays limited to the last-resort allowance. Camping on public property is not permitted, and public drug use is prohibited.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **Record homelessness, 2024:** HUD reported more than 770,000 people homeless on a single night in January 2024, an 18% increase from 2023. U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (2024) – [HUD Releases January 2024 Point-In-Time Count Report](https://archives.hud.gov/news/2024/pr24-327.cfm)

> [!question]- "If public camping is banned, where do homeless people go?"
> To emergency shelter, which the safety floor provides. The floor covers a basic necessity, not long-term housing; permanent housing comes from the market, opt-in programs, and private charity. Public spaces remain open for everyone's use rather than becoming encampments.

> [!question]- "Doesn't Housing First work better than shelters?"
> Supporters cite its housing results: a meta-analysis of randomized trials found Housing First participants about 2.46 times as likely to be stably housed, with fewer emergency-department visits and hospital stays, though no clear improvement in mental health or substance use. Results for its recipients do not make it a public duty. Housing First is a housing subsidy, so it is left to charities, private groups, and opt-in funding, which are free to run it. The public floor stays at emergency shelter, and the only public rent support is the last-resort allowance.


#### Rationale
Secure property and open land markets are how societies have historically absorbed population growth with rising living standards ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Property Rights|Authority & Civil Liberties]]). Homelessness has grown. HUD's 2024 point-in-time count found more than 770,000 people experiencing homelessness on a single night in January 2024, an 18% increase from 2023. HUD attributed part of the rise to steep rent increases and years of under-building, along with migration and natural disasters. Supply restrictions that hold back building bear directly on the problem. Randomized trials show Housing First keeps more people stably housed than treatment-first approaches, which is why the government ought to leave charities and opt-in programs free to run it while the public floor stays at shelter. The basic-necessities floor is set out in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]], and the rule on public drug use in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Drug Policy|Drug Policy]].

Blocking building is what pushes rents up, and a rent freeze adds a second shortage on top of the first: when rents are capped, owners take units off the rental market, so everyone still looking for a home faces fewer and costlier choices (data below). New York City has now frozen rents and is planning city-run grocery stores. As the commentator Brivael Le Pogam put it, a rent freeze is "the cure that worsens the disease." X – [Brivael Le Pogam, 17 Oct 2026](https://x.com/brivael/status/2111420678914085225)

## Government Home Lending

>[!tldr] In short
> The government should not use taxpayer money to make home loans cheaper. Federal lending and loan guarantees must cover their own costs, below-market rates need opt-in funding, and I would cut rules that only add closing costs. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac should leave conservatorship only once fully capitalized, as private companies with no federal backstop.

#### Position
I would prefer that federal home lending carry no hidden subsidy. Loans the government makes or guarantees ought to be priced to cover their own risk and costs, and any rate below that is a housing subsidy that requires taxpayer opt-in, like every other subsidy ([[#Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness|Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness]]). Federal requirements that add cost without protecting the borrower or the taxpayer, such as duplicate reviews, unnecessary appraisals and mandatory products that automated checks can replace, ought to be removed. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac ought to leave conservatorship only once they meet the full capital rule, and then as private companies with no federal guarantee, explicit or implied; until then their guarantee fees ought to cover the full risk they place on taxpayers.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the federal guarantee keep mortgages affordable?"
> Supporters argue that the backing behind Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac keeps the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage widely available at lower rates, and it does lower rates. The lower rate is paid for by risk that taxpayers carry without consent, which makes it a subsidy. The two companies are still in federal conservatorship, and at the end of the second quarter of 2026 their estimated capital ratios were about 2.84% (Fannie Mae) and 2.36% (Freddie Mac), below the Federal Housing Finance Agency's 4% leverage requirement and its 8% risk-based requirement. Releasing them before they meet that rule would leave taxpayers holding losses an undercapitalized company cannot absorb. Private lenders and securitizers can offer long fixed-rate loans priced to their real risk.

## Healthcare

>[!tldr] In short
> I want people to choose between a publicly supported plan and private insurance. No one should be required to buy coverage, but the public plan has one yearly sign-up window and a modest surcharge for joining late.

#### Position
Healthcare is organized around freedom of choice. Individuals may opt into publicly supported systems or purchase private coverage. Mandates are rejected. The voluntary public plan has one annual open-enrollment window, and people who join late pay a modest surcharge, modeled on Medicare Part B's 10 percent for each full year a person was eligible but not enrolled. No one is required to join or penalized for staying out; the surcharge applies only to those who choose to join later. Emergency care stays tax-funded, and people on the last-resort allowance are enrolled automatically.

> [!question]- "Without a mandate, will healthy people avoid coverage and drive up costs?"
> Critics warn of adverse selection, and Massachusetts shows the effect a mandate has: when it took effect, healthier people entered the individual market and average costs and premiums fell. The 2019 repeal of the federal penalty did not bring the collapse that warning predicts: some studies found no clear drop in coverage and others only a modest rise in people becoming uninsured. The government keeps choice and limits the problem without a mandate. The public plan has one annual enrollment window, and late joiners pay a modest surcharge, as in Medicare Part B, so people cannot wait until they are sick to sign up. No one is compelled to buy insurance.

> [!question]- "What about people who can't afford private coverage?"
> They can opt into a publicly supported system. Emergency medical care remains part of the safety floor, so no one in a genuine emergency is left without care ([[2. Political and Social Character#Economic Orientation|Economic Orientation]]). People on the last-resort allowance for severe disability or old-age poverty are enrolled in the public plan automatically, paid under that narrow exception ([[#Welfare and Entitlements]]).


#### Rationale
Compelling citizens to buy a product conflicts with my limits on state coercion. Offering both public and private options keeps access while preserving choice. Federal health spending is a major driver of long-term budget pressure, which supports keeping public systems opt-in. CBO projects major federal health spending to rise from 5.8% of GDP in 2025 to 8.1% in 2055, with Medicare rising from 2.6% to 5.2% of GDP. Without a mandate, a voluntary plan risks adverse selection: Hackmann, Kolstad, and Kowalski found that the Massachusetts mandate brought healthier people into the individual market and lowered average costs and premiums. After the federal penalty was repealed in 2019, one study comparing states that kept their own mandates found no significant change in coverage or marketplace enrollment. Medicare Part B shows a non-mandate tool. Its late-enrollment penalty adds 10% for each full 12-month period a person was eligible but not enrolled, usually for as long as they have Part B. The public plan borrows that design in modest form.

## Healthcare Funding, Emergency Care, and Drug Approval

>[!tldr] In short
> The public health option should be voluntary, with taxpayers funding it only by choice. I count emergency care among the basic services taxes pay for. Drug and insurance rules ought to exist only to protect safety and stop consumer abuse, and nobody should have to buy insurance.

#### Position
The publicly supported healthcare option is opt-in, and its funding follows the rule for all programs beyond core services: subsidies require taxpayer opt-in. Emergency services are among the core services funded by minimal necessary taxes. Drug and device regulation is legitimate for genuine safety and against anti-consumer practices, with the burden of proof resting on each regulation. Insurance regulation is limited to preventing fraud and anti-consumer practices. No one is required to buy coverage. Tax-funded research is limited to defense, biosecurity and pandemic defense, and the government's own core functions. Other medical and scientific research, including disease research, is funded by private money and taxpayer opt-in, under the same rules for every group, and research the government does fund must treat sex as a biological variable.

> [!question]- "What happens to people who have no coverage and need emergency care?"
> Emergency services are part of the core public functions funded by minimal necessary taxes. Current federal law also requires hospitals with emergency departments to screen anyone who arrives and to stabilize an emergency condition or transfer the patient appropriately, regardless of ability to pay. The government ought to keep emergency care within the safety floor while leaving routine coverage to individual choice.


#### Rationale
Most Americans already arrange coverage without a mandate: 92.0% had health insurance for some or all of 2024, with private coverage (66.1%) more common than public (35.5%). Federal health spending is the main driver of long-term budget pressure; CBO projects it to rise from 5.8% of GDP in 2025 to 8.1% in 2055. Keeping the public option opt-in prevents that pressure from becoming compulsory redistribution. The Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act (1986) requires a screening examination and stabilizing treatment or appropriate transfer in hospitals with emergency departments, without delay to ask about payment. Drug regulation follows the same burden-of-proof rule as all business regulation in [[#Infrastructure and Regulation]]. Federal law now permits non-animal alternatives in drug approval, consistent with [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Bioethics, Progress, & Technology]].

## Education

>[!tldr] In short
> I believe each state should fund basic K-12 education at about \$10,000 per child (more for disability as each state sets from need, with pre-K left out). The money follows the child to any public school, district or charter, statewide. Parents should pay for private and religious schools, and homeschooling families get a fully refundable \$1,000 tax credit per child. Schools ought to be run locally, with a curriculum that sticks to core skills and facts, not ideology. College should be paid for with personal loans, not federal ones, and state subsidies for colleges should end.

### K-12 Funding

>[!tldr] In short
> I'd have each state fund about \$10,000 per child per year for basic K-12 education from its general revenue, roughly what Utah and Idaho spend, with an extra amount for students with disabilities that each state sets from actual need. The money follows the child to any public school, district or charter, anywhere in the state. Private and religious schools stay legal but get no public money; parents pay for them. Homeschooling families get a \$1,000 tax credit per child per year. Pre-K is excluded.

#### Position
The \$10,000 per-child average is the level the evidence points to now — close to what Utah and Idaho spend — not a floor and not a slogan. It moves up or down only with outcome data; any change alters state tax calculations. Students with disabilities get an added amount each state sets from actual need; no fixed national figure. Publicly funded means publicly accountable: the money follows the child to any public school, district or charter, statewide. Private and religious schools stay legal with no public funds; parents pay. Homeschooling families get a fully refundable \$1,000 federal income-tax credit per child per year ([[#Homeschooling]]); pre-K is excluded. ^k12

**My view:** Since restricting immigration will lower the population, we want as many things as possible to help parents and raise birth rates to replacement level ([[#Taxation|Taxation]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]).

> [!question]- "Is \$10,000 per child enough?"
> Utah spent \$10,333 per pupil and Idaho \$10,247 in FY2023, against a national average of \$16,526, and both scored at or above the national results in 2024 NAEP eighth-grade math and reading (Utah 282 and 261, Idaho 278 and 261). New York spent \$30,012 and scored 271 and 257. Critics answer that the states' student populations differ, so raw averages are not a clean test. The comparison holds within the same groups, where the low spenders still match or beat New York: in 2024 eighth-grade math, white students scored 289 in Utah and 284 in Idaho against 283 in New York, and Hispanic students 260 and 256 against 253. Across OECD countries, higher spending tracks math scores only up to about \$75,000 per student over ages 6 to 15, and the United States spends more than \$150,000.

> [!question]- "Doesn't more money raise outcomes?"
> Researchers find gains on average: a review of U.S. school-spending evaluations finds that \$1,000 more per pupil, sustained for four years, raises test scores by about 0.03 standard deviations and college-going by about 2.8 percentage points, with smaller effects for advantaged students. That is \$4,000 per pupil for three-hundredths of a standard deviation, while Utah and Idaho match or beat far higher spenders near \$10,000. I hold an average of about \$10,000 per child as the level the evidence supports today. It is not fixed: if the data shows better outcomes from spending more, the level moves up, and if it shows the same outcomes from spending less, the level moves down. Any change in either direction alters the state tax calculations.

> [!question]- "If spending keeps rising and scores barely move, aren't schools just underfunded forever?"
> "Underfunded" without a fixed definition is a slogan, not a budget. Real per-pupil spending has risen for decades while NAEP gains have been small; from FY1992 to FY2024 staff grew far faster than enrollment, and non-teaching staff grew fastest (data in "Where did the extra money go?"). Cross-state spending and NAEP results show a weak relationship once poverty and income are accounted for; causal studies still find small average gains from sustained increases, on the order of a few hundredths of a standard deviation per \$1,000 per pupil over several years. I do not claim the correlation is literally zero. I claim the United States already spends past the point where more money, without better use, reliably buys large score gains, which is why I hold about \$10,000 per child as the working level and move it only with evidence ([[#Education|Education]]).

> [!question]- "Where did the extra money go?"
> From FY1992 to FY2024, public school students rose 17.2% while all staff rose 51.5%. Teachers rose 33.4% and non-teaching staff 69.0%. Real spending per pupil rose 68% while real teacher pay fell 6%.

> [!question]- "Why include charter schools?"
> Charter schools received about 30%, or \$7,147, less per pupil than district schools across 18 cities in 2019–20. CREDO's 2023 national study found charter students gained the equivalent of 16 more days of learning in reading and 6 in math per year than matched peers in district schools.


#### Rationale
Current spending is \$16,526 per pupil (Census, FY2023), and a large part of the growth since 1992 went to non-teaching staff rather than teachers. States near \$10,000 per pupil, such as Utah and Idaho, match or beat higher-spending states on NAEP. Funding that follows the child to any public school lets families leave schools that fail them, and charter schools have delivered gains at lower cost. Keeping public money out of private schools keeps it publicly accountable.

In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. Public K-12 schools collected about \$981.8 billion in revenue in FY2023, or \$19,871 per pupil: \$443.3 billion state, \$412.1 billion local, and \$126.4 billion federal (12.9 percent). Spending was \$18,905 per pupil in total expenditure and \$16,526 per pupil in current expenditure. The largest federal K-12 programs that block grants would replace are IDEA Part B grants to states (\$14.214 billion in FY2025) and Title I-A (\$18.4 billion in FY2025).

The evidence that local control by itself improves results is weak. Funding matters from low baselines and in poorer districts — older finance-reform studies find a 10 percent rise in per-pupil spending across all twelve years of school linked to 0.27 more completed years of education and 7.25 percent higher adult wages, with larger effects for children from low-income families — but those wage effects do not license New York-level spend. The United States already spends past the point where more money, without better use, reliably buys large NAEP gains, which is why I hold about \$10,000 per child as the working level and move it only with evidence. State funding of the per-child amount keeps the money guaranteed in poorer districts.

### Governance and Curriculum

>[!tldr] In short
> I would abolish the federal Department of Education and put control local first. States should set minimum core-skill standards; districts pick curriculum, limited to core skills and objective facts.

#### Position
The federal Department of Education is abolished outright, and its programs are ended or returned to the states. States that structured budgets around the transitional block grants are not cut off cold; their commitments are honored through the phase-out window. Control is local first. Local districts run schools, and the state guarantees the per-child funding, with state and federal law limited to child protections such as security of children's personal information and training and oversight for childcare, minimum core-skill standards, fraud and safety rules for public funds, and the protection of gifted programs and ability tracking ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#^gifted|Gifted programs and tracking]]). Charter schools set their own curriculum and remain publicly accountable; each private school sets its own curriculum. Public-school curriculum standards exist and carve away biases of any sort; private schools set their own. For public schools, states set minimum standards for core skills, and local districts pick curriculum and materials. Teaching focuses on core skills (literacy, numeracy, science, and civic knowledge) and is entirely objective about facts and subject knowledge, not ideological, with no forced patriotism. No framework is banned by viewpoint, but critical race theory, like any contested framework, may be taught only as one framework among others, never as settled fact, and no student or teacher may be required to affirm it ([[2. Political and Social Character#^cultural-marxism|Cultural Marxism]]).

Gifted programs and ability tracking stay. Every public school district keeps them, open to every student by merit, and entry may be decided by a test. No public school, district or charter, may cut, merge, or close a gifted program or an ability track to even out results between groups, and admission to a selective public school follows the test requirement in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#^test-required|Tests in selective admissions]]. Fully private schools, which take no public money, set their own programs and admissions, under the same bar on immutable traits as a requirement or a quota ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#^merit-rule|The merit rule]]). San Francisco moved eighth-grade algebra to high school to address racial gaps and brought it back ten years later, and Thomas Jefferson High School dropped its admissions test and cut offers to Asian-American students ([[2. Political and Social Character#^anti-merit|Anti-meritocratic beliefs in practice]]). ^gifted

**My view:** Gifted students should absolutely not be dragged down by less intelligent kids. Grouping students by what they can do lets each one move at the pace they can handle, and holding the fastest back to narrow a gap between groups punishes individuals for an average they did not set ([[2. Political and Social Character#^equality-of-outcome|From disparity to equal outcomes]]).

> [!question]- "Doesn't tracking widen gaps between groups?"
> Critics argue that it does, and San Francisco removed eighth-grade algebra on that ground. The gaps it aimed at persisted after the change ([[2. Political and Social Character#^anti-merit|Anti-meritocratic beliefs in practice]]). A gap between groups is a fact to be explained, not a reason to hold back the student who is ahead. What helps the students behind is better teaching for them, not a lower ceiling for everyone else.

> [!note]- Realistic transition
> In a realistic world, many systems — like taxes, like education, like all the systems that would be changing — would have some gradual period of change for most of these changes to happen. Here that means transitional block grants that phase out. That practical lag is a brief aside, not the main position.


#### Rationale
Education does not require a central ministry. Canada, Belgium, and Bosnia and Herzegovina have no national education ministry; Switzerland's cantons run its schools; Germany and Australia have national ministries but leave schools to their states. On PISA 2022, all five wealthy decentralized countries outscored the U.S. in math (Switzerland 508, Canada 497, Belgium 489, Australia 487, Germany 475, U.S. 465), while the U.S. scored 504 in reading, trailing only Canada (507) among them. Bosnia and Herzegovina did not take part in PISA 2022; in PISA 2018 it scored 406 in math and 403 in reading, far below the others. The comparison cannot show that decentralization causes these results, and Bosnia's scores show it does not guarantee them.

Local control is chosen on grounds of accountability and closeness to the families affected, not on outcome evidence. Restricting curriculum to objective knowledge and skills keeps public schools from becoming instruments of ideology. Curriculum is already set locally in most of the country: local entities develop curriculum in 43 states, the state alone in one (Rhode Island), and both in five. Common Core has no official tracker; roughly 30 states still use it in original, renamed, or revised form, and about 15 adopted and then repealed or replaced it.

### Homeschooling

>[!tldr] In short
> Homeschooling should be fully allowed, with no school-performance testing. I want oversight to be **tiered**: clean households file an annual paper or virtual welfare check-in - no surprise entry; complaint, prior-abuse history, or withdrawal-from-school after a CPS case triggers intensive unannounced visits. Homeschooling families receive a fully refundable \$1,000 federal income-tax credit per child per year ([[#K-12 Funding]]).

#### Position
Homeschooling is fully allowed, with no testing requirement. Child-welfare oversight is tiered, not universal quarterly entry.

**Clean households** (no open complaint, no substantiated prior abuse in the household, no withdrawal from school that followed a CPS investigation or open case): one annual paper or virtual welfare check-in with a child-protection caseworker — confirmation the child is alive, reachable, and not reported missing or injured; **no unannounced home entry** on that track.

**Triggered households** (any of: a credible welfare complaint; substantiated prior abuse or neglect in the household; withdrawal from school after a CPS investigation or while a CPS case was open): intensive unannounced welfare visits by a child-protection caseworker, at random times, at a cadence the caseworker sets from risk (and at least as often as needed to clear or escalate the case). Visits check the child's welfare only, never schoolwork.

Homeschooling families receive a fully refundable \$1,000 federal income-tax credit per child per year ([[#K-12 Funding]]). ^homeschool-credit


#### Rationale
Homeschooling carries no testing requirement because no study shows that state testing or progress reviews improve homeschool outcomes. The most comprehensive review of the research (Kunzman and Gaither, 2020) finds the literature cannot isolate the effect of homeschooling, let alone the effect of regulation. Where data cannot show either way, the less regulatory option applies. Private schools publish yearly results because parents choose among schools and need comparable information; a homeschooling family is not choosing among providers, so the same reason does not apply.

The real risk is abuse hidden behind a withdrawal from school. Knox et al. (2014) is a clinical series of 28 child-torture cases; 8 of 17 school-age victims (47%) had been removed from school and labeled homeschooled. That is a child-safety signal worth acting on when a **trigger** is present — complaint, prior abuse, or CPS-linked withdrawal — not a warrant for surprise state entry into every clean homeschool four times a year. An n=28 clinical series does not justify universal quarterly unannounced visits. The answer is tiered welfare oversight: annual paper or virtual check-in for clean households; intensive unannounced visits when a trigger fires — not school-performance testing, and not surveillance of every family that educates at home.

### Private Education Oversight

>[!tldr] In short
> Private and religious schools have the full right to exist and should be privately funded. I'd have them meet background-check, audit, and anti-fraud rules and publish a yearly norm-referenced test of their choice. Homeschooling keeps its testing exemption.

#### Position
Private and religious schools have the full right to exist. They are privately funded, with no public vouchers or education savings accounts. Private schools, religious schools, and any other privately funded education provider meet safety and fraud rules (background checks, financial audits, no fraud) and give a yearly nationally norm-referenced test of the school's choice, with results published. Homeschooling families are covered by the no-fraud rule and keep the testing exemption in the Homeschooling topic.


#### Rationale
State law on education is limited to child protections, minimum core-skill standards, and fraud and safety rules; these rules apply them to privately funded schools without public funds attached. A test of the school's choice gives parents comparable results without imposing the state curriculum. Florida's tax-credit scholarship, where schools choose a nationally norm-referenced test, raised college enrollment to 57 percent from 51 percent, while Louisiana's state-test model left students 0.41 standard deviations lower in math after one year with no significant effect on college entrance. The two programs differ in many ways, so the comparison cannot isolate testing rules as the cause.

### Higher Education and Student Loans

>[!tldr] In short
> Higher education sits outside public K-12 funding. I would make Pell Grants opt-in, end state subsidies, and stop new federal student lending; existing loans and contracts are honored.

#### Position
Higher education sits outside public K-12 funding. Pell Grants are converted to opt-in, taxpayer-designated funding through a voluntary checkoff. State subsidies for higher education end. Existing commitments to higher-education subsidy recipients are honored through the transition window; they are not cut off cold. Student loans for college are personal loans, not federal loans from taxpayers; anyone who pursues higher education does so on a personal loan, not on the taxpayer's dime. New federal lending stops, and existing federal loans are collected on their current terms. Existing loan contracts, including forgiveness terms in force when they were signed, are honored, and no new forgiveness programs are created.

> [!question]- "Won't ending federal student loans shut people out of college?"
> College remains open to anyone who finances it through a personal loan. Ending federal lending and phasing out state subsidies removes the taxpayer from the risk of a borrower's degree choice, and the cost and value of each degree are settled between the student, the lender, and the college.


#### Rationale
In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. Pell Grants reached 7.3 million students in 2024-25 at a cost of \$38.6 billion, with an average grant of \$5,320 and a maximum of \$7,395. State and local support for higher education totaled \$149.2 billion in FY2025. Public Service Loan Forgiveness and related programs had discharged \$93.4 billion for about 1.25 million borrowers through April 2026.

Federal student loans place the cost of a borrower's degree choice on taxpayers while any benefit stays private, which conflicts with my limits on redistribution and my emphasis on personal responsibility. The federal government is currently the main lender to U.S. college students through the Direct Loan program. Deming and Walters found that a 10% increase in public colleges' spending raised enrollment by about 3% and raised persistence and degree completion, while tuition cuts had no discernible effect on enrollment.

When the state subsidizes demand without freeing supply, prices rise, and federal student lending shows it (data below). As the commentator Brivael Le Pogam put it, "Guaranteed student loans, and universities raise tuition." X – [Brivael Le Pogam, 17 Oct 2026](https://x.com/brivael/status/2111420678914085225)

> Findings as reported by the study's authors.
>- Federal loan-limit increases of 2007–08 and 2008–09: institutions more exposed to the higher federal loan maximums raised sticker-price tuition by about 60 cents for each dollar of increase in the subsidized-loan maximum, with smaller but positive effects for unsubsidized loans; the effect was strongest for more expensive degrees, private institutions and two-year or vocational programs. Defenders of federal lending point to the chance that colleges offset higher sticker prices with their own grants. For the institutions that report grant data (about 60% of the sample), the same paper finds the opposite: schools cut their own grants by about 20 cents per dollar of added subsidized lending, so the price rise is not offset, which is one more reason federal student lending phases out under my plan. Lucca, Nadauld & Shen, Federal Reserve Bank of New York – [Credit Supply and the Rise in College Tuition: Evidence from the Expansion in Federal Student Aid Programs (Staff Report 733, revised 2017)](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr733.html)

## Infrastructure and Regulation

>[!tldr] In short
> Taxes should pay to keep core infrastructure safe and working. I would regulate businesses only for safety, serious environmental harm, worker exploitation, and consumer protection, and leave everything else to the market.

#### Position
Core infrastructure is maintained to safe and functional standards with tax revenue. Business regulation is legitimate only for genuine safety, prevention of serious environmental harm (as defined in [[9. Energy & Environment#Regulation and Conservation|Energy & Environment]]), prevention of clear worker exploitation, and protection against anti-consumer practices. Outside these domains, the market operates freely.

> [!question]- "Who decides what counts as genuine safety or clear exploitation?"
> The burden of proof rests on the regulation. A rule must show a real safety, environmental, worker-exploitation, or consumer-protection harm it addresses. Rules that cannot meet that test are removed, and the market governs everything else.


#### Rationale
Roads, bridges, and water systems are public goods that markets do not reliably provide, and their condition directly affects safety. Regulation beyond clear harms raises costs and is prone to capture by established firms. Dawson and Seater find that federal regulation accumulated since 1949 has significantly reduced U.S. output growth. Djankov and colleagues find that heavier entry regulation across 85 countries goes with more corruption and larger unofficial economies rather than better goods. Limiting regulation to defined harms keeps government effective where it is needed and out of the way elsewhere.
