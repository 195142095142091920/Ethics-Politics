## **Core Principles** 

Limited government, individual responsibility, low taxation, and maximum cultural and economic freedom, with only minimal safety nets. The state prioritizes free markets, personal choice, and fiscal restraint. Opt-in social programs and basic public services are acceptable; mandatory redistribution is not, beyond the narrow exceptions named in Welfare and Entitlements. Minimal necessary taxes fund core services: K-12 education, police, fire, emergency services, infrastructure upkeep, and the bare-minimum safety floor defined in Welfare and Entitlements. How the education tax is defined and collected, and who is exempt or opts in, remains open in the Taxation section.

## **Economic System** 

#### Position 
Market-based systems grounded in secure property rights and price signals have produced the largest sustained gains in material living standards, poverty reduction, and innovation in recorded history. Comparative evidence favors these systems over socialist or heavily state-directed alternatives. Historical cases of sustained socialist organization produced chronic shortages, lower productivity, and eventual stagnation or collapse relative to more market-oriented peers. Hybrid systems that preserved significant private ownership and price signals performed better than those that suppressed them.

The state’s economic role should remain limited to core public goods - national defense, domestic public safety, courts, basic infrastructure, a bare-minimum safety net, and a basic K-12 funding floor paid through a separate education tax that parents pay for their own children rather than through general taxes - plus a narrow set of evidence-based rules against force, fraud, and severe safety failures. Broad economic management, industrial planning, and taxpayer-funded corporate subsidies are rejected. There is no minimum wage; wages are set by voluntary agreement, and hardship is covered by the bare-minimum safety net ([[#Labor and Unions|Labor and Unions]]). A modest citizen dividend, funded from income the nation earns as an owner, such as royalties and lease payments on federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum, given back to citizens rather than income taken through taxation, is in place now, unconditionally; it does not wait on automation unemployment or a legislative trigger. It stays universal and limited, and it does not become mandatory redistribution or undermine work.

> [!question]- "Isn't a citizen dividend redistribution?"
> No. It is funded from a share of national product, not by taking income from some citizens through compelled taxation to pay others. A dividend funded by compelled redistribution would fall under the general rejection of mandatory redistribution.

> [!question]- "Don't markets leave some people behind?"
> Markets do not guarantee equal outcomes, and the framework does not claim they do. A minimal floor for basic necessities is funded by minimal necessary taxes, and anyone who wants to fund more can opt in to do so. What the framework rejects is mandatory redistribution beyond that floor and state direction of the economy, because the historical record shows those approaches produce shortages and stagnation that leave far more people behind. As markets spread after 1990, the share of the world living in extreme poverty (under \$3.00 a day) fell from 43.4% in 1990 to 10.1% in 2024, according to the World Bank. In 2023, the poorest 10% in the most economically free quarter of countries had incomes 7.8 times higher than the poorest 10% in the least free quarter, and extreme poverty affected 2% of people in the freest quarter compared with 52% in the least free.

#### Key Elements
- Strong presumption in favor of open markets and private property.
- Comparative superiority of market systems over socialist and heavily state-directed alternatives.
- Government confined to public goods and a narrow regulatory core.
- Rejection of corporate subsidies and most forms of industrial policy.
- No minimum wage; wages set by voluntary agreement.
- Citizen dividend in place now, unconditionally: a share of national product returned to every citizen, not income taken through taxation ([[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#AI and Automation|AI and Automation]]).

#### Rationale 
Comparative historical evidence shows that societies with greater economic freedom and stronger market institutions have achieved higher long-run growth, faster poverty reduction, and greater consumer abundance than those that suppressed private property and price signals. Targeted safety and anti-fraud rules address real market failures, but regulatory scope expands easily and is prone to capture; therefore the burden of proof remains on further intervention. Corporate subsidies distort capital allocation and reward political connection more reliably than economic value.

On minimum wages the evidence is genuinely contested. Some well-known studies (notably Card & Krueger) find little or no employment loss from moderate increases. Other research and several meta-analyses find negative employment effects, particularly for teenagers and the least skilled, with the size of the effect varying by the magnitude of the increase and local labor-market conditions. The weight of evidence does not support large or rapid increases as costless. The framework abolishes the minimum wage on rights grounds, not on the employment evidence alone ([[#Labor and Unions|Labor and Unions]]).

> Let markets do most of the heavy lifting because they have the best record at creating widespread prosperity. Keep government focused on the basics and on stopping clear harm. Avoid special favors for corporations. Be careful with wage mandates. Stay open to new ideas if technology changes the game, but don’t abandon the principles that produced the gains we already have.

## Labor and Unions

#### Position
Workers may form and join unions, and employers and workers may bargain freely, under freedom of association. No one is compelled to join or pay a union as a condition of work. Occupational licensing must meet the same burden of proof as any regulation: it is justified only where it prevents real harm to consumers, not to limit competition. There is no minimum wage. A minimum wage dictates the terms of a voluntary contract between a worker and an employer, and it bars people whose labor is worth less than the floor from working at all; neither is consistent with negative rights. Wages are set by agreement, including collective agreements reached by unions that workers join voluntarily. Low pay is supplemented by the earnings-only top-up and hardship is covered by the bare-minimum safety floor, both set out in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]], not by price controls on labor. Fraud, coercion, wage theft, and forced labor remain crimes. The abolition covers state minimum wages too: rights do not change at state lines, and the top-up uses one national zero-bracket line.

> [!question]- "Doesn't banning compelled dues weaken unions?"
> Unions remain free to organize and bargain. They keep members by delivering value, as any voluntary association does.

> [!question]- "Without a minimum wage, won't workers be exploited?"
> Exploitation through force, fraud, or coercion stays illegal: wage theft, deceptive contracts, and forced labor are rights violations and are prosecuted. What ends is the state setting the price of a voluntary agreement. Workers keep the right to refuse a job, to quit, and to join a union and bargain collectively. Several of the highest-wage countries in Europe, including Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Austria, and Italy, have no national minimum wage; wages there are set by collective agreements. Those agreements rest on very strong unions, which the framework allows only as voluntary associations, so the outcome in the United States would not simply copy theirs. People who still cannot earn enough are covered by the safety floor.

> [!question]- "Why abolish the minimum wage instead of keeping a small one?"
> A floor of any size is a rule that two consenting adults may not agree on certain terms, and it hits hardest those whose skills are worth the least. The federal minimum already binds very few people: in 2024, 1.0 percent of hourly paid workers earned \$7.25 or less. The safety floor, funded by minimal necessary taxes, handles hardship directly rather than through a price control that can price people out of work.

> [!question]- "Without a minimum wage, how do low earners get by?"
> Through pay set by agreement plus the earnings-only top-up built into the flat tax's zero bracket. A low earner's pay is topped up through the ordinary tax return, and the top-up shrinks gradually as earnings rise, reaching zero at the zero-bracket line, so taking more hours or a raise always leaves the worker better off. The small citizen dividend adds a little on top. The support comes through the tax system rather than from a rule that the employer must pay more, so no one is priced out of a job ([[#Welfare and Entitlements]]).

#### Key Elements
- Unions are voluntary associations.
- No compelled membership or dues.
- Licensing only where it prevents real consumer harm.
- No minimum wage; wages set by voluntary agreement, individual or collective.
- Abolition covers state minimum wages too; one national zero-bracket line.
- Fraud, coercion, wage theft, and forced labor remain crimes.
- Low pay supplemented by the earnings-only top-up; hardship covered by the bare-minimum safety floor.

#### Rationale
Union membership was 9.9% of U.S. wage and salary workers in 2024 (14.3 million), with 32.2% in the public sector and 5.9% in the private sector, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. In *Janus v. AFSCME* (2018) the Supreme Court held that compelling public employees to pay agency fees violates the First Amendment. Freedom of association is set out in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Freedom of Association|Authority & Civil Liberties]].

In 2024, 82,000 U.S. workers earned exactly the federal minimum of \$7.25 an hour and 760,000 earned less, together 1.0 percent of hourly paid workers, down from 13.4 percent in 1979; most were young, and about two-thirds worked in leisure and hospitality, where tips may supplement wages. As of July 2026, Denmark, Italy, Austria, Finland, and Sweden had no national minimum wage, and in those countries, as in Iceland, Norway, and Switzerland, minimums are set by collective agreements for specific sectors. In 2022 the share of low-wage earners was 4.1 percent in Sweden, 6.5 percent in Finland, 8.8 percent in Italy, and 9.7 percent in Denmark, against 14.7 percent across the EU. Portugal, which has a statutory minimum wage, had the lowest share, at 1.8 percent, so the absence of a minimum wage does not by itself explain low-pay rates.

The research on employment effects is contested. Neumark and Shirley's review of U.S. studies found a clear preponderance of negative employment estimates, strongest for teens, young adults, and the less educated. Cengiz, Dube, Lindner, and Zipperer, studying 138 state minimum-wage increases, found that the overall number of low-wage jobs was essentially unchanged five years later. The framework does not rest the abolition on the employment evidence: it rests on the right of workers and employers to set their own terms.

## Antitrust

#### Position
Size alone does not justify intervention. Firms that grow by serving customers better have violated no one's rights. Intervention is justified against fraud, collusion to fix prices or divide markets, and coercive conduct, and against genuine monopoly bottlenecks where refusal would leave people without reasonable alternatives for essential services. Any intervention carries the burden of proof, as all regulation does.

> [!question]- "Won't large firms abuse their power without stronger antitrust?"
> Abuse through fraud, collusion, or coercion is prosecuted. A large firm without such conduct faces competition and new entrants; breaking it up for its size punishes success rather than wrongdoing.

#### Key Elements
- No intervention for size alone.
- Collusion, fraud, and coercion prosecuted.
- Narrow monopoly-bottleneck exception.
- Burden of proof on intervention.

#### Rationale
The Sherman Act prohibits contracts and conspiracies in restraint of trade and monopolization (15 U.S.C. §§ 1–2). The bottleneck exception follows [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Freedom of Association|Authority & Civil Liberties]].

> Big is not illegal. Collusion, fraud, and coercion are prosecuted, and genuine bottlenecks over essential services can be regulated.

## **Taxation** 

#### Position 
The tax system across all levels of government should be low, simple, and limited to funding core public functions.

At the federal level the centerpiece is a flat income tax with a large zero bracket and a short, transparent phase-in. Income below roughly \$40,000-\$45,000 for single filers and \$80,000-\$90,000 for joint filers (adjusted over time) faces a zero rate. A brief linear phase-in range then applies so that the rate rises smoothly to the full flat rate of 18-22%. Income above the phase-in range faces that single flat rate. Capital gains and dividends receive preferential but still flat treatment. Most deductions, credits, and special preferences are eliminated to keep the base broad and the rules simple. The one refundable feature is the earnings-only top-up set out in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]]: within the zero bracket, earned income draws a negative income tax that phases out to zero at the zero-bracket line.

Other taxes are kept secondary and constrained:

- A modest, broad-based retail sales tax may serve as a supporting or transitional revenue source. A European-style VAT is rejected in favor of the more transparent sales tax.
- Corporate tax should be low and simple, ideally structured so business income is taxed only once.
- Payroll taxes should decline as Social Security and Medicare are reformed toward more contribution-linked designs and a smaller residual safety net.
- Across-the-board employer payroll tax cuts and permanent full expensing of business investment (bonus depreciation) are the preferred tools for raising U.S. employment. Both let businesses keep their own money rather than receiving taxpayer funds.
- Property taxes remain primarily local, moderate, and tied to core local services (police, fire, roads, basic schools). They are retained but constrained.
- Estate and inheritance taxes are rejected.
- Excise taxes and tariffs are acceptable only when narrow and justified by clear externalities or national-interest policy.

Permanent progressive income-tax rate schedules with rising marginal rates are rejected; a temporary surtax with an explicit end date is the sole transitional exception. The overall tax burden should remain low and matched to essential functions only: national defense, public safety, basic infrastructure, [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Education|a limited education funding floor]], debt service, and a small austere safety net. Temporary transitional measures (a high-end surtax or modest sales tax with an explicit end date) may be used while spending is brought under control.

> [!question]- "Are payroll tax cuts and full expensing just corporate subsidies by another name?"
> No. A subsidy transfers taxpayer money to a business. A payroll tax cut or full expensing takes less of the business's own money and transfers nothing. Both are consistent with the rule that taxpayer money subsidizes no business unless taxpayers opt in, and both apply across the board rather than to chosen firms or groups.

> [!question]- "Do these tax cuts stop outsourcing?"
> No study measures whether any incentive reduces outsourcing itself; the evidence measures domestic employment and investment. Across-the-board payroll tax cuts and full expensing are the two broad tools with measured gains in U.S. jobs.

#### Key Elements
- Flat federal income tax with a large zero bracket and a short linear phase-in.
- Earnings-only top-up (negative income tax) inside the zero bracket, phasing out at the zero-bracket line.
- Low, simple corporate tax; business income taxed once.
- Across-the-board employer payroll tax cuts.
- Permanent full expensing of business investment.
- Modest retail sales tax instead of a VAT; local, moderate property taxes; no estate tax.
- Tax burden matched to essential functions, alongside spending restraint.

#### Rationale 
High and complex taxes distort work, saving, investment, and compliance. U.S. federal revenue as a share of the economy has remained relatively stable for decades even when top statutory rates changed dramatically; very high rates do not reliably produce proportionally higher revenue. Federal revenue ranged from 13.2% to 18.0% of GDP during the 1950s, when the top rate was 91%, and CBO puts the 50-year average at 17.3% of GDP. Flat-tax reforms in other countries have shown that simplification is workable and need not collapse collections. An IMF review of flat-tax adoptions found that personal income tax revenue rose in some countries, such as Latvia, Lithuania, and Russia, and fell in others, with total revenue effects that varied by country.

A large zero bracket increases take-home pay for lower- and middle-income households and supports private spending. A short linear phase-in removes the sharp notch that would otherwise encourage people to hold income just below the threshold. Lower marginal rates above the phase-in reduce disincentives to work and invest. A visible retail sales tax is preferable to a VAT because it is more transparent and less prone to quiet rate increases. Corporate and payroll taxes should not serve as primary redistribution tools. Property tax is retained at the local level because it is a practical and accountable way to fund local order and infrastructure; eliminating it would require less accountable replacements. Estate taxes confiscate property that has already been taxed.

No study measures whether any incentive reduces outsourcing itself. The evidence instead measures domestic employment and investment, and the two broad tools with measured gains in U.S. jobs are across-the-board payroll tax cuts and full expensing. In Norway, the abolition of lower regional payroll tax rates showed that each 1-percentage-point payroll tax increase cut local employment by 1.37% while wages fell only 0.32%, so lower payroll taxes support employment where wages do not fully adjust. A Swedish payroll tax cut for young workers raised their employment by 2–3 percentage points with no change in net wages. Bonus depreciation raised eligible investment by 10.4% (2001–04) and 16.9% (2008–10), and areas more exposed to it gained employment at roughly \$20,000–\$53,000 of forgone revenue per job, though earnings per worker did not rise. Targeted hiring credits, Opportunity Zones, domestic-content procurement rules, and firm-specific deals show weaker, short-lived, or far more costly results, often above \$110,000 per job.

This structure is sustainable only alongside spending restraint. It can support strong defense, core public goods, and a minimal safety floor. It cannot support the present long-term trajectory of major entitlement programs without reforming those programs. The tax rules and the commitment to a smaller federal government are designed to fit together.

> [!info]- Funding K-12 education
> K-12 is funded by a separate education tax. The tax follows the child, not the household, and parents pay it by default, since it is their child's education. Parents who homeschool or send their children to private school are exempt. Childless taxpayers do not pay by default. Parents who cannot afford the tax get a sliding scale or hardship waiver, funded by the general-revenue backstop as a narrow, targeted subsidy for the child's basic education, not large-scale redistribution.

## Welfare and Entitlements

#### Position
People who followed the rules and are on benefits should not be punished when the system changes to something better, so there is a phase-out period that honors existing commitments. The obligation is to the person, not to the old funding stream: a person's status, meaning their entitlement, is honored through the transition, while the funding sources behind those benefits may change.

Existing welfare and entitlement programs are aggressively reduced to bare-minimum safety nets. Mandatory redistribution beyond a bare-minimum floor is rejected; that floor is funded by minimal necessary taxes as set out in Core Principles, and an opt-in funding model for remaining social programs is the preferred long-term structure. Benefits, when provided, cover only basic necessities and do not support comfort or lifestyle expansion. Privatization and structural reform of Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, and similar programs are pursued where feasible.

The bare-minimum floor contains two income parts. Each is a narrow, named exception to the rule against compelled redistribution, alongside emergency shelter, emergency medical care, and the K-12 backstop.

**Earnings-only top-up.** Low earners receive a top-up built into the flat tax's zero bracket: a negative income tax paid only on earned income, never for not working. It phases out gradually and reaches zero at the zero-bracket line, so there is no cliff where earning one more dollar costs a benefit, and it is administered through the ordinary tax return rather than a separate program. Its rate and peak are not yet set. It replaces the minimum wage as the way low pay is supplemented, and it supplements work rather than replacing it.

**People who cannot work.** Support comes in layers, in order: (1) private disability insurance, with employers enrolling workers by default and workers free to opt out; the self-employed and people between jobs get the same opt-out choice through their tax return, and the state does not run the insurance. This sits alongside the grandfathered Social Security opt-in set out in [[#Social Security and Medicare Transition]]; (2) people who can work some are covered by the top-up, which rewards every dollar earned; (3) charity and mutual aid, including respite for family caregivers; and (4) as a last resort, a strictly means-tested allowance for severe disability and for the elderly poor, paid from general revenue under the same backstop logic as grandfathered Social Security, with fraud enforcement and periodic medical reviews. The last-resort allowance is a separate named exception, not an opt-in program, so the rule that opt-in programs never fall back on general taxation does not apply to it. The citizen dividend ([[#Economic System]]) is paid to everyone but is small and is not counted on as a floor.

> [!question]- "Would cutting entitlements abandon people who paid into the system?"
> No. People who followed the rules and are on benefits are not punished when the system changes; there is a phase-out period that honors existing commitments. The person's entitlement is honored through the transition, even as the funding behind it shifts. Privatization and restructuring are pursued where feasible, and a bare-minimum safety net covering basic necessities remains. What is rejected is mandatory redistribution beyond that floor.

> [!question]- "What happens if opt-in funding falls short?"
> The program scales to what its contributors fund; it does not fall back on general taxation. The bare-minimum safety floor for basic necessities is funded separately by minimal necessary taxes, so a shortfall in an opt-in program never removes that floor.

> [!question]- "Isn't the top-up redistribution?"
> Yes, and the framework says so. It is paid from general revenue, so it is a narrow, named exception to the rule against compelled redistribution, like emergency shelter, emergency medical care, and the K-12 backstop. It is limited in three ways: it is paid only on earned income, it ends at the zero-bracket line, and it replaces the minimum wage and separate wage-subsidy credits rather than adding to them. Redistribution beyond the named exceptions remains rejected.

> [!question]- "What about people who can't work?"
> They are covered in layers. Employers enroll workers by default in private disability insurance, and workers may opt out; the self-employed and people between jobs get the same choice through their tax return. The insurance is private; the state does not run it. Those within the grandfathered Social Security system keep that protection. People who can work some draw the earnings-only top-up on what they earn. Charity and mutual aid, including respite for family caregivers, come next. As a last resort, people with severe disabilities and the elderly poor receive a strictly means-tested allowance from general revenue, under the same backstop logic as grandfathered Social Security. It is checked by fraud enforcement and periodic medical reviews, and it is a narrow exception, not a general welfare entitlement.

#### Key Elements
- People on benefits who followed the rules are not punished; a phase-out period honors existing commitments.
- Status (the person's entitlement) is honored through the transition; funding sources may change.
- Aggressive reduction of welfare and entitlements to bare-minimum safety nets.
- No mandatory redistribution beyond a bare-minimum floor.
- Opt-in funding for remaining social programs.
- Benefits limited to basic necessities.
- Earnings-only top-up in the flat tax's zero bracket: paid only on earned income, phasing out at the zero-bracket line with no cliff, administered through the tax return; a narrow exception to no compelled redistribution.
- For people who cannot work: private disability insurance, with default enrollment by employers (or through the tax return for the self-employed and those between jobs) and the right to opt out, not run by the state; the grandfathered Social Security opt-in; the top-up for partial work; charity and mutual aid, including caregiver respite.
- Last resort: a strictly means-tested allowance for severe disability and the elderly poor, from general revenue under the backstop logic, with fraud enforcement and periodic medical reviews; a narrow exception.
- Privatization and structural reform of Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, and similar programs where feasible.

#### Rationale
In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. Mandatory redistribution takes from some citizens to fund others, which the framework treats as coercion beyond the state's core role. Major entitlement programs also face long-term funding shortfalls under current law, so structural reform is needed regardless. The 2025 Trustees Reports project Social Security's combined trust funds to be depleted in 2034, with 81% of scheduled benefits then payable, and Medicare's Hospital Insurance trust fund in 2033. An opt-in model lets those who wish to fund social programs above the floor do so, while the minimal floor for basic necessities stays funded by minimal necessary taxes. Voluntary giving alone could not yet carry the floor: SNAP benefits in FY2024 (\$92.9 billion) exceeded all U.S. charitable giving to human services in 2024 (\$91.15 billion).

An earnings-only top-up is the tool with the strongest evidence for low earners. Hoynes and Patel found that a \$1,000 increase in the Earned Income Tax Credit raised single mothers' employment by 7.3 percentage points and cut the share of their families below the poverty line by 9.4 points. Paying it only on earned income matters: in the Seattle-Denver income maintenance experiment, which guaranteed income whether or not people worked, husbands cut their hours by about 9 percent and wives by about 20 percent. Delivering the top-up through the flat tax replaces the EITC's separate rules, which produce large errors: the Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration reported that 27.3 percent of EITC payments in FY2024 (\$15.9 billion of \$58.4 billion) were improper. One return with one phase-out leaves fewer rules to get wrong, though improper payments will not vanish and must still be policed.

In August 2026, 7.3 million people received Supplemental Security Income, averaging \$738.73 a month, and 7.0 million disabled workers received earned Social Security Disability Insurance benefits averaging \$1,635.90. The need is real: in 2023, 24.2 percent of working-age adults with disabilities living in the community were in poverty, against 9.9 percent of those without. Means-tested programs are also error-prone: SSI's improper-payment rate rose from 9.41 percent in FY2019 to 10.62 percent (about \$6.5 billion) in FY2023, which is why the last-resort allowance carries fraud enforcement and periodic medical review. Work-focused design shrinks the group who cannot work at all: after the Netherlands required employers to attempt rehabilitation before a disability claim (2002) and replaced its disability law with one built around remaining work capacity (2006), new disability claims fell by about 25 and 21 percentage points respectively, and the caseload by more than 60 percent. Voluntary institutions once carried much of this load: by David Beito's conservative estimate, one in three adult American men belonged to a fraternal society in 1920.

> [!info]- What is an opt-in funding model?
> An opt-in model funds a program through voluntary contributions from those who choose to support it, rather than through mandatory taxes on everyone. A voluntary checkoff, where a taxpayer chooses to direct a contribution to a program, counts as opt-in.

## Child Support

#### Position
Voluntary conception creates a support duty on both genetic parents who consented to the sex. Support is enforced by civil order first; willful refusal after an order is a rights violation, a fraud on the child's claim, with escalating penalties. In cases of rape, the victim parent owes no duty created by the rape, and the rapist parent owes full support.

> [!question]- "Does a parent who was raped owe support?"
> No. Duties arise from voluntary responsibility, and rape is not voluntary for the victim. The rapist parent owes full support.

#### Key Elements
- Support duty on both genetic parents who consented to the sex.
- Civil order first.
- Willful refusal after an order is a rights violation with escalating penalties.
- Victim parent in a rape owes no duty created by the rape.
- Rapist parent owes full support.

#### Rationale
Duties arise from voluntary responsibility, matching the voluntary-procreation responsibility in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Axioms|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]. A parent who consented to the sex took on the child's claim; refusing an order to meet it defrauds the child. Rape is not voluntary for the victim, so no duty arises for the victim from it. The U.S. Census Bureau reports that in 2022 about one in four children under 21 lived with only one parent while the other parent lived outside the household.

## Social Security and Medicare Transition

#### Position
Social Security and Medicare become opt-in only. Participation and contributions are voluntary; no one is compelled to pay into either program or to rely on it, and those who opt out arrange their own retirement savings and coverage. Current retirees are protected before the new system comes in: their benefits are an existing commitment that is not a rights violation, and existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition to the new system. People within ten years of full retirement age get the same protection as current retirees, grandfathered on current benefit formulas, as part of the same honored commitment. This protection is part of the position, not a practical concession. The separate bare-minimum safety floor for basic necessities, defined in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]], is unaffected.

> [!note]- Realistic transition
> In a realistic world, many systems — like taxes, like education, like all the systems that would be changing — would have some gradual period of change for most of these changes to happen. That practical lag is a brief aside, not the main position.

> [!question]- "Doesn't moving to opt-in break the promise made to people who paid in?"
> Current retirees are protected before the new system comes in, because existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition; people who planned around the current system are not cut off, and the basic-necessities floor remains. The programs are also not financed for the long term under current law, so continuing them unchanged would itself break the promise of scheduled benefits.

> [!question]- "Who pays for the protected benefits, and what about people who paid in but are more than ten years from retirement?"
> Protected benefits are funded by opt-in contributions plus a general-revenue backstop. This keeps the insurance framing, where people are repaid on what they paid in, and the backstop covers anyone who could not contribute enough, without compelled redistribution. People more than ten years from full retirement age keep a credit for the contributions they already paid, payable at retirement.

#### Key Elements
- Social Security and Medicare are opt-in only.
- No compelled contributions; participation is voluntary.
- Current retirees are protected before the new system comes in, as an honored existing commitment.
- People within ten years of full retirement age get the same protection, grandfathered on current benefit formulas.
- Protected benefits are funded by opt-in contributions plus a general-revenue backstop.
- People more than ten years from full retirement age keep a credit for contributions already paid, payable at retirement.
- The bare-minimum safety floor is unaffected.

#### Rationale
Compelled contributions to retirement and health programs are redistribution beyond the floor, which the framework rejects. Existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition, which is why current retirees are protected before the new system comes in. The protection extends to people within ten years of full retirement age because the transition risk lands hardest on them: they have planned and paid in around the current system and have the least time left to build their own retirement savings and coverage. The programs also face shortfalls under current law. The 2025 Trustees Reports project Social Security's combined trust funds to be depleted in 2034, with 81% of scheduled benefits payable after that, and Medicare's Hospital Insurance trust fund in 2033. CBO projects Medicare spending to rise from 2.6% of GDP in 2025 to 5.2% in 2055. Structural reform is needed regardless; the framework sets its direction toward voluntary participation.

- Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services – [2025 Medicare Trustees Report](https://www.cms.gov/oact/tr/2025)
- Congressional Budget Office – [The Long-Term Budget Outlook: 2025 to 2055](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61270)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Social Security and Medicare become voluntary: nobody is forced to pay in. Current retirees, and people within ten years of full retirement age, are protected before the new system comes in, as an honored commitment, and the separate basic safety floor stays.

## Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness

#### Position
Housing supply is governed by property rights and the market. Ordinary zoning that coordinates land use is allowed, but regulation that eliminates the economically beneficial use of property, or destroys most of its value, is a taking and requires compensation. Population growth is met through densification, development, and voluntary exchange, not political allocation of land. Housing subsidies require taxpayer opt-in. For homelessness, the safety floor provides emergency shelters only; it is not a housing program. Camping on public property is not permitted, and public drug use is prohibited.

> [!question]- "If public camping is banned, where do homeless people go?"
> To emergency shelter, which the safety floor provides. The floor covers a basic necessity, not long-term housing; permanent housing comes from the market, opt-in programs, and private charity. Public spaces remain open for everyone's use rather than becoming encampments.

#### Key Elements
- Housing supply is a market matter governed by secure property rights.
- Ordinary zoning is allowed; value-destroying regulation is a compensable taking.
- Growth is absorbed through densification and development.
- Housing subsidies require taxpayer opt-in.
- The safety floor provides emergency shelters only and is not a housing program.
- No camping on public property; no public drug use.

#### Rationale
Secure property and open land markets are how societies have historically absorbed population growth with rising living standards ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Property Rights|Authority & Civil Liberties]]). Homelessness has grown: HUD's 2024 point-in-time count found more than 770,000 people experiencing homelessness on a single night in January 2024, an 18% increase from 2023, and HUD attributed part of the rise to steep rent increases and years of under-building, along with migration and natural disasters.[^hud-pit] Supply restrictions that hold back building bear directly on the problem. The basic-necessities floor is set out in [[#Welfare and Entitlements]], and the rule on public drug use in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Drug Policy|Criminal Justice & Security]].

[^hud-pit]: Figures taken from HUD's December 27, 2024 press release on the 2024 AHAR; the full report could not be retrieved for this draft. A future data-overhaul pass will verify them against the report.

##### Sources
- U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development – [HUD Releases January 2024 Point-In-Time Count Report](https://archives.hud.gov/news/2024/pr24-327.cfm)
- U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development – [The 2024 Annual Homelessness Assessment Report (AHAR) to Congress, Part 1](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/sites/default/files/pdf/2024-AHAR-Part-1.pdf)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Housing is left mainly to property owners and the market. Basic zoning is allowed, but rules that wipe out property value must be paid for, and housing subsidies need voluntary funding. The safety floor offers emergency shelter only, not housing; camping and drug use in public are banned.

## Healthcare

#### Position
Healthcare is organized around freedom of choice. Individuals may opt into publicly supported systems or purchase private coverage. Mandates are rejected.

> [!question]- "Without a mandate, will healthy people avoid coverage and drive up costs?"
> The framework accepts that trade-off in favor of individual choice. People choose between a publicly supported option and private coverage, and no one is compelled to buy insurance. Personal responsibility for that choice is part of the design.

> [!question]- "What about people who can't afford private coverage?"
> They can opt into a publicly supported system. Emergency medical care remains part of the safety floor, so no one in a genuine emergency is left without care ([[2. Political and Social Character#Economic Orientation|Economic Orientation]]).

#### Key Elements
- Freedom of choice as the organizing principle.
- Opt-in publicly supported systems available.
- Private coverage available.
- No mandates.

#### Rationale
Compelling citizens to buy a product conflicts with the framework's limits on state coercion. Offering both public and private options keeps access while preserving choice. Federal health spending is a major driver of long-term budget pressure, which supports keeping public systems opt-in. CBO projects major federal health spending to rise from 5.8% of GDP in 2025 to 8.1% in 2055, with Medicare rising from 2.6% to 5.2% of GDP.

##### Sources
- Congressional Budget Office – [The Long-Term Budget Outlook: 2025 to 2055](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61270)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> People choose between a publicly supported plan and private insurance. No one is required to buy coverage.

## Healthcare Funding, Emergency Care, and Drug Approval

#### Position
The publicly supported healthcare option is opt-in, and its funding follows the rule for all programs beyond core services: subsidies require taxpayer opt-in. Emergency services are among the core services funded by minimal necessary taxes. Drug and device regulation is legitimate for genuine safety and against anti-consumer practices, with the burden of proof resting on each regulation. Insurance regulation is limited to preventing fraud and anti-consumer practices. No one is required to buy coverage.

> [!question]- "What happens to people who have no coverage and need emergency care?"
> Emergency services are part of the core public functions funded by minimal necessary taxes. Current federal law also requires hospitals with emergency departments to screen anyone who arrives and to stabilize an emergency condition or transfer the patient appropriately, regardless of ability to pay. The framework keeps emergency care within the safety floor while leaving routine coverage to individual choice.

#### Key Elements
- The public option is opt-in, and its subsidies require taxpayer opt-in.
- Emergency services are a tax-funded core service.
- Drug and device regulation is limited to genuine safety and anti-consumer practices, with the burden of proof on the rule.
- Insurance regulation targets fraud and anti-consumer practices.
- No coverage mandate.

#### Rationale
Most Americans already arrange coverage without a mandate: 92.0% had health insurance for some or all of 2024, with private coverage (66.1%) more common than public (35.5%). Federal health spending is the main driver of long-term budget pressure; CBO projects it to rise from 5.8% of GDP in 2025 to 8.1% in 2055. Keeping the public option opt-in prevents that pressure from becoming compulsory redistribution. The Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act (1986) requires a screening examination and stabilizing treatment or appropriate transfer in hospitals with emergency departments, without delay to ask about payment. Drug regulation follows the same burden-of-proof rule as all business regulation in [[#Infrastructure and Regulation]]. Federal law now permits non-animal alternatives in drug approval, consistent with [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Bioethics, Progress, & Technology]].

> The public health option is voluntary, and taxpayers fund it only by choice. Emergency care is part of the basic services taxes pay for. Drug and insurance rules exist only to protect safety and stop consumer abuse, and nobody has to buy insurance.

## Education

### K-12 Funding

#### Position
Some level of basic K-12 education is publicly funded through the separate, parent-paid education tax ([[#Taxation|Taxation]]), regulated to avoid administrative bloat and waste. Pre-K is not part of the floor. Special education is included in the floor through a disability weight added to the per-pupil amount. Public funding goes to public schools, including charter schools; publicly funded means publicly accountable. Families may switch between public schools. Private schools, religious or secular, and homeschooling are privately funded.

> [!question]- "Can a limited funding floor still produce a highly educated population?"
> Yes. The floor guarantees core skills for every child, and high-ability students can accelerate into demanding options. Families, employers, philanthropy, and students themselves continue to invest above the floor.

#### Key Elements
- Publicly funded basic K-12 education, kept free of administrative bloat and waste.
- Public funding goes to public schools, including charter schools; publicly funded means publicly accountable.
- Families may switch between public schools.
- Private schools and homeschooling are privately funded, and those parents are exempt from the education tax.
- Pre-K excluded from the floor; special education included through a disability weight.

#### Rationale
In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. Public K-12 schools collected about \$981.8 billion in revenue in FY2023, or \$19,871 per pupil: \$443.3 billion state, \$412.1 billion local, and \$126.4 billion federal (12.9 percent). Spending was \$18,905 per pupil in total expenditure and \$16,560 per pupil in current expenditure. The largest federal K-12 programs that block grants would replace are IDEA Part B grants to states (\$14.214 billion in FY2025) and Title I-A (\$18.4 billion in FY2025).

The evidence that local control by itself improves results is weak, while the evidence that school funding matters is strong: a 10 percent increase in per-pupil spending across all twelve years of school leads to 0.27 more completed years of education and 7.25 percent higher adult wages, with larger effects for children from low-income families. The state backstop on the funding floor keeps that floor guaranteed in poorer districts.

### Governance and Curriculum

#### Position
The federal Department of Education is abolished outright, and its programs are ended or returned to the states. States that structured budgets around the transitional block grants are not cut off cold; their commitments are honored through the phase-out window. Control is local first: local districts run schools, and the state backs up the funding floor, with state and federal law limited to child protections such as security of children's personal information and training and oversight for childcare, minimum core-skill standards, and fraud and safety rules for public funds. Charter schools set their own curriculum and remain publicly accountable; each private school sets its own curriculum. Public-school curriculum standards exist and carve away biases of any sort; private schools set their own. For public schools, states set minimum standards for core skills, and local districts pick curriculum and materials. Teaching focuses on core skills (literacy, numeracy, science, and civic knowledge) and is entirely objective about facts and subject knowledge, not ideological, with no bias against frameworks such as critical race theory or toward forced patriotism.

> [!note]- Realistic transition
> In a realistic world, many systems — like taxes, like education, like all the systems that would be changing — would have some gradual period of change for most of these changes to happen. Here that means transitional block grants that phase out. That practical lag is a brief aside, not the main position.

#### Key Elements
- Federal Department of Education abolished outright; its programs ended or returned to states, with any block-grant run-off as a practical aside.
- Local-first control; the state backs up the funding floor; state and federal law limited to child protections, minimum core-skill standards, and fraud and safety rules for public funds.
- Charter schools set their own curriculum and remain publicly accountable; private schools set their own curriculum.
- Public schools: states set minimum core-skill standards; local districts pick curriculum and materials.
- Curriculum limited to core skills and objective facts, without ideological bias in either direction.

#### Rationale
Education does not require a central ministry. Canada, Belgium, and Bosnia and Herzegovina have no national education ministry; Switzerland's cantons run its schools; Germany and Australia have national ministries but leave schools to their states. On PISA 2022, all five wealthy decentralized countries outscored the U.S. in math (Switzerland 508, Canada 497, Belgium 489, Australia 487, Germany 475, U.S. 465), while the U.S. scored 504 in reading, trailing only Canada (507) among them. Bosnia and Herzegovina did not take part in PISA 2022; in PISA 2018 it scored 406 in math and 403 in reading, far below the others. The comparison cannot show that decentralization causes these results, and Bosnia's scores show it does not guarantee them.

Local control is chosen on grounds of accountability and closeness to the families affected, not on outcome evidence. Restricting curriculum to objective knowledge and skills keeps public schools from becoming instruments of ideology. Curriculum is already set locally in most of the country: local entities develop curriculum in 43 states, the state alone in one (Rhode Island), and both in five. Common Core has no official tracker; roughly 30 states still use it in original, renamed, or revised form, and about 15 adopted and then repealed or replaced it.

### Homeschooling

#### Position
Homeschooling is fully allowed, with no testing requirement.

#### Key Elements
- Homeschooling fully allowed with no testing requirement.

#### Rationale
Homeschooling carries no testing requirement because no study shows that state testing or progress reviews improve homeschool outcomes; the most comprehensive review of the research (Kunzman and Gaither, 2020) finds the literature cannot isolate the effect of homeschooling, let alone the effect of regulation. Where data cannot show either way, the less regulatory option applies. Private schools publish yearly results because parents choose among schools and need comparable information; a homeschooling family is not choosing among providers, so the same reason does not apply.

### Private Education Oversight

#### Position
Private and religious schools have the full right to exist. They are privately funded, with no public vouchers or education savings accounts. Private schools, religious schools, and any other privately funded education provider meet safety and fraud rules (background checks, financial audits, no fraud) and give a yearly nationally norm-referenced test of the school's choice, with results published. Homeschooling families are covered by the no-fraud rule and keep the testing exemption in the Homeschooling topic.

#### Key Elements
- Full right of private and religious schools to exist; privately funded, with no public vouchers or education savings accounts.
- Private schools, religious schools, and other privately funded providers meet safety and fraud rules: background checks, financial audits, no fraud.
- Yearly nationally norm-referenced test of the school's choice, with results published.
- Homeschooling keeps its testing exemption.

#### Rationale
State law on education is limited to child protections, minimum core-skill standards, and fraud and safety rules; these rules apply them to privately funded schools without public funds attached. A test of the school's choice gives parents comparable results without imposing the state curriculum. Florida's tax-credit scholarship, where schools choose a nationally norm-referenced test, raised college enrollment to 57 percent from 51 percent, while Louisiana's state-test model left students 0.41 standard deviations lower in math after one year with no significant effect on college entrance; the two programs differ in many ways, so the comparison cannot isolate testing rules as the cause.

### Higher Education and Student Loans

#### Position
Higher education sits outside the public funding floor. Pell Grants are converted to opt-in, taxpayer-designated funding through a voluntary checkoff. State subsidies above the K-12 floor end. Existing commitments to higher-education subsidy recipients are honored through the transition window; they are not cut off cold. Student loans for college are personal loans, not federal loans from taxpayers; anyone who pursues higher education does so on a personal loan, not on the taxpayer's dime. New federal lending stops, and existing federal loans are collected on their current terms. Existing loan contracts, including forgiveness terms in force when they were signed, are honored, and no new forgiveness programs are created.

> [!question]- "Won't ending federal student loans shut people out of college?"
> College remains open to anyone who finances it through a personal loan. Ending federal lending and phasing out state subsidies removes the taxpayer from the risk of a borrower's degree choice, and the cost and value of each degree are settled between the student, the lender, and the college.

#### Key Elements
- Pell Grants converted to opt-in, taxpayer-designated funding (voluntary checkoff).
- State subsidies for higher education above the K-12 floor end.
- No new federal student loans; existing loans collected on current terms; existing contracts honored, no new forgiveness.

#### Rationale
In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. Pell Grants reached 7.3 million students in 2024-25 at a cost of \$38.6 billion, with an average grant of \$5,320 and a maximum of \$7,395. State and local support for higher education totaled \$149.2 billion in FY2025. Public Service Loan Forgiveness and related programs had discharged \$93.4 billion for about 1.25 million borrowers through April 2026.

Federal student loans place the cost of a borrower's degree choice on taxpayers while any benefit stays private, which conflicts with the framework's limits on redistribution and its emphasis on personal responsibility. The federal government is currently the main lender to U.S. college students through the Direct Loan program.

### Trade and Alternative Pathways

#### Position
Trade schools, apprenticeships, and certificate programs are equal pathways under the same rules.

#### Key Elements
- Trade schools, apprenticeships, and certificates named as equal pathways under the same rules.

## Infrastructure and Regulation

#### Position
Core infrastructure is maintained to safe and functional standards with tax revenue. Business regulation is legitimate only for genuine safety, prevention of serious environmental harm (as defined in [[9. Energy & Environment#Regulation and Conservation|Energy & Environment]]), prevention of clear worker exploitation, and protection against anti-consumer practices. Outside these domains, the market operates freely.

> [!question]- "Who decides what counts as genuine safety or clear exploitation?"
> The burden of proof rests on the regulation. A rule must show a real safety, environmental, worker-exploitation, or consumer-protection harm it addresses. Rules that cannot meet that test are removed, and the market governs everything else.

#### Key Elements
- Tax-funded core infrastructure maintained to safe and functional standards.
- Regulation limited to safety, serious environmental harm, worker exploitation, and anti-consumer practices.
- Free market outside those domains.

#### Rationale
Roads, bridges, and water systems are public goods that markets do not reliably provide, and their condition directly affects safety. Regulation beyond clear harms raises costs and is prone to capture by established firms. Dawson and Seater find that federal regulation accumulated since 1949 has significantly reduced U.S. output growth, and Djankov and colleagues find that heavier entry regulation across 85 countries goes with more corruption and larger unofficial economies rather than better goods. Limiting regulation to defined harms keeps government effective where it is needed and out of the way elsewhere.

- American Society of Civil Engineers – [Report Card for America's Infrastructure](https://www.infrastructurereportcard.org/)
- Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs – [Reginfo.gov](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/)
- Dawson & Seater, Journal of Economic Growth – [Federal Regulation and Aggregate Economic Growth (2013)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jecgro/v18y2013i2p137-177.html)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Taxes pay to keep core infrastructure safe and working. Businesses are regulated only for safety, serious environmental harm, worker exploitation, and consumer protection. Everything else is left to the market.

## Fiscal Policy

#### Position
A balanced budget is the proper target. Deficit spending is minimized. Long-term policy aims at reducing and eventually eliminating the national debt. Defense is the top spending priority; reductions come from other areas.

> [!question]- "Is eliminating the national debt realistic?"
> It is a long-term aim, not an immediate demand. Balanced budgets stop the debt from growing, and sustained restraint reduces it over time. The direction matters as much as the timeline.

#### Key Elements
- Balanced budget as the target.
- Minimal deficit spending.
- Long-term reduction and eventual elimination of the national debt.

#### Rationale
Persistent deficits shift costs to future taxpayers and raise interest payments that crowd out other spending. Federal debt and interest costs have grown substantially. CBO projects debt held by the public to rise from 101% of GDP in 2026 to 120% in 2036, above the 1946 record of 106%, and net interest to rise from \$1.04 trillion to \$2.14 trillion over the same period. Fiscal restraint is consistent with the framework's limits on government and its preference for low taxation. Federal debt passed 100% of the economy in 2013, when both debt and GDP were about \$16.7 trillion. The debt has been eliminated before, briefly, in 1835.

##### Sources
- U.S. Treasury Fiscal Data – [Understanding the National Debt](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/americas-finance-guide/national-debt/)
- Congressional Budget Office – [The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61882)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> The budget should balance, deficits should be kept to a minimum, and the national debt should be paid down and eventually eliminated.

## Monetary Policy

#### Position
Stable money follows from fiscal restraint. With balanced budgets and debt reduction, the government does not rely on the central bank to finance deficits, and monetary policy aims at price stability. Inflation acts as an unlegislated tax on savings and wages, so preventing it is part of keeping the tax burden visible and matched to essential functions. The Federal Reserve is reformed, not abolished. It keeps a single mandate, price stability under a published rule, and the employment mandate is dropped. It does not finance deficits, and it does not run asset purchases that work as deficit finance. As lender of last resort it lends only to solvent banks, against good collateral, at a penalty rate, and it does not bail out insolvent firms, whose losses would otherwise fall on taxpayers who never consented. It is fully audited in public. Competing private currencies remain legal. Any profits it remits to the Treasury go to debt reduction.

> [!question]- "Isn't some inflation needed to manage the economy?"
> Price stability is the aim. Balanced budgets remove the main pressure for inflationary finance, and stable prices let people plan, save, and invest.

> [!question]- "Why not abolish the Fed?"
> Before the Fed, money held its value over the long run but swung sharply, with frequent deflation and recurring bank panics in 1873, 1893, and 1907 that destroyed depositors' savings. The Fed's worst failure, the 1929 to 1933 collapse, came from its own discretion, as Ben Bernanke acknowledged in 2002. The lesson the framework draws is to limit discretion, not to remove the institution: a single price-stability rule, no deficit finance, and lending only to solvent banks. A return to gold is not favored, since countries that left the gold standard recovered from the Depression sooner.

#### Key Elements
- Price stability as the goal.
- No deficit financing through the central bank.
- Inflation treated as a hidden tax.
- Follows from balanced budgets.
- The Federal Reserve is reformed, not abolished.
- Single mandate: price stability under a published rule; the employment mandate is dropped.
- No deficit monetization and no asset purchases that function as deficit finance.
- Lender of last resort only to solvent banks, against good collateral, at a penalty rate; no bailouts of insolvent firms.
- Full public audit.
- Competing private currencies legal.
- Any Fed profits remitted to the Treasury go to debt reduction.

#### Rationale
Consumer prices rose 9.1% over the year ended June 2022, the largest 12-month increase since November 1981, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Balanced budgets are set out in [[#Fiscal Policy]]. By the Minneapolis Fed's consumer price index, the standard deviation of annual inflation was about 5.7 points in 1801 to 1912 and about 1.5 points in 1984 to 2025, but prices rose about 32-fold from 1913 to 2025, so a 1913 dollar buys about 3 percent of what it did. NBER business-cycle dates show average contractions of 21.6 months in 1854 to 1919 and 10.3 months in 1945 to 2020, though Christina Romer found that older data overstate pre-1914 volatility. The Federal Reserve Act sets three goals: maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates. The Fed remitted \$107.4 billion to the Treasury for 2021; since then its interest costs have exceeded its income, and its deferred asset stood at \$243.5 billion at the end of 2025.

## Public-Sector Size and Civil Service

#### Position
The size of government follows from its functions. Spending and staffing are matched to essential functions, with defense the top priority, and functions that private firms can perform are contracted out, as government research already is. Agencies with no essential function, such as the federal Department of Education, are abolished. When an agency is abolished, career staff who built their lives around these jobs deserve severance or placement help: that is the commitment side, honoring people who followed the rules. Political appointees are different; their roles end with the office, and there is no transition obligation to them.

> [!question]- "Doesn't contracting out reduce accountability?"
> Contracts set measurable deliverables and can be ended. A contractor that fails loses the work, which is a stronger check than the near-permanence of a public office.

#### Key Elements
- Staffing matched to essential functions.
- Defense the top priority.
- Contracting out where private firms can perform.
- Non-essential agencies abolished.
- Career staff at abolished agencies get severance or placement help; political appointees do not.

#### Rationale
In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. The federal civilian workforce was about 2.3 million on September 30, 2024, according to the Office of Personnel Management. Government R&D through private contracts is set out in [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Intellectual Property and Innovation|Bioethics, Progress, & Technology]].

##### Sources
- Office of Personnel Management – [New Data Shows Trump Administration's Progress in Right-Sizing the Federal Bureaucracy](https://www.opm.gov/news/new-data-shows-trump-administration%CA%BCs-progress-in-right-sizing-the-federal-bureaucracy/)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Government is sized to its essential functions, with defense first. Work private firms can do is contracted out, and agencies without an essential function are abolished. Career staff get severance or placement help; political appointees' roles end with the office.

## Energy Policy

#### Position
A gradual shift toward cleaner energy sources is accepted. Nuclear power is incentivized, first through deregulation such as streamlined licensing and permitting. Any public financial support for nuclear is drawn only from taxpayers who opt in to fund it; no taxpayer is compelled to subsidize nuclear or any other energy industry. Renewables may be encouraged but are not mandated. Energy markets remain competitive and consumer-oriented, without taxpayer subsidies (absent opt-in) or coercive industrial policy.

> [!question]- "Does incentivizing nuclear contradict opposition to subsidies?"
> No. Nuclear is incentivized first by removing the regulatory and permitting obstacles that add to its cost and delay, and any public money comes only from taxpayers who opt in, consistent with [[9. Energy & Environment#Subsidies and Mandates|Energy & Environment]]. Nuclear is singled out because of its safety record and reliability. Energy policy is set out further in [[9. Energy & Environment|Energy & Environment]].

#### Key Elements
- Gradual transition to cleaner energy.
- Nuclear power incentivized: deregulation first; any public money only from taxpayers who opt in.
- Renewables encouraged, not mandated.
- Competitive, consumer-oriented energy markets.
- No taxpayer subsidies (absent opt-in) or coercive industrial policy.

#### Rationale
A reliable, affordable energy supply underpins economic growth, so transition must not disrupt it. Nuclear power produces large amounts of low-carbon electricity with one of the lowest death rates per unit of energy of any source. Construction cost is the largest barrier to new reactors, so streamlined licensing works alongside standardized designs and market competition rather than replacing them. Competitive markets drive innovation more efficiently than mandates.

> The country shifts slowly toward cleaner energy. Nuclear power is encouraged mainly by cutting red tape, with any public money coming only from taxpayers who choose to chip in. Renewables are welcome but not required, and energy markets stay competitive without taxpayer subsidies.

## Agriculture and Food Policy

#### Position
Farming receives no taxpayer subsidies without opt-in funding, consistent with the rule for every industry. Food safety is regulated to consumer and public health standards. Lab-grown meat is supported as the best path to ending animal agriculture, and regulation does not block it from competing.

> [!question]- "Won't farmers fail without subsidies?"
> Farms compete like other businesses. Opt-in funding remains available for those who choose to support agriculture, and consumers are protected by safety standards rather than by subsidies.

#### Key Elements
- No subsidies without opt-in funding.
- Food safety to consumer and public health standards.
- Lab-grown meat supported and allowed to compete.
- Long-term aim of ending animal agriculture.

#### Rationale
Direct government payments to farm producers were about \$54 billion in 2020, measured in 2024 dollars, largely pandemic assistance, according to USDA's Economic Research Service. The no-subsidy rule is set out in [[9. Energy & Environment#Subsidies and Mandates|Energy & Environment]], and lab-grown meat in [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Bioethics, Progress, & Technology]].
