## Core Principles 
Strong individual liberties and property rights with government limited to protecting sentient rights, public safety, and order. Opt-in mechanisms where possible; harsh accountability for criminals and officials; skepticism of overreach but support for effective enforcement.

- **Surveillance State** (Practical application / tension with privacy; better after privacy is defined)
- **Due Process & Rule of Law** (Procedural protections and accountability once the state acts)
- **Police & Law Enforcement** (The concrete enforcement arm of the above principles)
- **Voting & Elections** (How political authority is selected and constrained — placed near the end because it is more about the machinery of government than core civil liberties)

## Individual Liberty vs State Power

>[!tldr] In short
> The government should leave people alone unless they are actively harming others or about to do so. That is the core rule.
> 
> Speech stays free except for real threats and calls to immediate violence. Being watched while you are out in public is normal; being tracked everywhere by permanent databases is not something that should be accepted lightly.
> 
> Liberty comes first. The state steps in only to stop rights violations, and it has to justify every step it takes.

#### Position
The state may use force only when it is necessary to stop people from violating the [[Glossary#Negative rights|negative rights]] of others — mainly force, fraud, or the direct imposition of serious non-consensual harm. Liberty is the default. Any mandate, speech restriction, or surveillance power has to clear a high bar: it must be necessary, narrowly aimed at the actual problem, and temporary when the threat is temporary. Personal choices end when they cross into violating someone else’s rights, not merely when they create some diffuse or indirect effect.

> [!question]- "Doesn't almost every choice affect someone else? Where does that leave the line?"
> Almost every choice has some effect on others, which is why indirect effect is not the test. The test is whether the choice violates someone's negative rights through force, fraud, or the direct imposition of serious non-consensual harm. A choice that others dislike, or that changes a social environment they would prefer to keep, does not meet that test. A choice that injures, defrauds, or directly endangers someone does.

#### Key Elements
- Coercion is justified to protect negative rights, not to pursue broad “best interest” or social optimization.
- Speech is protected except for true threats and incitement to imminent violence.
- Ordinary observation in public is acceptable; mass, persistent technological surveillance needs stronger justification.
- Those who impose serious non-consensual risks can forfeit some of the protections they would otherwise claim.

#### Rationale 
Rights are treated as side-constraints, not as goals that can be balanced away whenever a net benefit appears. The state does not get to override liberty simply because a policy is believed to be good for people overall. It acts when one person is violating, or is about to violate, another’s rights. The practical test used is simple: Is someone violating (or imminently about to violate) another’s negative rights? Is the response necessary and proportionate? If the answer to either question is no, the state should stay out.

##### Speech
Speech follows the same logic. The line is drawn at true threats and incitement to imminent lawless action. Once the state starts policing ideology, offense, or “misinformation,” it is no longer protecting rights, it is managing thought. That is incompatible with free inquiry and a limited role for government.

##### Surveillance
Public surveillance sits in a middle position. There is no reasonable expectation of privacy against ordinary observation while walking down the street. Continuous bulk tracking and permanent facial-recognition databases are different; they concentrate power and change the relationship between citizen and state even if no traditional “search” occurs. NIST testing of face-recognition algorithms found false-positive rates that often differed by factors of 10 to 100 across demographic groups. Normal public-order policing is accepted while tighter limits are required on the more sweeping technological versions.

> [!example] Proportionate response means the force or punishment used in reply to a rights violation should be matched to the severity and nature of the violation. Enough to stop it, deter it, and reflect the seriousness of what was done, but not wildly excessive relative to the harm.

## Government Structure and Checks

>[!tldr] In short
> Government exists to protect rights and must justify every expansion. The branches stay separate, new rules carry the burden of proof, and agencies cannot go beyond the laws that authorize them.

#### Position
Government power is bounded by the same test applied to every use of coercion: it exists to protect sentient rights and must justify each extension. Separated legislative, executive, and judicial powers are retained so that no single branch can expand state authority unchecked. The burden of proof rests on any new regulation, not on the people regulated, and administrative rules carry no more authority than the statutes that authorize them. Emergency authority lapses automatically unless the legislature renews it. I believe that when a federal action is unconstitutional, the ruling should protect everyone's rights the same way, through class actions or fast Supreme Court review, so a right never depends on which district a person lives in. Single district judges should not set national policy by themselves.

> [!question]- "Doesn't a limited government struggle to act when needed?"
> No. A limited government acts decisively within its core functions: defense, policing, courts, and the protection of rights. The limits apply to expansion beyond those functions, which must be justified rather than presumed.

#### Key Elements
- Government power bounded to protecting sentient rights.
- Separation of powers retained.
- Burden of proof on every new regulation.
- Agency rules no broader than authorizing statutes.
- Emergency powers expire unless renewed.
- Unconstitutional federal actions blocked for everyone through class actions or fast Supreme Court review, not by a single district judge.

#### Rationale
The Constitution vests legislative power in Congress, executive power in the President, and judicial power in the courts (Articles I–III). In *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024) the Supreme Court overruled *Chevron* deference, holding that courts must exercise independent judgment on whether an agency acted within its statutory authority. That aligns with the rule that the burden of justification rests on the regulator, set out in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Infrastructure and Regulation|Domestic Policy & Governance]].

## Term Limits

>[!tldr] In short
> No term limits on any elected office. Every elected office runs on a three-year cycle, and re-election stays on the table based on performance. Federal offices need a constitutional amendment to match that structure, including the presidency and Congress.

#### Position
There are no term limits on any elected office. Re-election is always on the table based on performance: if they do a good job, voters can re-elect them and they continue; bad performance ends it. Somebody who is successful can stay indefinitely based on the people's want. This applies to all elected offices: the presidency, Congress (representatives and senators), governors, and every other elected position. Election cycles are three years for every elected office. Keeping re-election always available kills lame-duck corruption, because the incentive to stay clean never drops; there is always another election coming.

> [!question]- "Don't term limits stop career politicians and corruption?"
> No. Officials who cannot run again are lame ducks, and the accountability evidence runs the other way: Brazilian mayors who could seek re-election diverted about 27% fewer resources than mayors in their final term. Studies of U.S. state legislative term limits find turnover and a shift of influence toward governors, agencies, and lobbyists rather than a proven drop in corruption. Accountability comes from voters facing another election on a short cycle, not from a hard exit date.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the Twenty-Second Amendment already limit the presidency?"
> Yes under current law. This position replaces that structure: the presidency, like every other elected office, has no term limit and runs on a three-year cycle. Putting that in force for federal offices requires constitutional amendment, including changing or repealing the Twenty-Second Amendment and aligning House and Senate terms with the three-year cycle. Under *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton* (1995), states cannot add congressional term limits on their own.

> [!question]- "Does the whole Senate turn over at once, and what about judges and state offices?"
> No. Each Senate term lasts three years. The Senate stays staggered in thirds, with one class up every year on the three-year cycle, so there is always a fresh electoral check without the whole chamber turning over at once. Elected judges are on the same three-year cycle. States adopt the three-year cycle by changing their state constitutions, starting at the next regular election after adoption.

#### Key Elements
- No term limits on any elected office.
- Three-year election cycles for every elected office.
- Re-election always available based on performance.
- Each Senate term lasts three years, staggered in thirds, one class up each year; elected judges included; states adopt the cycle by state constitutional change at the next regular election.
- Scope: presidency, Congress (House and Senate), governors, and every other elected position.
- Federal implementation requires constitutional amendment (including the Twenty-Second Amendment and congressional terms).

#### Rationale
Accountability that never turns off beats a forced last term. Ferraz and Finan (2011) found Brazilian mayors eligible for re-election misappropriated about 27% fewer resources than lame-duck mayors in randomized federal audits. Alt, Bueno de Mesquita, and Rose (2011) found U.S. governors eligible for re-election outperformed term-limited governors on growth, taxes, spending, and borrowing costs. State legislative term-limit studies find higher turnover and a shift of influence toward governors and the bureaucracy, not a measured anti-corruption gain. The framework therefore rejects adding term limits and shortens the cycle to three years so voters can act while the incentive to stay clean is still live. Changing presidential and congressional terms requires amendment; *Thornton* bars states from adding congressional qualifications on their own.

## Federalism

>[!tldr] In short
> Defense, borders, and immigration are federal; schools are local with a state funding floor. Rights apply the same everywhere, and programs are opt-in rather than imposed from above.

#### Position
Powers are placed at the level closest to the people that can carry them out. National defense, foreign policy, immigration, border enforcement, and national election-integrity standards (ID, authentication, counting method) are federal; states and localities cannot obstruct them, which is why sanctuary policies are abolished. Schools are run locally, with each state funding about \$10,000 per child that follows the child to any public school in the state, and the federal Department of Education abolished. Programs funded by opt-in money follow the same principle: participation is chosen, not imposed from the top. I believe policing is local by default. The federal government may reinforce or take over local police only where local authorities fail to stop violent crime or riots, under a time-limited emergency that Congress must renew. The District of Columbia, as a federal district, is Congress's direct responsibility.

> [!question]- "Doesn't local control produce unequal outcomes between places?"
> Some variation is expected and acceptable. Rights protections apply equally everywhere; what varies is how localities choose to deliver services, and people and families can move or choose among providers.

#### Key Elements
- Federal: defense, foreign policy, immigration, borders, national election-integrity standards (ID, authentication, counting method).
- No sanctuary jurisdictions obstructing federal law.
- Local school control with a state funding floor; no federal Department of Education.
- Rights protections uniform nationwide.
- Opt-in funding over top-down mandates.
- Policing local by default; federal reinforcement or takeover only where local authorities fail to stop violent crime or riots, under a time-limited emergency Congress must renew.

#### Rationale
The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states or the people the powers not delegated to the United States. Immigration and naturalization are federal powers under Article I, Section 8, and in *Arizona v. United States* (2012) the Supreme Court held that federal immigration law preempts conflicting state measures. The education structure is set out in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Education|Domestic Policy & Governance]].

## Emergency Powers

>[!tldr] In short
> Emergency powers must be necessary, narrow, and temporary. They expire automatically unless the legislature votes to renew them, free speech keeps working, and the state does not keep extra powers once the danger passes. During an emergency, charging, trial, and sentencing move to the law enforcement officers or military handling it.

#### Position
Emergency powers are held to the same test as any mandate: necessary, narrowly aimed at the actual threat, and temporary. Every emergency declaration and every power exercised under it expires automatically on a fixed date unless the legislature votes to renew it. Renewal requires a fresh justification on current evidence. In an emergency, charging, trial, and sentencing move to the law enforcement officers or military handling it ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Gun Crime and Violence|Gun Crime and Violence]]). In a declared state of emergency involving riots or elevated crime, the military and police handling it may impose and carry out a death sentence on the spot. The emergency does not lower the [[Glossary#Capital evidentiary standard|capital evidentiary standard]]: the officer must be certain of the act to that standard before acting. Every on-the-spot sentence is recorded, including body-camera footage, and an ordinary civilian court reviews the record after the emergency ends; an officer who carried out a sentence the record does not support is charged as for murder. Speech stays protected except for true threats and incitement to imminent violence. Open-ended emergency powers, and the permanent expansion of state authority after the acute danger has passed, are rejected.

**My view:** I believe emergencies add leniency: in an emergency, charging, trial, and sentencing move to the law enforcement officers or military handling it.

> [!question]- "Can a government respond fast enough to a real crisis under these limits?"
> Yes. The executive can act immediately when a severe, direct threat appears. The limits govern duration, not speed: the powers lapse on their own unless the legislature affirmatively renews them, so extraordinary authority cannot continue by inertia.

#### Key Elements
- Emergency powers follow the necessity, narrowness, and time-limit test.
- Every declaration and power expires automatically unless the legislature renews it.
- Renewal requires fresh justification on current evidence.
- Speech protections are not suspended; in an emergency, charging, trial, and sentencing move to the law enforcement officers or military handling it.
- In a declared state of emergency involving riots or elevated crime, the military and police may impose and carry out death sentences on the spot, under the capital evidentiary standard; every case is recorded and reviewed by a civilian court afterward.
- No permanent expansion of state power after the emergency ends.

#### Rationale
Under the National Emergencies Act, a declared emergency ends only by joint resolution or presidential proclamation, or if the President fails to renew it each year; Congress is directed to meet every six months to consider termination. The default favors continuation, and emergencies accumulate: as of October 2, 2026, 51 declared national emergencies were in effect. Automatic expiry with legislative renewal reverses the default, so the burden rests on continuing a power rather than on ending it.

## Property Rights

>[!tldr] In short
> 
> Once ownership is properly established, it is meant to last. The state may take property only for narrow public purposes and must pay full value. Ordinary zoning can exist, but rules that destroy the real value of ownership without payment are treated as takings. There are two exceptions. Under the oath-standard carve-out, places promoting anti-American or anti-constitutional causes, such as Nazi, communist, or compulsory socialist sites, or mosques under the excluded-religions amendment, may be barred from being built or rebuilt. Under the [[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]], domestic animal-agriculture, commercial fishing, aquaculture, breeding-mill, and animal-testing businesses are compelled to shut down their animal operations. Firms that earn most of their revenue from them convert fully to cultivated meat or plant-based farming or are liquidated, and others divest that line. Owners and shareholders receive no compensation. After workers' unpaid wages and creditors are paid, all liquidation and conversion proceeds go to the state-run sanctuaries. Land the sanctuaries need from owners outside these industries is bought at fair value, and a family on a liquidated farm keeps its house and house lot. That breaks the property-rights part of my negative-rights view, and I accept that tradeoff for this plan only; the general rule stands for everything else. Intellectual property is protected for practical reasons even though it rests on thinner foundations than physical property. 
> 
> Owners may use necessary force to stop theft or intrusion; lethal force is available only when the intrusion is violent or carries a clear threat to persons or the security of the home. Pure non-violent theft is handled through restitution and criminal penalties, not lethal force. As population grows and time passes, adjustment happens through voluntary exchange, denser development, and new value creation - not through political resetting of existing titles. Secure property rights are treated as a basic condition for investment, agency, and a high-trust society.

#### Position 
Property rights rank among the strongest negative rights. Once ownership is legitimately acquired, it is permanent and may be defended with proportionate force. Eminent domain is limited to narrow public-use cases with full compensation. Ordinary zoning is not treated as a taking, but regulation that eliminates the economically beneficial uses of property, or destroys the great majority of its value, is treated as a taking and requires compensation. The first exception is the bar on building or rebuilding places that promote anti-American or anti-constitutional causes, under the oath standard ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Oath of Office and Perjury|Oath of Office and Perjury]]). The second is the [[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]: domestic animal-agriculture, commercial fishing, aquaculture, breeding-mill, and animal-testing businesses are compelled to shut down their animal operations. Firms that earn most of their revenue from them convert fully to cultivated meat or plant-based farming or are liquidated, and others divest that line. Owners and shareholders receive no compensation. After workers' unpaid wages and creditors are paid, all liquidation and conversion proceeds go to the state-run sanctuaries. Land the sanctuaries need from owners outside these industries is bought at fair value, and a family on a liquidated farm keeps its house and house lot, and the shutdown itself is not treated as a taking. I accept that this breaks the property-rights part of my negative-rights view; the general rule still stands for all other property. Intellectual property is protected for practical reasons even though its foundations are thinner than physical property. As population grows and time passes, adjustment occurs through voluntary exchange, development, and new value creation rather than political resetting of titles.

> [!question]- "If ownership is permanent, doesn't that lock in past injustices and leave later generations with nothing?"
> Permanence applies to legitimately acquired property. A title that rests on force or fraud was never legitimate, and the rights violation behind it can be answered through ordinary legal process. Later generations are not left with nothing: as population grows, adjustment comes through voluntary exchange, development, and new value creation. What the framework rejects is political redistribution of titles that were legitimately acquired.

> [!question]- "Can an owner use property to harm the neighbors?"
> No. Property is a negative right and stops where it violates others' rights. Pollution that harms others or imposes a severe, non-consensual risk of serious harm on them makes the polluter liable ([[9. Energy & Environment#Pollution and Liability|Pollution and Liability]]).

> [!question]- "Why protect intellectual property if its foundations are thinner?"
> It is protected for practical reasons of incentive and coordination, and subject to reasonable limits, rather than as a natural right equal to physical property.

#### Key Elements
- Legitimate acquisition creates durable ownership that the state is obligated to protect.
- Eminent domain is restricted to genuine public-use purposes, full compensation, and strict process, except the Sanctuary Plan exception below: land the sanctuaries need from owners outside the covered industries is bought at fair value, and covered firms receive nothing.
- Baseline zoning is allowed; regulation that eliminates economically beneficial uses or destroys most of the property’s value is treated as a taking, except the shutdown of animal agriculture, commercial fishing, breeding mills, and animal testing below.
- Exception to the takings and baseline zoning rules: the construction or rebuilding of places that promote anti-American or anti-constitutional causes may be banned. These are the categories of the sworn-in oath standard: Nazism, communism, compulsory socialism ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Socialism|Socialism]]), and Islam under the excluded-religions amendment. Examples are a Nazi gathering place with its symbols, an anti-American communist stronghold, or a new mosque ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Oath of Office and Perjury|Oath of Office and Perjury]], [[4. Religion & Secularism#Religious Freedom|Religious Freedom]]).
- Second exception to the takings and compensation rules: under the [[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]], domestic animal-agriculture, commercial fishing, aquaculture, breeding-mill, and animal-testing businesses are compelled to shut down their animal operations. Firms that earn most of their revenue from them convert fully to cultivated meat or plant-based farming or are liquidated, and others divest that line. Owners and shareholders receive no compensation. After workers' unpaid wages and creditors are paid, all liquidation and conversion proceeds go to the state-run sanctuaries. Land the sanctuaries need from owners outside these industries is bought at fair value, and a family on a liquidated farm keeps its house and house lot. I accept that this breaks the property-rights part of my negative-rights view; the general rule stands for everything else.
- Intellectual property receives protection for incentive and coordination reasons, subject to reasonable limits.
- Lethal force is justified when an intrusion is violent or carries a clear threat to persons or the security of the home. Pure non-violent property loss does not authorize lethal force.
- Population growth and the passage of time are handled through markets, densification, and new forms of value, not through periodic redistribution of existing titles.
- Clear and stable property rights historically support higher investment, capital formation, and living standards.

#### Rationale 
Secure property rights protect the ability to plan, invest, and build without constant fear that what has been created can be taken by force or by political decision. Evidence across countries and centuries shows that clearer, more stable ownership tends to produce more investment and higher incomes. Where ownership is insecure, people invest less, informal arrangements multiply, and conflict over resources becomes more common.

The idea that all land begins as government property until it is sold is set aside. The state’s role is to recognize and defend legitimate holdings once they exist, not to act as the original owner of every piece of territory. Once title is justly acquired, permanence is the default. Permanence does not freeze use. Owners remain free to sell, lease, subdivide, or redevelop. As population grows, rising demand raises the return to more intensive use. Land held in low-value ways faces growing market pressure to change hands or change use. Ownership protects the right to decide; it does not protect any particular use from competition.


> [!info]- What counts as legitimate holdings / just acquisition?
> Legitimate (or “just”) acquisition means the holding was obtained without violating others’ negative rights. The main routes are:
>
>- **First acquisition** of something previously unowned (classic homesteading / effective occupancy and improvement).
>- **Voluntary transfer** from a prior legitimate owner (sale, gift, inheritance, exchange).
>- **Rectification** in limited cases where a clear prior rights violation can be traced and corrected (stolen property returned, etc.).
>
>What is _not_ legitimate acquisition:
>
>- Conquest or seizure by force
>- Fraud
>- Political grant that simply takes from one private party to give to another
>- “We voted to take it” without a narrow public-use justification and full compensation
>
The framework does not require a perfect historical genealogy back to the first human. In practice it operates with a strong presumption in favor of long-settled, currently recognized titles and a high bar for disrupting them. The key test is whether the current claim rests on a chain of voluntary transfers or original appropriation rather than on ongoing aggression or pure political allocation.


Eminent domain stays an exception. It is acceptable only for clear public-use projects and only with full compensation and strong safeguards. Expanding it to private economic development or general revenue goals turns a limited tool into a political transfer mechanism. That expansion is rejected.

Zoning sits in the middle. Some basic coordination rules can reduce conflict without destroying ownership. When regulation becomes so heavy that formal title remains while the economically beneficial uses of the property are eliminated, or the great majority of its value is destroyed, the regulation functions as a taking and requires compensation.

Intellectual property rests on weaker ground than physical property. Ideas can be used by many people at once, and the boundaries are conventional. Protection is still maintained because limited exclusivity gives people reason to create and helps coordinate complex production. Because the justification is practical, the length and scope of protection remain open to evidence and adjustment.

Defense of property follows ordinary proportionality. An owner may use the force needed to stop the violation. Lethal force is justified when the intrusion is violent or carries a clear threat to persons or the security of the home. Pure non-violent theft or conversion does not authorize lethal force; it is handled through restitution and scaled criminal penalties. Once the immediate threat ends, the response shifts to recovery of the loss and proportionate punishment. This limit governs an owner's immediate defense; the state's sentencing of repeat offenders after conviction is a separate matter, set out in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Repeat Offenders and Forfeiture of the Right to Life|Criminal Justice & Security]].

As time passes and population increases, the framework does not promise every person a land allotment. It keeps peaceful acquisition and voluntary exchange open, and it refuses to treat existing ownership as a pool that may be reallocated whenever demographic pressure rises. Larger populations are supported by denser development, technological improvement, and the creation of new forms of value - all of which function better when ownership is secure. Societies that have maintained relatively stable property rights while allowing markets in land and housing have generally absorbed population growth with rising average living standards. Systems that make ownership politically revisable in the name of access have more often produced both insecurity and poorer overall results.

Strong property rights shrink the realm of political allocation, encourage investment, and support a high-trust order. Weakening them for short-term flexibility or redistributive convenience reintroduces the uncertainty the institution exists to limit.

## Self-Defense and Defense of Others

>[!tldr] In short
> People may use force to protect themselves or others anywhere, with no duty to retreat. An aggravated, avoidable killing is murder and goes to the mandatory capital track; near-justified self-defense is a lesser homicide with a lighter sentence set by the judge.

#### Position
A person may use force to stop an attack on themselves or on another person. There is no duty to retreat and no location limit: the right applies at home, in a vehicle, and in public alike. Whether a killing in a claimed act of self-defense is lawful turns on an intent test. A killing that was aggravated and avoidable is murder and goes to the mandatory capital track. "Avoidable" means the attack had already been stopped or could have been stopped without killing; it never means the defender could have retreated. A killing in near-justified self-defense is a lesser homicide, with a lighter sentence decided by the judge. Defense of property follows the separate rule in [[#Property Rights]].

> [!question]- "Doesn't removing the duty to retreat everywhere invite needless killings?"
> No. The absence of a duty to retreat does not protect a killer who acted with intent beyond defense. An aggravated, avoidable killing is murder and carries the mandatory capital sentence, and a killing that came close to justified self-defense but fell short is still punished as a lesser homicide.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the evidence show stand-your-ground laws increase homicides?"
> The evidence leans that way. RAND's review rates as supportive the evidence that stand-your-ground laws increase total and firearm homicides. The position does not rest on that evidence; it rests on principle. Running should never be the first option for a person facing an attack, and a threat should never feel free to intimidate because its target is expected to retreat. The intent test is the safeguard: an aggravated, avoidable killing is still murder on the capital track.

> [!question]- "Who has to prove whether a killing was self-defense?"
> The prosecution. It must prove intent for a lesser-homicide charge, and must prove any aggravating factor that moves a killing to the capital track, not just the act itself ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Lesser Homicide|Lesser Homicide]]).

#### Key Elements
- Force in defense of self or others.
- No duty to retreat.
- No location limit.
- Intent test for killings claimed as self-defense.
- Aggravated, avoidable killing is murder on the mandatory capital track.
- Near-justified self-defense is a lesser homicide with a lighter sentence decided by the judge.

#### Rationale
As of January 1, 2025, 35 states had stand-your-ground statutes or statutes extending castle doctrine beyond the home, according to RAND. RAND also reports supportive evidence that these laws are associated with increases in total and firearm homicides; the intent test answers that risk by treating an aggravated, avoidable killing as murder under the mandatory penalty in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Criminal Justice & Security]]. Qualification for gun ownership and red flag laws are set out in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Gun Rights|Cultural & Social Issues]].

## Freedom of Association

>[!tldr] In short
> 
> People, clubs, and religious groups are free to choose whom they associate with. Association is free only so long as it violates no one's rights: joining or organizing a group to commit or plan rights violations, such as a conspiracy, gang, or organized violent group, is not protected ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Terrorism and Domestic Extremism|Terrorism and Domestic Extremism]]), and no one may be held in a group against their will. Employers and businesses open to the public may not hire, fire, serve, or treat anyone differently for race, sex, sexual orientation, or another protected trait, in either direction, so there are no quotas or DEI preferences, though wide advertising is fine. Businesses may still decline to create expressive work they object to. Outside employment and public business, compelling association is coercion and needs the same high justification as any other use of state force.

#### Position
Private individuals and voluntary organizations have the right to decide whom they associate with in personal, religious, and club life, and to include or exclude on any basis. Employment and public business are different. Hiring, firing, pay, promotion, and service in a business open to the public may not turn on race, sex, sexual orientation, or another immutable or protected trait, because treating a person worse or better for what they are is a rights violation ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Equal Treatment in Employment and Business|Equal Treatment in Employment and Business]]). The rule is symmetric: it bars preferences and quotas, including private DEI preferences, as firmly as exclusion. Voluntary outreach that does not affect selection, such as advertising widely, remains legal. Sex means biological sex: male, female, and rare intersex conditions; gender identity is not a legal category. Outside those traits, employers and businesses keep wide freedom to choose on merit, conduct, and fit, and a business may decline to create expressive work it objects to. Broad mandates that police ordinary preferences in private social life are rejected.

> [!question]- Doesn't this lead to segregation, or discrimination?
> Legal segregation and private freedom of association are not the same thing. Historical Jim Crow was primarily state-enforced separation - laws that required racial division and often forbade private integration. A pure association rule does the opposite: the state may neither compel association nor compel separation. In personal, religious, and club life, private parties may exclude or include and the law stays neutral; in employment and public business, one neutral rule bars treatment by protected traits in either direction.
>
Markets and reputation raise the cost of widespread exclusion. Forced association can produce physical proximity without the trust that makes integration valuable. Every anti-discrimination regime still excludes - they simply transfer the decision from the rights-holder to the state. The framework rejects state-mandated segregation, permits voluntary sorting in private association, and treats exclusion or preference by protected traits in employment and public business as a rights violation.

> [!question]- "Could a business refuse service because of race?"
> No. A business open to the public may not refuse a customer, and an employer may not refuse a worker, because of race, sex, sexual orientation, or another protected trait. A business may still decline to create a message or expressive work it objects to, whoever asks, as the Supreme Court held in 303 Creative v. Elenis. Private clubs, religious bodies, and personal relationships remain free to choose their members. The state itself may never compel separation.

#### Key Elements
- Freedom of association is a direct extension of individual liberty and property rights.
- Personal, religious, and club association is free; members may be chosen on any basis. No new places of worship may be built for religions designated as excluded, and existing ones are capped with no expansion or state support; home practice with the household and invited guests stays free ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Religious Freedom|Religious Freedom]]). The same bar on building or rebuilding covers places that promote the other anti-constitutional causes of the oath standard ([[#Property Rights|Property Rights]]).
- Association is protected only while it violates no one's rights: conspiracies, gangs, and organized violent groups formed to commit or plan rights violations are not protected, and no one may be held in a group against their will.
- In employment and public business, no hiring, firing, or treatment by race, sex, sexual orientation, or another protected trait, in either direction: no exclusion, quotas, or DEI preferences.
- Government as an employer gives equal treatment on everything except anti-American and anti-constitutional beliefs, following the sworn-in oath standard: Nazism, communism, compulsory socialism, and Islam under the excluded-religions amendment ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Oath of Office and Perjury|Oath of Office and Perjury]]).
- Voluntary outreach that does not affect selection, such as advertising widely, stays legal.
- Businesses may decline to create expressive work they object to, for any customer.
- Further non-discrimination conditions only for genuine monopoly bottlenecks or direct safety risks.
- Outside employment and public business, compelled association is coercion and requires the same high justification as other state force.
#### Rationale
Freedom of association follows from the same negative-rights logic applied to liberty and property. If people own their labor, their time, and their property, they must be free to decide the terms on which they share them. Forcing a private party to associate, serve, or employ against their will is a form of compelled labor or compelled use of property. That compulsion is justified only when it is necessary to stop a rights violation, not when it is intended to produce a preferred social pattern. Employment and public business are where the framework finds such a violation. An employer or business that rejects or prefers a person for an immutable or protected trait judges them by group rather than by merit or conduct, and stopping that is the justification for the rule.

The historical and practical case for wide association rights is straightforward. Voluntary sorting allows cooperation among people who share values, risk tolerances, or purposes. It reduces conflict, supports high-trust environments, and lets different communities test different norms. When the state overrides those choices in the name of inclusion or equity, it substitutes political allocation for private coordination and often generates resentment and evasion rather than genuine integration.

Anti-discrimination rules are not rejected in every form. Rules that prohibit violence, fraud, or clear safety hazards (for example, refusing to serve someone who is actively threatening others) fit inside the negative-rights frame. In personal, religious, and club life, rules that treat ordinary preference or cultural affinity as illegal do not. In employment and public business, preference or exclusion by protected trait, including statistical discrimination by group, is barred because it judges people by group. Once the law begins policing the reasons for private choices rather than the presence of force or fraud, it expands beyond protecting rights and into managing social outcomes. That expansion is inconsistent with limited government and with the priority given to negative rights.

Public accommodations and common carriers follow the protected-trait rule like any public business. Beyond those traits, a business holding a genuine bottleneck position on essential services can be held to further narrow non-discrimination conditions, with the burden on the state to show necessity and narrow tailoring.

Membership organizations, religious groups, and social clubs keep the full right to choose their members, and religious bodies choose their own clergy and religious roles. The right to exclude is what makes the right to include meaningful. Without it, association becomes a state-managed status rather than a private decision.

In short, freedom of association is treated as a core negative right. The state may intervene to stop rights violations. It may not intervene to force people into relationships they do not want.

## Privacy Rights

>[!tldr] In short
> 
> Privacy means control over access to one’s body, home, private spaces, and personal information. Others do not get to enter, search, or collect without consent or a clear, limited legal process. Ordinary observation in public is acceptable; turning public presence into permanent, identifiable records is limited to public protection by the government and requires opt-in for companies. Companies may offer services that involve data sharing, but the terms must be honest and the ability to decline must be real. Government access should be targeted and process-based, not open-ended. Privacy protects against intrusion while still allowing focused investigation when rights are actually being violated.

#### Position 
Privacy protects a person from non-consensual intrusion into their body, home, personal spaces, and personal information. The default is control: others, including the state, do not get access without consent or a clear, limited justification tied to stopping rights violations. Ordinary observation in open public spaces carries no reasonable expectation of privacy. Entry into private spaces, searches of persons or belongings, and collection of personal data all require either consent or proper legal process. Mass, open-ended collection of data or permanent surveillance systems that convert public presence into lasting records face a higher bar.

> [!question]- **“Doesn’t strong privacy protection just help criminals and block legitimate security needs?”** 
> Privacy is not a right to hide criminal activity. Targeted investigation based on probable cause, warrants for specific places or data, and ordinary public observation remain available. The limit is on bulk, preemptive, or indefinite collection and on entering private spaces without process. Strong default privacy raises the cost of casual intrusion and mission creep while still allowing focused enforcement when force, fraud, or serious harm is actually at stake.

#### Key Elements
- The home and other genuinely private spaces receive strong protection against entry and search without consent or a warrant (or equivalent process).
- Bodily integrity and personal effects are protected against non-consensual search or seizure.
- Ordinary observation in open public places does not violate privacy; people who expose themselves to public view have no reasonable claim to remain unseen.
- Personal data is treated as an extension of the same principle: collection and use require clear, informed consent or proper legal process.
- Secondary use of data beyond what was disclosed, or bulk retention of ordinary activity, requires fresh justification.
- Privacy is a negative right against intrusion, not a positive right to conceal everything or to demand that others ignore what is openly displayed.

#### Rationale
Privacy, in both its traditional and informational forms, protects the ability to maintain a personal domain free from unwanted interference. Without it, agency shrinks. If the state or private actors can enter the home, search the person, or compile detailed records of private life at will, the individual no longer sets the terms of access. That is a form of control even when no physical injury occurs.

The classic core remains the home, the person, and private papers or effects. Entry or search in these domains requires consent or a specific, evidence-based legal process. General warrants, open-ended authorization, or routine intrusion without individualized suspicion are incompatible with the right. This is ordinary negative-rights protection applied to physical space and personal boundaries.

Public space is different. What a person knowingly exposes to open view, like walking on a street, speaking in a park, or attending a public gathering, carries no reasonable expectation of privacy against ordinary observation. The problem arises when technology turns transient public presence into permanent, searchable, identifiable records. Continuous bulk recording, facial-recognition databases, and long-term tracking change the character of public life. They concentrate power and create lasting exposure that ordinary observation never did. That shift justifies tighter limits even though the initial setting is public.

Informational privacy follows the same logic. Personal data is not simply “out there” for the taking. Collection by the state or by companies requires clear notice and genuine consent, or a proper legal process when consent is absent. Hidden collection, vague terms, or designs that make refusal impractical undermine the condition that makes consent meaningful. Where a service is difficult to avoid, the demand for transparency and workable choices is higher. Data-driven services are not prohibited; it requires that they rest on agreement rather than default extraction.

State power receives the strictest application. Targeted collection under warrant for specific investigations fits inside rights-based enforcement. Open-ended bulk collection, indefinite retention of ordinary activity, or repurposing data for unrelated goals does not. Once detailed records on the population become routine, the capacity for abuse grows and becomes hard to reverse. A 2022 FISA Court opinion found that FBI personnel ran more than 278,000 noncompliant queries of Section 702 data, including queries tied to protest participants and campaign donors.

Privacy is therefore handled as a side-constraint on intrusion. It protects the home, the person, private spaces, and personal information from non-consensual access. It does not guarantee invisibility in public, nor does it block focused investigation of actual rights violations. The practical goal is to keep control with the individual by default while still permitting necessary, limited responses when force, fraud, or serious harm is at issue.

## Surveillance State

>[!tldr] In short
> Facial recognition and social credit are opt-in only. Cameras in public are fine, but companies must notify people and get their consent before using that footage for anything beyond viewing. Tech-government partnerships are fine if rights and consumer protections are respected.

#### Position
Facial recognition by companies is always opt-in and never mandated, though people in public have no reasonable expectation of privacy; government use of public-space data for public protection follows [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Surveillance and Digital Technology|Section 10]]. Social credit systems are opt-in and never mandated. Partnerships between tech companies and government are acceptable as long as sentient rights are respected and consumer protections are upheld. Surveillance is acceptable in public areas. Any further data mining of public surveillance data by a company requires that the person be notified and opt into sharing before the data is used in any way beyond viewing.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **NSA bulk phone records:** a U.S. appeals court ruled in 2020 that the program exposed by Edward Snowden was unlawful. BBC News (2020) – [NSA surveillance exposed by Snowden ruled unlawful](https://www.bbc.com/news/technology-54013527)

> [!question]- "If public spaces carry no expectation of privacy, why require consent for data mining?"
> Being seen in public is different from having that footage processed, profiled, and traded. Viewing public surveillance is permitted; turning it into a data product about a specific person requires notice and opt-in consent. This keeps public safety intact while protecting individuals from commercial exploitation of their data.

> [!question]- "Doesn't public-area surveillance build a surveillance state?"
> Viewing public spaces is permitted because there is no reasonable expectation of privacy there. Mass, open-ended collection and permanent surveillance systems that turn public presence into lasting records face a higher bar ([[#Privacy Rights|Privacy Rights]]), and any use of the data beyond viewing requires notice and opt-in. Facial recognition by companies and social credit systems are opt-in only.

#### Key Elements
- Facial recognition opt-in only, never mandated.
- Social credit systems opt-in only, never mandated.
- Tech-government partnerships allowed if sentient rights and consumer protections are upheld.
- Public-area surveillance permitted.
- Notification and opt-in consent required before public surveillance data is used beyond viewing.

#### Rationale
The framework's privacy rule distinguishes public presence from private data. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches, and the Supreme Court has recognized that new technologies can intrude on privacy even when data is gathered in public or held by third parties, as in Carpenter v. United States on cell-site location records and Kyllo v. United States on thermal imaging. Consent-based rules for data use beyond viewing follow that line while allowing ordinary public surveillance for safety.

> [!info]- What is a social credit system?
> A social credit system scores individuals based on behavior, finances, or compliance, and attaches rewards or penalties to the score. The position here allows such systems only on a voluntary, opt-in basis.

## Due Process and Rule of Law

>[!tldr] In short
> Everyone answers for what they do, and people with more power answer to a higher standard. Assets can be taken only to settle broken contracts or unpaid debts between private parties. Bail is viewed with suspicion, and judges are vetted more strictly so that repeat offenders are stopped.

#### Position
All people are held liable for their actions. Higher elected officials and those with great responsibility are offered more in salary but held to higher standards. Assets can be forfeited when contracts are broken or debts go unpaid between private actors. The bail system is viewed with skepticism. Judges are vetted far more stringently so that punishment is applied appropriately, because repeat offenders causing great damage is treated as something that should never happen. Death penalty and incarceration positions are set out in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Criminal Justice & Security]].

> [!question]- "Does limiting asset forfeiture to private disputes leave the state without tools against criminals?"
> No. [[Glossary#Rights forfeiture|Forfeiture]] here is a remedy between private parties: a broken contract or an unpaid debt allows the injured party to recover assets. Punishment of criminals is handled through the criminal sentencing, restitution, and compelled-labor rules in Criminal Justice & Security, where offenders pay their victims from their own accounts and assets.

#### Key Elements
- Universal liability for one's actions.
- Higher pay paired with higher standards for officials with greater responsibility.
- Asset forfeiture limited to broken contracts and unpaid debts between private actors.
- Skepticism of the bail system.
- Stricter vetting of judges and sentencing that prevents repeat offending.
- Death penalty and incarceration handled in Criminal Justice & Security.

#### Rationale
Equal liability under law is the foundation of a high-trust society, and power that escapes accountability invites abuse. Greater authority therefore carries a stricter standard. Asset forfeiture in the civil-enforcement sense has drawn constitutional limits, including the Supreme Court's holding in Timbs v. Indiana that the Excessive Fines Clause applies to the states; restricting forfeiture to private contract and debt disputes keeps the remedy tied to an actual injured party. Federal pretrial release law already allows detention of defendants who pose a danger, which the Court upheld in United States v. Salerno. Repeat offending is a documented pattern in national recidivism data, which is why judicial vetting and sentencing aim at preventing it. Among prisoners released from 34 states in 2012, 62% were rearrested within three years and 71% within five years.

> [!info]- What is asset forfeiture?
> Asset forfeiture is the legal transfer of property to another party because of its connection to a wrong. Civil forfeiture lets the government take property tied to suspected crime, often without a conviction. The position here allows forfeiture only to satisfy broken contracts or unpaid debts between private parties.

## Pretrial Detention and Bail

>[!tldr] In short
> Defendants are released before trial unless a judge finds they are dangerous or likely to flee. Money does not decide who stays in jail, and those held must be tried within 90 days or released while the charge stands.

#### Position
An accused person has not been convicted and keeps their rights until a court finds otherwise. Pretrial detention is allowed only on an individualized judicial finding that the person poses a serious danger to others or a real risk of flight, made at a prompt hearing with counsel. Detention is not set by ability to pay. Detained defendants receive swift trials so that detention does not become punishment before conviction.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **Kalief Browder:** held at Rikers Island for years without trial after a robbery arrest, reportedly beaten and kept in solitary, his case was dismissed in 2013; he killed himself in 2015. NPR (2015) – [Kalief Browder, Jailed For Years Without Trial, Kills Himself](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2015/06/08/412842780/kalief-browder-jailed-for-years-at-rikers-island-without-trial-commits-suicide)
> - **Waukesha, 2021:** the man charged in the Christmas parade attack had been released on \$1,000 bail, an amount the county's district attorney later called inappropriately low. NPR (2021) – [Wisconsin Christmas parade case raises questions around bail](https://www.npr.org/2021/11/24/1059041774/wisconsin-christmas-parade-case-raises-questions-around-bail)

> [!question]- "Won't releasing defendants endanger the public?"
> Those who pose a serious danger are detained. The rule removes wealth as the deciding factor: a dangerous person with money is not released, and a harmless person without money is not held.

> [!question]- "How swift is a swift trial?"
> A detained defendant must be brought to trial within 90 days of arrest. Only the defendant can ask for more time; the prosecution and the court cannot extend the deadline on their own. If the state misses it, the defendant is released pending trial under ordinary conditions, and the charge stands. Federal law already sets a similar clock. The Speedy Trial Act requires an indictment within 30 days of arrest and a trial within 70 days of indictment or first appearance, though it excludes many periods of delay and its remedy is dismissal rather than release. Detention never becomes an open-ended punishment before conviction.

#### Key Elements
- Presumption of release before trial.
- Detention only on an individual finding of danger or flight risk.
- Prompt hearing with counsel.
- No detention based on inability to pay.
- Swift trials for those detained: trial within 90 days of arrest, extendable only at the defendant's request; a missed deadline means release pending trial, and the charge stands.

#### Rationale
At midyear 2023, 70% of people held in local jails had not been convicted of the crime they were held for. In *United States v. Salerno* (1987) the Supreme Court upheld federal pretrial detention based on a judicial finding of dangerousness after an adversarial hearing, which is the structure this position adopts. Speed matters because people admitted to jail from July 2022 to June 2023 spent an average of 32 days in custody before release.

## Civil Asset Forfeiture

>[!tldr] In short
> The state does not take property from people who have not been convicted, except assets of foreign cartels formally designated as war enemies or Foreign Terrorist Organizations, which are seized as enemy property on the combatant track, and true contraband, which may be seized and destroyed or returned to owners. Domestic gangs and undesignated actors still require a conviction. One further stated exception: under the [[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]], animals are removed from animal agriculture, fishing, breeding mills, and testing to state-run sanctuaries without a conviction, and the businesses are compelled to convert, divest, or liquidate, with no compensation to owners or shareholders. After a conviction, the offender's assets go to the victims first; leftover goes to a victim restitution fund, then the general treasury — no seizing agency keeps what it seizes. The government must prove the link by clear and convincing evidence, give notice within 7 days, and hold a hearing within 30 days before any sale.

#### Position
Property is not taken by the state without a conviction. No property may be seized or forfeited absent a criminal conviction for the offense the seizure is tied to, except as set out below for designated enemy assets and true contraband. The government bears the burden of proving that the property is tied to that offense by clear and convincing evidence, not a preponderance of the evidence. Owners must receive written notice within 7 days of any seizure and have the right to contest it at a hearing held within 30 days; no seized property may be sold or transferred before that hearing is decided. Assets are forfeited when a person is convicted of a rights violation, and they go first to restitution for the victims. After victims are fully paid, leftover forfeited assets go to a victim restitution fund, then the general treasury; no seizing agency keeps what it seizes. True contraband — items illegal to possess — may be seized without a conviction and destroyed, or returned to rightful owners if stolen. Lawful property is never forfeited without a conviction, and an owner may contest disputed status at the 7-day/30-day hearing. Assets of foreign cartels formally designated as war enemies or Foreign Terrorist Organizations are seized as enemy property on the combatant track, without a domestic civilian conviction, consistent with [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Mexico and Latin America|Foreign Policy & International Relations]]. Domestic gangs and undesignated actors still require a conviction and full due process. The one further exception is the [[9. Energy & Environment#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]: animals are removed from animal agriculture, fishing, breeding mills, and testing to state-run sanctuaries without a conviction, and those businesses are compelled to convert, divest, or liquidate, with no compensation to owners or shareholders; after workers and creditors are paid, the proceeds go to the sanctuaries, and a family on a liquidated farm keeps its house and house lot. I accept that this breaks the property-rights part of my negative-rights view; the conviction rule stands for everything else. Between private parties, assets can be forfeited when contracts are broken or debts go unpaid, through ordinary legal process.

## Role of Juries

>[!tldr] In short
> Conviction requires a unanimous jury of at least twelve, in civil and criminal cases alike. A guilty plea cannot avoid the mandatory death penalty, and every plea offer is made on the record. Juries can nullify, but a nullification acquittal in a mandatory-death-penalty case may be retried once before a different jury.

#### Position
**Unanimity.** A unanimous jury is required to convict. Reciprocity limits the protection a rights violator receives in sentencing and treatment once guilt is established. It does not lower the procedural bar for establishing guilt.

**Plea deals.** Pleading guilty to murder does not reduce or avoid the mandatory death penalty. A plea may affect only non-capital elements of a sentence. Every plea offer must be made on the record, with the offered terms disclosed to the court.

**Civil juries.** The same unanimity rule applies in every case, civil or criminal. Civil cases keep their lower standard of proof, but not a lower verdict threshold.

**Jury nullification.** Juries have the power to nullify. Judges instruct juries to follow the laws given.

**Retrial after nullification.** If a jury acquits through nullification in a case carrying a mandatory death penalty, the state may retry the case once before a different jury. This knowingly departs from the Fifth Amendment double jeopardy rule, under which an acquittal is final.

**Jury size.** Twelve is the minimum jury size, required alongside the unanimity rule. Six-person panels are not permitted.

> [!question]- "Doesn't a unanimity rule let a single holdout block justice?"
> The rule protects the innocent first. A conviction can lead to the capital track, so the standard of proof has to be met for every juror, not most of them. A hung jury allows a retrial; a wrongful conviction cannot be undone after execution.

> [!note]- Revision threshold
> If the jury acquittal rate in capital cases reaches 5% sustained over a five-year window, the nullification stance is revisited: from allowing nullification with judges not disclosing it, toward judges instructing juries that they may not nullify. Until that threshold is met, the current nullification and retrial rules stand. For context, recent federal jury acquittal across all offenses runs roughly 11–14% of jury trials (AO/Pew), a background rate rather than a nullification measure.

#### Key Elements
- Unanimous jury required to convict.
- Guilt for the act itself is decided by the unanimous jury; whether agency was absent is a separate finding by a forensic panel and judge ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Insanity Defense|Insanity Defense]]).
- Pleading guilty to murder does not reduce or avoid the mandatory death penalty; pleas affect only non-capital elements.
- Every plea offer on the record, with terms disclosed to the court.
- Same unanimity rule in civil and criminal cases; civil cases keep their lower standard of proof.
- Juries have the power to nullify; judges instruct juries to follow the laws given.
- One retrial before a different jury after a nullification acquittal in a mandatory-death-penalty case.
- A capital-case acquittal rate of 5% sustained over five years triggers a rethink of nullification, toward judges instructing juries they may not nullify.
- Twelve is the minimum jury size; six-person panels are not permitted.

#### Rationale
Twelve-person juries are more accurate and more representative. In Saks's 1977 mock-jury studies, as reported in *Ballew v. Georgia*, 12-person juries reached the correct verdict 83% of the time, compared with 69% for 6-person juries, in the student experiment, and 71% against 57% in the experiment with former jurors. In *Ballew v. Georgia*, the Supreme Court calculated that a group making up 10% of the community would be missing from 53% of 6-person juries; the same math puts that figure at about 28% for 12-person juries. A 1997 meta-analysis of 17 studies (Saks & Marti) found no significant difference in verdicts by jury size, but larger juries included more minority members and deliberated longer.

## Voting and Elections

>[!tldr] In short
> Voters must show ID, mail-in ballots are fully authenticated and encrypted, and [[Glossary#Felony and misdemeanor|felons]] cannot vote. A constitutional amendment would require passing a fair civics exam to vote. Political money is limited to individual donations, and corporations do not get the voice of people.

#### Position
Voter ID is federally mandated. Mail-in voting is reformed for complete authentication of the voter and transmitted through encrypted channels with world-class cybersecurity. Felons cannot vote; a felony is any offense worth two or more marks, or any capital-tier crime ([[Glossary#Felony and misdemeanor|Felony and misdemeanor]]). Voting is not treated as a universal right. A constitutional amendment requires voters to pass a bipartisan, unbiased, thorough examination of political and social knowledge. Those who fail to meet the standard of knowledge of the government and the laws they vote on cannot vote. Campaign finance is reformed because money in politics is dangerous and disadvantages small candidates. Corporations do not have the voice of people, contrary to Citizens United, and finance is limited to individual donations rather than organized donation campaigns. These limits apply to contributions to candidates and campaigns, not to independent speech.

> [!question]- "Is a voter knowledge test a return to Jim Crow literacy tests?"
> The history is real: literacy tests were administered selectively to exclude Black voters, which is why federal law bans them today, and any knowledge test has to answer to that record. The test proposed here is bipartisan, unbiased, and applied to everyone through a constitutional amendment, not through discretionary local officials. Its purpose is that those choosing the government understand the government and the laws they vote on. The test rests on principle, not on evidence that it would improve outcomes.

> [!question]- "Why can't felons vote, even after their sentence?"
> Some consequences of forfeiture outlast the sentence by rule, and the bar on felons voting is one of them ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#General Rules of Forfeiture|General Rules of Forfeiture]]). A felony here means any offense worth two or more marks, or any capital-tier crime; a misdemeanor, worth one mark or a fine without a mark, does not bar voting ([[Glossary#Felony and misdemeanor|Felony and misdemeanor]]).

> [!question]- "Don't limits on political money restrict free speech?"
> The limits apply to contributions to candidates and campaigns, not to independent speech. Finance is limited to individual donations because corporations do not have the voice of people, and money in politics disadvantages small candidates.

> [!question]- "Doesn't voter ID suppress turnout, especially among minority voters?"
> The best evidence says it does not. A nationwide study of 1.6 billion voter-file observations from 2008 to 2018 found that strict ID laws had "no negative effect on registration or turnout, overall or for any group defined by race, gender, age, or party affiliation." The same study found no effect on fraud, actual or perceived, so ID is justified here as a verification standard for every ballot, not as a fraud cure. The knowledge exam is a different matter: unlike ID, it is designed to exclude voters who fail it, and pass rates may differ by group. That is why its pass rates are published broken down by race, sex, age, and party, and any question that one group fails at a markedly higher rate is reviewed by the bipartisan commission for bias.

> [!question]- "Who writes the voter knowledge exam?"
> A commission with equal membership from both major parties writes and maintains it. The question bank is published in advance as a public study guide, questions test facts about the structure of government and the laws voters decide on, never opinions, and the exam is free and can be retaken. Any citizen can challenge a question in court as biased. The commission audits the question bank each cycle, and pass rates are published so anyone can check whether the test is excluding a group.

#### Key Elements
- Federal voter ID mandate.
- Mail-in voting with full authentication and encrypted transmission.
- No [[Glossary#Felony and misdemeanor|felon]] voting (felony: two or more marks, or any capital-tier crime).
- Constitutional amendment requiring a bipartisan civics and knowledge exam to vote.
- Exam pass rates published by race, sex, age, and party; any question one group fails at a markedly higher rate is reviewed by the bipartisan commission for bias.
- Campaign contributions limited to individual donations.
- Opposition to Citizens United's treatment of corporate political spending.

#### Rationale
Elections are only legitimate if every vote is cast by an eligible, verified voter. The Supreme Court upheld photo ID requirements in Crawford v. Marion County Election Board, and states have adopted ID rules widely. Nationwide panel data from 2008 to 2018 found strict ID laws did not lower registration or turnout for any group; ID is justified here as a verification standard for every ballot, not as a cure for measured fraud. The Court held in Richardson v. Ramirez that states may disenfranchise felons under the Fourteenth Amendment. Federal law currently bans literacy tests nationwide, and the Court has struck down conditions such as poll taxes, which is why a knowledge requirement is proposed through constitutional amendment rather than statute. The requirement rests on principle; the literacy-test history is why it is built around bipartisan authorship, a public question bank, and audit. Citizens United removed limits on independent corporate political spending; limiting finance to individual donations restores elections to individual citizens.

> [!info]- What did Citizens United decide?
> In Citizens United v. FEC (2010), the Supreme Court held that the First Amendment bars the government from limiting independent political expenditures by corporations, unions, and other associations. Direct contributions to candidates remain subject to limits.

## Election Administration

>[!tldr] In short
> Elections require ID, authenticated mail ballots, and clean voter rolls, with money limited to individual donations. The rules are set before each election.

#### Position
Elections are administered to ensure that only eligible voters vote and that every valid ballot is counted. Voters show identification, mail-in ballots are fully authenticated and encrypted, and people convicted of [[Glossary#Felony and misdemeanor|felonies]] are removed from the rolls because they cannot vote. Political money is limited to individual donations to candidates and campaigns. Election procedures are fixed in law before each election rather than changed during it.

> [!question]- "Doesn't strict administration make voting harder?"
> Identification and authentication protect each lawful vote from being diluted by an unlawful one. Every eligible citizen can meet the requirements, and confidence in the count is what makes the result legitimate.

#### Key Elements
- Voter ID at the polls.
- Authenticated, encrypted mail-in ballots.
- [[Glossary#Felony and misdemeanor|Felons]] removed from voter rolls.
- Individual donations only.
- Rules fixed before the election.

#### Rationale
Under the Constitution and federal law, the President is chosen by 538 electors, and a majority of 270 electoral votes is required to win. The administration rules above apply within that structure, and each state chooses its presidential electors by ranked-choice voting ([[#Ranked-Choice Voting|Ranked-Choice Voting]]); the voting rights rules are set out in [[#Voting and Elections]]. Term length, the absence of term limits, and the three-year election cycle for every elected office are set out in [[#Term Limits]].

## Ranked-Choice Voting

>[!tldr] In short
> I require ranked-choice voting for every election in the country, federal, state, and local. It runs on paper ballots, published rounds, fixed rules, and clear notice that partly ranked ballots can be exhausted. Its effects on turnout and polarization are mixed, and it does not guarantee a majority of all ballots.

#### Position
I require ranked-choice voting for every election in the country: federal, state, and local. Because the Constitution gives Congress power over federal elections but not over state and local ones, I enact the requirement through a constitutional amendment, as I do the voter knowledge exam ([[#Voting and Elections|Voting and Elections]]). It runs on paper ballots, every round of the count is published, the rules are fixed in law before the election ([[#Election Administration|Election Administration]]), and voters are told plainly that a ballot ranking only some candidates may be exhausted before the final round.

> [!question]- "Doesn't ranked-choice voting guarantee a majority winner?"
> No, only a majority of ballots still counted in the final round. Burnett and Kogan examined more than 600,000 ballots from four local elections and found that in every one the winner received less than a majority of all ballots cast, because exhausted ballots dropped out of the count. That limit is why exhaustion is disclosed to voters rather than hidden.

> [!question]- "Isn't it too confusing for voters?"
> The evidence from Maine says mostly not. In the 2018 race for Maine's 2nd Congressional District, the Republican led the first round 46.33% to 45.58% and the Democrat won after transfers, 50.62% to 49.38%. Only 621 ballots, 0.21% of the total, were exhausted through apparent confusion. The framework's voter knowledge exam ([[#Voting and Elections|Voting and Elections]]) also means every voter has shown knowledge of the structure of government.

> [!question]- "Does it raise turnout or reduce polarization?"
> The evidence is mixed. A 2024 study using administrative turnout records found substantially higher turnout and more direct voter contact where ranked-choice voting is used, while earlier work by Kimball and Anthony found negligible turnout effects outside the runoffs it replaces. The National Conference of State Legislatures summarizes research on Maine as showing only a modest reduction in polarization. I do not require it for promised effects; I require it because it lets voters rank every candidate and ends the spoiler problem.

> [!question]- "Doesn't a national requirement override the states?"
> Yes, and I accept that. Seventeen states had banned ranked-choice voting by 2025. I already set national rules for how votes are cast and verified, such as voter ID and authenticated mail ballots; how votes are counted is part of the same integrity standard, and a vote should not count differently depending on the state it is cast in. States keep running their own elections under that standard ([[#Federalism|Federalism]]). Voters have also shown support where it was tested: Alaska's 2024 measure to repeal its system failed by 743 votes after a recount, 50.12% to 49.88%.

#### Key Elements
- Required for every federal, state, and local election, by constitutional amendment.
- Paper ballots and published round-by-round results.
- Rules fixed in law before the election.
- Ballot exhaustion disclosed to voters in advance.
- Required on principle, not on promised turnout or moderation effects.

#### Rationale
I fix the integrity rules for every election, such as identification, authentication, and rules set in advance, and the counting method is part of those rules, so ranked-choice voting is required everywhere. Ranked-choice voting also reduces the spoiler problem that keeps small candidates from competing, a concern the framework already raises about money in politics ([[#Voting and Elections|Voting and Elections]]). Against that, it does not guarantee a true majority of all ballots, its counts take longer, and its measured effects on turnout and polarization are mixed. I accept those trade-offs for a count that lets every voter rank every candidate.

> - R Street Institute (2021) – [An Analysis of Ranked Choice Voting in Maine](https://rstreet.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/Final-Short-106.pdf)

## Direct Democracy and Citizen Initiatives

>[!tldr] In short
> States and localities may offer citizen initiatives and referendums. A constitutional amendment creates a national initiative for federal statutes and amendments, reaching the ballot with signatures from 1% (statute) or 2% (amendment) of qualified voters within 18 months. Measures pass by more than half of the votes cast with no minimum turnout, and amendments also need a majority of the states, 26 of 50, each counted by its own voters. No initiative can override protected rights, and every measure faces court review. Only exam-qualified voters with ID take part, under rules fixed in advance, and outcomes rest with those who vote.

#### Position
Citizen initiatives and referendums are supported at the state and local level, and a national citizen initiative is supported for federal statutes and constitutional amendments, enacted through a constitutional amendment. Each state decides whether to offer its own initiatives and sets its own signature thresholds. No initiative, state or national, can override the rights the framework protects, such as negative rights, equal treatment, and due process. Every measure is subject to court review like any other law ([[#Due Process and Rule of Law|Due Process and Rule of Law]]). Only voters who qualify under the voter knowledge exam and show identification may sign petitions and vote on measures. The rules for each measure are fixed in law before signatures are gathered ([[#Voting and Elections|Voting and Elections]]; [[#Election Administration|Election Administration]]). A national initiative reaches the ballot with signatures from 1% of qualified voters in the last federal election for a federal statute, or 2% for a constitutional amendment, gathered within 18 months. These thresholds are the framework's own choice, scaled from the Swiss model rather than taken from it. A measure passes by a simple majority of votes cast, more than half, with blank and spoiled ballots not counted and no minimum turnout. A constitutional amendment by initiative needs a double majority: a national popular majority plus a majority of the states, 26 of 50. A state counts toward that majority when its own voters approve the measure, as Swiss cantons do.

> [!question]- "Doesn't direct democracy let majorities vote away minority rights?"
> It can, and Switzerland shows it. Swiss men rejected women's federal suffrage in 1959 by 66.9% and approved it only in 1971, with 65.7% in favor. The canton of Appenzell Innerrhoden voted it down again in 1990, and the Federal Supreme Court imposed it on 27 November 1990 under the constitution's equality clause. In 2009 voters approved a ban on building minarets with 57.5% of the vote. That record is why initiatives here cannot override protected rights and stay subject to court review.

> [!question]- "Won't voters simply pass anything that is put to them?"
> The Swiss record says no. Of 237 federal popular initiatives put to a vote since 1891, only 26 were accepted by the people and the cantons, about 11%. Voters reject most proposals, which makes the initiative a check on the legislature rather than a replacement for it.

> [!question]- "Doesn't low turnout let a small group decide?"
> No. Average turnout in Swiss federal votes in 2009 was 46.1%, though the minaret vote that year drew 53.76%. There is no minimum turnout: a measure passes by more than half of the votes cast, with blank and spoiled ballots not counted. The goal is that the vote is held and open to every qualified voter, even if only a small share takes part. Those unwilling to vote are subject to the choices of those who do, and the outcome rests with the participants.

> [!question]- "How can there be a national initiative under the Constitution?"
> Only through a constitutional amendment. Article I vests federal legislative power in Congress, and Article V allows amendments only through Congress or a convention of the states, so the national initiative is created by an amendment ratified in the ordinary way. Once in place, it lets citizens propose federal statutes and constitutional amendments directly. A proposal reaches the ballot with signatures from 1% of qualified voters in the last federal election for a statute, or 2% for an amendment, gathered within 18 months. These figures are the framework's own choice, scaled from Switzerland's 100,000 signatures in 18 months, not a Swiss rule.

> [!question]- "Wouldn't a national initiative let big states outvote small ones?"
> Not on the Constitution. An amendment by initiative needs a double majority: a national popular majority and a majority of the states, 26 of 50, as Switzerland requires a majority of the people and of the cantons. A state counts toward that majority when its own voters approve the measure, just as the result of the popular vote in a canton determines that canton's vote. Article V already protects the states' role by requiring three-quarters of them to ratify amendments proposed by Congress. A federal statute by initiative needs a national popular majority and stays subject to the same rights limits and court review.

#### Key Elements
- Initiatives and referendums supported at the state and local level.
- A national citizen initiative for federal statutes and constitutional amendments, created by constitutional amendment.
- Signatures from 1% of qualified voters in the last federal election for a statute and 2% for an amendment, gathered within 18 months (the framework's own figures, scaled from the Swiss model).
- Passage by a simple majority of votes cast; blank and spoiled ballots not counted; no minimum turnout.
- Amendments by initiative need a national popular majority plus a majority of the states (26 of 50); a state counts when its own voters approve.
- Each state sets whether to offer its own initiatives and its signature thresholds.
- No initiative overrides protected rights; all measures are subject to court review.
- Only exam-qualified voters with ID may sign and vote.
- Rules fixed in law before signatures are gathered.

#### Rationale
Switzerland's system is the most developed model. A popular initiative needs 100,000 valid signatures within 18 months to force a national vote on a constitutional amendment. An optional referendum needs 50,000 signatures within 100 days to put a new law to a vote, and every constitutional amendment requires a double majority of voters and cantons. In the United States, 26 states provide a statewide initiative, referendum, or both. In Pacific States Telephone & Telegraph Co. v. Oregon (1912), the Supreme Court declined to hear a claim that Oregon's initiative process violated the Guarantee Clause, treating the question as one for Congress, so state initiatives stand. Initiatives fit the framework's federalism and its preference for power held close to citizens ([[#Federalism|Federalism]]). The Swiss record also shows the risk: majorities have voted on the rights of minorities, and only a court restored women's vote in one canton. The framework therefore pairs the initiative with firm rights limits and the same voter qualifications that apply to every election. At the national level the Constitution gives no initiative power today. So the framework creates one by amendment, with signature thresholds of 1% and 2% of qualified voters in the last federal election, the framework's own figures scaled from the Swiss model. The double majority for amendments by initiative, 26 of 50 states counted by each state's own vote, keeps the states' role that Article V protects. Turnout is not a condition of validity: a vote open to every qualified voter is decided by more than half of the votes cast by those who take part.
