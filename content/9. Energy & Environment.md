## Core Principles
Practical, gradual transition guided by free-market innovation and sentient-rights considerations. Avoid disruptive mandates or taxpayer-funded subsidies. Prioritize human (and herbivore) welfare over apocalyptic framing while conserving nature where it does not conflict with rights or development.

## Climate Change

#### Position
Climate change is both natural and human-induced. The threat is real but not apocalyptic. Policy supports the long-term wellbeing of sentient individuals, but not to the point of disrupting and upending current energy supply chains. The transition to greener infrastructure is phased in slowly. In an ideal, though unrealistic, society, climate change would be less severe because animal agriculture would be eliminated.

> [!question]- "If climate change is real, why not act faster?"
> Rapid transition would disrupt energy supply chains that sentient beings depend on, causing harm of its own. A slow, phased shift addresses the problem without that disruption.

> [!question]- "What about catastrophic tail risk?"
> Present agents may not impose extreme, unjustified risk of death on future sentient beings ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Duties to Future Generations|Duties to Future Generations]]). If credible evidence shows that risk, the phased timeline can be accelerated, with the burden of proof on the rule.

#### Key Elements
- Climate change both natural and anthropogenic.
- Real but not apocalyptic.
- Policy aimed at long-term sentient wellbeing without upending energy supply.
- Slow, phased shift to greener infrastructure.
- Ideally, elimination of animal agriculture would reduce human-induced warming.

#### Rationale
The IPCC attributes recent warming principally to human activity while projecting a range of outcomes that depend on emissions. Livestock is a significant source of greenhouse gas emissions (about 12% of human-caused emissions) according to the FAO, which links the sentientist case against animal agriculture to the climate question. Measured transition balances long-term wellbeing against present needs.

##### Sources
- IPCC – [AR6 Synthesis Report: Climate Change 2023](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/)
- Food and Agriculture Organization – [Livestock's Long Shadow (2006)](https://www.fao.org/4/a0701e/a0701e00.htm)
- Food and Agriculture Organization – [New FAO report maps pathways towards lower livestock emissions (2023)](https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/new-fao-report-maps-pathways-towards-lower-livestock-emissions/en/)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Climate change is real and partly human-caused, but not apocalyptic. The country shifts slowly to greener infrastructure without disrupting energy supply. Ending animal agriculture would be the ideal fix.

## Adaptation and Disaster Policy

#### Position
Climate change is real but gradual, so adaptation is handled as a practical matter. Taxpayer subsidies for risk, including subsidized insurance for building in high-risk areas, require opt-in funding like any other subsidy. Owners bear the risks of where and how they build, and private insurance prices those risks.

> [!question]- "What happens to people hit by a disaster?"
> Emergency response, rescue, and public safety remain government functions. What changes is the long-term subsidy of rebuilding in high-risk places, which shifts costs to taxpayers who did not choose the risk.

#### Key Elements
- Adaptation treated as practical, not apocalyptic.
- Subsidies only through opt-in funding.
- The 18 percent cap on annual flood insurance premium increases removed.
- Owners bear location risk.
- Emergency response remains a public function.

#### Rationale
The National Flood Insurance Program owed the Treasury \$22.525 billion after borrowing \$2 billion on February 10, 2025, according to the Congressional Research Service. Under FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0, about one-third of National Flood Insurance Program policyholders already pay full-risk premiums, and 9 percent will eventually need increases of more than 300 percent, according to the Government Accountability Office. Annual increases for most policyholders are limited to 18 percent by statute; GAO estimated it would take until 2037 for 95 percent of current policies to reach full-risk premiums, leaving a \$27 billion premium shortfall. The 18 percent cap delays full-risk pricing until 2037 and hides a 27 billion dollar shortfall, so it should be removed. Any assistance for policyholders comes only through opt-in funding, not taxpayer subsidies. The no-subsidy rule is set out in [[#Subsidies and Mandates]].

##### Sources
- U.S. Government Accountability Office – [Flood Insurance: FEMA's New Rate-Setting Methodology Improves Actuarial Soundness but Highlights Need for Broader Program Reform (GAO-23-105977, July 31, 2023)](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105977)
- Congressional Research Service (Horn) – [National Flood Insurance Program Borrowing Authority](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IN10784)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Climate adaptation is practical. Owners carry the risk of where they build, subsidies need opt-in funding, the 18 percent flood insurance cap is removed, and emergency rescue stays public.

## Energy Mix

#### Position
Fossil fuels remain for now while greater effort goes into renewables. Fossil fuels may continue in industries that benefit most from them, while other industries move to renewables that suit them better. Entire forced phase-outs are not supported; renewable technology advances through incentivization and innovation by private companies.

> [!question]- "Does keeping fossil fuels slow the transition?"
> Each industry uses the energy source that serves it best, and private innovation makes renewables competitive on their merits. Forced phase-outs impose costs before alternatives are ready.

#### Key Elements
- Fossil fuels remain where most beneficial.
- Greater effort into renewables.
- No forced phase-outs.
- Private innovation and incentivization drive renewable advancement.

#### Rationale
Fossil fuels still supply most U.S. energy, so abrupt phase-outs would disrupt the economy. Technology learning and competition have lowered the cost of utility-scale solar by about 85% since 2010, and unsubsidized solar and wind now compete on cost, which supports a transition led by innovation rather than mandate.

- U.S. Energy Information Administration – [U.S. Energy Facts Explained](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/us-energy-facts/)
- Lazard – [Levelized Cost of Energy+ (June 2025)](https://www.lazard.com/media/uounhon4/lazards-lcoeplus-june-2025.pdf)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Fossil fuels stay where they work best while renewables grow through private innovation. There are no forced phase-outs.

## Grid Reliability and Permitting

#### Position
A reliable grid comes first. No source is forced off the grid before replacements are ready, and permitting is streamlined so that new generation and transmission can connect. Regulation carries the burden of proof, and nuclear power in particular is deregulated and expanded.

> [!question]- "Doesn't faster permitting harm the environment?"
> Protections for sanctuary lands, protected species, and public health remain. Streamlining removes delay, not those standards.

#### Key Elements
- Reliability first.
- No forced phase-outs.
- Streamlined permitting.
- Nuclear deregulated.

#### Rationale
Nearly 2,600 GW of generation and storage capacity was waiting in U.S. interconnection queues at the end of 2023, according to Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. The North American Electric Reliability Corporation projects summer peak demand to rise by about 224 GW through 2035 and flags the MISO and PJM regions for seasonal shortfall risk. The no-phase-out rule is set out in [[#Energy Mix]].

##### Sources
- Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory – [Queued Up: 2024 Edition](https://emp.lbl.gov/publications/queued-2024-edition-characteristics)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Reliability comes first, no source is forced off early, and permitting is streamlined while environmental protections stay.

## Regulation and Conservation

#### Position
Environmental regulations exist on the specific grounds of conserving and maintaining sanctuary lands and herbivorous animal populations. Pollution and waste are regulated to a level that meets consumer and public health standards.

> [!question]- "Why protect herbivores specifically rather than all wildlife?"
> Under the framework, herbivores can live without causing harm to other sentient beings, so their populations and habitats receive priority protection. This follows from the sentientist ethic set out in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]].

#### Key Elements
- Regulations focused on sanctuary lands and herbivorous animal populations.
- Pollution and waste regulated to protect public health and consumer standards.

#### Rationale
Regulation is justified where it protects sentient beings from harm. Pollution harms public health, which grounds rules like the Clean Air Act. Conservation regulation centers on the populations the framework values most. About 12.9% of U.S. land and inland waters is currently protected and managed mainly for biodiversity, the baseline sanctuary policy builds from.

## Pollution and Liability

#### Position
Pollution that harms others, or directly imposes a severe, non-consensual risk of serious harm on them, violates their negative rights. It is regulated to a level that meets consumer and public-health standards, and polluters are liable for the harm they cause to people and property. Greenhouse gases count as pollution to the extent that emissions exceed the natural baseline; the natural contribution is not attributed to anyone. Greenhouse-gas rules follow the phased, non-disruptive transition set out in [[#Climate Change]]. Each environmental rule carries the burden of showing the harm it addresses.

> [!question]- "Doesn't pollution regulation conflict with the framework's skepticism of regulation?"
> No. Preventing serious environmental harm is one of the four domains where business regulation is legitimate. The burden-of-proof rule still applies: a pollution rule must show the harm it prevents, and rules that cannot are removed.

> [!question]- "How can anyone be liable for diffuse greenhouse-gas harm?"
> Where harm cannot be traced to a specific emitter, emissions above the natural baseline are handled by regulation on the phased timeline rather than by individual lawsuits. Traceable harm to persons or property remains liable in court.

#### Key Elements
- Harmful pollution violates the negative rights of those harmed.
- Pollution is regulated to consumer and public-health standards.
- Polluters are liable for harm to persons and property.
- Greenhouse gases count as pollution above the natural baseline.
- Greenhouse-gas rules follow the phased transition in Climate Change.
- Each rule must show the harm it addresses.

#### Rationale
Axiom 6 treats the direct imposition of severe, non-consensual risk as crossing the forfeiture threshold, and property rights protect owners against physical invasion of their land and bodies. Air pollution is the clearest documented case: EPA's peer-reviewed second prospective study estimated that the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments would prevent over 230,000 early deaths in 2020, with central benefits exceeding costs by more than 30 to one. Climate change is treated as both natural and human-induced, so only the human-caused share above the natural baseline is counted as pollution. Rules with demonstrated effects meet the burden of proof in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Infrastructure and Regulation|Domestic Policy & Governance]].

##### Sources
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency – [Benefits and Costs of the Clean Air Act 1990–2020, the Second Prospective Study](https://www.epa.gov/clean-air-act-overview/benefits-and-costs-clean-air-act-1990-2020-second-prospective-study)
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency – [Summary of the Clean Air Act](https://www.epa.gov/laws-regulations/summary-clean-air-act)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Pollution that harms people violates their rights. It is limited to what public-health standards allow, polluters pay for the damage, and greenhouse gases count as pollution above the natural baseline, regulated on the site's gradual climate timeline.

## Subsidies and Mandates

#### Position
Government subsidies for green technology mean taxpayer money, which is theft; the government subsidizes nothing unless taxpayers opt in. There are no mandates. Goals exist, and competition booms in the free market.

> [!question]- "Without subsidies, can clean technology compete?"
> Free-market competition drives advancement, and taxpayers who want to support green technology can opt in. Using mandatory taxes to pick winners is rejected.

#### Key Elements
- No government subsidies for green tech without taxpayer opt-in.
- Taxpayer funding without consent viewed as theft.
- No mandates (EVs, net-zero, and similar).
- Goals encouraged through free-market competition.

#### Rationale
Compelled funding of private industries violates property rights and distorts markets. Competition rewards technologies that deliver value at the lowest cost, which is how energy innovation advances most reliably. The Energy Information Administration counted \$183.3 billion (2022 dollars) in federal energy subsidies over FY 2016 to 2022, 46 percent of it for renewables, and 70 percent delivered through the tax code.

##### Sources
- U.S. Energy Information Administration – [Federal Financial Interventions and Subsidies in Energy in Fiscal Years 2016–2022](https://www.eia.gov/analysis/requests/subsidy/)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> No green-tech subsidies unless taxpayers opt in, and no mandates. The market drives clean energy forward.

## International Climate Agreements

#### Position
International efforts to raise awareness of human-induced climate change can be supported, but animal agriculture, a major human driver of climate change, is the one whose elimination also serves sentient-rights protections. In a more realistic view, the United States is not beholden to any accord that goes against the freedom of its people and nation. Accords that align with reasonable U.S. conservation and regulatory laws for the public safety of sentient beings can be followed. Policy that infringes on rights-respecting citizens is not mandated.

> [!question]- "Does withdrawing from accords undermine global climate efforts?"
> Accords consistent with reasonable U.S. conservation and safety law can be followed. What is rejected is binding commitments that override the freedom of Americans.

#### Key Elements
- Support for awareness efforts.
- Animal agriculture viewed as the main human driver in the ideal view.
- No accords that infringe American freedom or rights.
- Accords followed when consistent with reasonable U.S. conservation and safety law.

#### Rationale
Sovereignty applies to climate policy as to foreign policy. The Paris Agreement relies on nationally determined contributions, which shows that climate commitments can be set by each nation's own priorities.

- UNFCCC – [The Paris Agreement](https://unfccc.int/process-and-meetings/the-paris-agreement)
- Food and Agriculture Organization – [Livestock's Long Shadow (2006)](https://www.fao.org/4/a0701e/a0701e00.htm)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Awareness efforts are welcome, and ending animal agriculture is the ideal contribution. The U.S. follows climate accords only when they match reasonable U.S. law and do not restrict Americans' freedom.

## Nuclear Power

#### Position
Nuclear power is expanded because it has been proven safe. Safety remains crucial, as it is everywhere else.

> [!question]- "What about accidents like Chernobyl and Fukushima?"
> Accidents show why safety standards are crucial, not why nuclear power should be abandoned. Measured by deaths per unit of energy, nuclear is among the safest sources available.

#### Key Elements
- Expand nuclear power.
- Proven safe when properly managed.
- Safety standards remain crucial.

#### Rationale
Nuclear power has one of the lowest death rates per unit of energy produced of any source and emits very little carbon. It provides steady output that intermittent sources cannot, making it central to a gradual clean-energy transition.

## Nuclear Waste and Fuel Cycle

#### Position
Nuclear power is expanded, and spent fuel is managed under the same safety standards that make nuclear power safe. Storage and recycling technologies are allowed to develop under deregulation, and storage sites follow the development rule: low-density areas away from protected species.

> [!question]- "Isn't waste the unsolved problem of nuclear power?"
> Spent fuel has been stored safely for decades. Allowing recycling and permanent storage to develop removes regulatory obstacles rather than technical ones.

#### Key Elements
- Expansion of nuclear power.
- Safety standards for spent fuel.
- Recycling and storage allowed to develop.
- Sites in low-density areas.

#### Rationale
The Nuclear Waste Policy Act of 1982 makes the federal government responsible for permanent disposal of spent fuel (42 U.S.C. § 10131). About 97,061 metric tons of commercial spent fuel were in storage at the end of 2024, 46,864 tons in dry storage and 50,197 tons in pools, according to a U.S. Department of Energy inventory. Siting follows [[#Conservation vs Development]].

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Nuclear power expands, spent fuel is stored safely, recycling can develop, and storage sites go in low-density areas.

## Conservation vs Development

#### Position
Nature is preserved so long as sentient rights are not violated. Resource extraction generally takes place in quarries and lower-density areas such as deserts, sited away from the habitat of protected species such as the desert tortoise. Technology is, for the most part, built in and alongside rights-respecting nature rather than demolishing it.

> [!question]- "Doesn't any development harm wildlife?"
> Development is directed toward low-sentient-density areas to minimize harm, and integration with nature is preferred over demolition. The aim is to minimize rights violations, not halt development.

#### Key Elements
- Preserve nature where sentient rights are not violated.
- Extraction preferred in low-sentient-density areas such as deserts and quarries.
- Technology integrated with rights-respecting nature rather than demolishing it.

#### Rationale
The sentientist framework weighs harm to sentient beings, so where development occurs matters. Concentrating extraction in areas with few sentient animals limits the harm of growth.

## Water Resources and Rights

#### Position
Water rights are property rights, held, traded, and protected like other property. Polluting another's water is a rights violation under the pollution rule, with liability for the harm. Water is allocated through property and markets rather than political rationing.

> [!question]- "Won't markets leave people without water?"
> Markets direct water to its most valued uses and reward conservation. Public health standards for drinking water remain.

#### Key Elements
- Water rights as property.
- Pollution of water is a violation.
- Markets over rationing.
- Drinking-water health standards.

#### Rationale
Western states generally allocate water under prior appropriation, in which rights rest on beneficial use and seniority. Pollution liability is set out in [[#Pollution and Liability]].

- Cornell LII – [Prior appropriation doctrine](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/prior_appropriation_doctrine)

>[!tldr]- Summary

## Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat

#### Position
Animal agriculture should be banned outright and instantly. That is the rule that keeps the framework sentientist: farming sentient animals for food is a rights violation, so the ban itself is not phased in.

> [!note]- Realistic transition: how this gets done
> The ban is the position. In a realistic world, many systems — like taxes, like education, like all the systems that would be changing — would have some gradual period of change for most of these changes to happen. On the real earth, the phase-out of animal agriculture runs in these steps:
> 1. **Immediate halt to expansion.** No new animal farms, feedlots, slaughterhouses, or grazing permits, and no expansions of existing ones, from day one.
> 2. **No renewals; not an honored commitment.** Existing operating licenses and federal grazing permits are not renewed and may be revoked early. They are not an honored commitment, because animal agriculture is a rights violation. Existing grazing permits pay market-rate fees while any wind-down lasts.
> 3. **Breeding stops first.** Operations stop breeding new animals immediately, so herds shrink as existing animals are moved to sanctuary rather than slaughtered.
> 4. **Five-to-ten-year wind-down for existing operations.** Every existing animal-agriculture operation closes within a fixed window of five to ten years, with anti-cruelty and sentience rules enforced in full during the wind-down.
> 5. **Replacements scale up.** Regulatory barriers to lab-grown meat and plant-based food are cleared so supply grows as animal products leave the market. Any public support for the industry itself is opt-in only, like any subsidy; the industry does not get transition funding.
> 6. **Land and workers.** Former grazing and farm land shifts to crops, sanctuary land, and herbivore habitat. Displaced ranchers and farm workers get transition support — job placement or retraining. The industry itself does not.
> 7. **Enforcement.** Inspections, import bans on animal products, and the sentience-graded penalties in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Criminal Justice & Security]] apply to anyone who keeps operating past the deadline.
>
> This lag is the practical answer to "how would you get this done?" It is secondary and does not soften the ban.

Habitat protection centers on sanctuary lands and herbivore populations. No taxpayer money goes to farm or grazing businesses except through taxpayer opt-in, like any subsidy. No new grazing permits are issued on federal land. Existing permits are not renewed, may be revoked early, and pay market-rate fees during the wind-down; they are not an honored commitment, and grazing does not displace protected herbivore habitat.

> [!question]- "Doesn't this hurt ranchers?"
> Ranchers can operate on private land and compete without subsidies. Public sanctuary land serves the herbivore populations it protects. The ban on animal agriculture is the position; any real-world lag in how systems change does not rewrite that rule.

#### Key Elements
- Animal agriculture banned outright and instantly as the primary rule.
- Any gradual change in practice is a realistic aside, like taxes or education, not a softening of the ban.
- Sanctuary lands and herbivores prioritized.
- No taxpayer money to farm or grazing businesses except through taxpayer opt-in.
- No new federal grazing permits; existing permits are not renewed, may be revoked early, and pay market rates during the wind-down.
- Lab-grown meat is the replacement path.

#### Rationale
Killing sentient animals for food violates negative rights, so animal agriculture is banned on the same sentientist ground as other deliberate killings outside self-defense ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Animal Rights and Sentientism|Animal Rights and Sentientism]]). The 2025 federal grazing fee is \$1.35 per animal unit month (AUM) on about 18,000 Bureau of Land Management permits, while private grazing leases the same year ran from about \$11 to \$47.50 per AUM. About 12.9% of U.S. land and inland waters is protected and managed mainly for biodiversity, as cited in [[#Regulation and Conservation]]. Lab-grown meat is set out in [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Bioethics, Progress, & Technology]].

##### Sources
- Bureau of Land Management – [BLM, USDA Forest Service announce 2025 grazing fees](https://www.blm.gov/press-release/blm-usda-forest-service-announce-2025-grazing-fees)
- Montana State University Extension – [Grazing lease rates (USDA NASS)](https://www.montana.edu/extension/aglease/Grazing.html)
- U.S. Geological Survey – [PAD-US: Protected Areas Database of the United States](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/gap-analysis-project/science/pad-us-data-overview)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Animal agriculture is banned outright and instantly. In practice, like taxes or education, systems may change over a period, but that lag is not the position. Existing operations close on a five-to-ten-year wind-down with no new permits or expansions, existing grazing permits are not renewed and may be revoked early, and transition support goes to workers only. Habitat rules protect sanctuary lands and herbivores, farm and grazing businesses get no taxpayer money without opt-in, and lab-grown meat is the replacement.

## Commercial Fishing and Aquaculture

#### Position
Commercial fishing and aquaculture of sentient animals are banned outright and instantly. That is the rule that keeps the framework sentientist: catching or farming sentient animals for food is a rights violation, so the ban itself is not phased in. Rights protections for fish phase in as they are developed.

Like land-based animal farming in [[#Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat|Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat]], commercial fishing and aquaculture are banned as the position. On the realistic transition layer, existing wild-capture fishing and aquaculture operations close on a five-to-ten-year wind-down; new operations and expansions halt immediately; displaced workers get job placement or retraining, not the industry.

Fish follow the same sentience gradient as the murder rules in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Criminal Justice & Security]]: killing higher-sentience fish is on the capital track, and killing lower-sentience fish is punished and counts as a mark against the offender.

> [!note]- Realistic transition: how this gets done
> The ban is the position: wild commercial fishing and aquaculture of sentient animals are banned and abolished. In a realistic world, many systems — like taxes, like education, like all the systems that would be changing — would have some gradual period of change for most of these changes to happen. On the real earth, the phase-out runs in these steps:
> 1. **Immediate halt to new operations and expansion.** No new wild-capture fishing operations, vessels, or licenses, and no new fish farms, hatcheries, or expansions of existing operations, from day one.
> 2. **No renewals; not an honored commitment.** Existing commercial fishing licenses, quotas, and aquaculture licenses are not renewed and may be revoked early. They are not an honored commitment, because catching or farming sentient animals is a rights violation.
> 3. **Breeding and stocking stop first.** Aquaculture operations stop breeding and stocking new animals immediately, and remaining animals are moved to sanctuary rather than slaughtered or harvested.
> 4. **Five-to-ten-year wind-down for existing operations.** Every existing wild-capture fishing operation and aquaculture operation closes within a fixed window of five to ten years, with anti-cruelty and sentience rules enforced in full during the wind-down.
> 5. **Replacements scale up.** Regulatory barriers to cultivated seafood and plant-based seafood alternatives are cleared so supply grows as animal products leave the market. The industry itself gets no transition funding; any public support is opt-in only, like any subsidy.
> 6. **Workers.** Displaced fishing and aquaculture workers get transition support — job placement or retraining. The industry itself does not.
> 7. **Enforcement.** Inspections, the seafood import ban, bycatch bans, and the sentience-graded penalties in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Criminal Justice & Security]] apply to anyone who keeps operating past the deadline.
>
> This lag is the practical answer to "how would you get this done?" It is secondary and does not soften the ban.

Which fish are sentient follows the best available science. Shellfish and bivalves, such as oysters and mussels, are also covered by the ban, with no exception based on sentience consensus. Bycatch and incidental kills in non-fishing marine activity are banned. Imports of wild-caught or farmed seafood are banned. No taxpayer money supports fishing or fish-farming businesses except through taxpayer opt-in, like any subsidy. The long-term replacement is cultivated seafood produced through private innovation. In the bioengineering future of predation abolishment and the reordering of nature against predation and suffering, fish and the oceans are classified within that framework.

> [!question]- "Are fish even sentient?"
> Sentience is determined by the best available scientific consensus, as for any being ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sentience Determination|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]). Species that the best available science identifies as sentient are protected.

#### Key Elements
- Commercial fishing and aquaculture of sentient animals are banned outright and instantly.
- Like land-based animal farming, existing wild-capture fishing and aquaculture operations close on a five-to-ten-year wind-down. New operations and expansions halt immediately, and displaced workers get job placement or retraining, not the industry.
- Any gradual change in practice is a realistic aside, like taxes or education, not a softening of the ban.
- Fish follow the sentience gradient: higher sentience is capital, lower sentience is punished and tracked.
- The industry itself gets no transition funding; workers get job placement or retraining only.
- Shellfish and bivalves are covered by the ban, with no sentience-based exception.
- Bycatch and incidental kills in non-fishing marine activity are banned.
- Imports of wild-caught or farmed seafood are banned.
- No subsidies except through taxpayer opt-in.
- Cultivated seafood is the long-term replacement, through private innovation.
- Sentience by scientific consensus decides which species are covered.
- Fish and the oceans fall within the future bioengineering framework of predation abolishment.

#### Rationale
Animal agriculture and aquaculture are abolished because they kill sentient beings that hold negative rights; farmed and wild-caught sentient fish fall under the same rule. Because every catch is a killing of a sentient being, the ban is the position. Like land-based animal farming, existing wild-capture fishing and aquaculture operations close on a five-to-ten-year wind-down on the realistic transition layer, with new operations and expansions halted immediately and job placement or retraining for displaced workers, not the industry. Subsidies follow the opt-in rule set out in [[9. Energy & Environment#Subsidies and Mandates|Subsidies and Mandates]]. Predation is treated as a rights violation ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Wild-Animal Predation|Wild-Animal Predation]]), and the oceans are included in the future reordering of nature against predation and suffering. The scale is significant: in 2022, U.S. commercial fishermen landed 8.4 billion pounds of seafood valued at \$5.9 billion, and U.S. aquaculture produced an estimated 663 million pounds valued at \$1.7 billion.

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Commercial fishing and fish farming of sentient animals are banned outright and instantly as the position. Like land-based animal farming, existing operations close on a five-to-ten-year wind-down on the realistic transition layer, with new operations and expansions halted immediately and job placement or retraining for workers only. In practice, like taxes or education, systems may change over a period, but that lag is not the position. The ban covers shellfish, bycatch, and seafood imports, with no subsidies unless taxpayers opt in. The best available science decides which fish are sentient, cultivated seafood is the replacement, and fish and the oceans fall within the future reordering of nature against predation.
