## Core Principles
Practical, gradual transition guided by free-market innovation and sentient-rights considerations. Avoid disruptive mandates or taxpayer-funded subsidies. Prioritize human (and herbivore) welfare over apocalyptic framing while conserving nature where it does not conflict with rights or development.

## Climate Change

>[!tldr] In short
> Climate change is real and partly human-caused, but not apocalyptic. The country shifts slowly to greener infrastructure without disrupting energy supply. Animal agriculture is banned and shut down immediately as a rights violation, which also cuts human-caused warming.

#### Position
The climate varies naturally, but the IPCC finds that essentially all warming since the late 1800s is human-caused. The threat is real but not apocalyptic. Policy supports the long-term wellbeing of sentient individuals, but not to the point of disrupting and upending current energy supply chains. The transition to greener infrastructure is phased in slowly. Ending animal agriculture, which is banned as a rights violation and shut down immediately ([[#Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat|Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat]]), also makes climate change less severe; the ban rests on rights, and the climate gain is a side effect.

> [!question]- "If climate change is real, why not act faster?"
> In my judgment, rapid transition would disrupt energy supply chains that sentient beings depend on, causing harm of its own. That is a judgment about risk, not a measured cost estimate. A slow, phased shift addresses the problem without that disruption.

> [!question]- "What about catastrophic tail risk?"
> Present agents may not impose extreme, unjustified risk of death on future sentient beings ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Duties to Future Generations|Duties to Future Generations]]). If credible evidence shows that risk, the phased timeline can be accelerated, with the burden of proof on the rule.

#### Key Elements
- Natural climate variability exists; recent warming is essentially all human-caused.
- Real but not apocalyptic.
- Policy aimed at long-term sentient wellbeing without upending energy supply.
- Slow, phased shift to greener infrastructure.
- Animal agriculture banned and shut down immediately; ending it also reduces human-induced warming.

#### Rationale
The IPCC attributes recent warming to human activity: it puts the human-caused rise from 1850–1900 to 2010–2019 at about 1.07°C (likely 0.8–1.3°C), out of about 1.1°C observed, with natural drivers contributing between −0.1°C and +0.1°C. It projects a range of outcomes that depend on emissions. Livestock is a significant source of greenhouse gas emissions (about 12% of human-caused emissions) according to the FAO, which links the [[Glossary#Sentience basis (sentientism)|sentientist]] case against animal agriculture to the climate question. Measured transition balances long-term wellbeing against present needs.

## Adaptation and Disaster Policy

>[!tldr] In short
> Climate adaptation is practical. Owners carry the risk of where they build, subsidies need opt-in funding, the 18 percent flood insurance cap is removed, and emergency rescue stays public.

#### Position
Climate change is real but gradual, so adaptation is handled as a practical matter. Taxpayer subsidies for risk, including subsidized insurance for building in high-risk areas, require opt-in funding like any other subsidy. Owners bear the risks of where and how they build, and private insurance prices those risks.

> [!question]- "What happens to people hit by a disaster?"
> Emergency response, rescue, and public safety remain government functions. What changes is the long-term subsidy of rebuilding in high-risk places, which shifts costs to taxpayers who did not choose the risk.

#### Key Elements
- Adaptation treated as practical, not apocalyptic.
- Subsidies only through opt-in funding.
- The 18 percent cap on annual flood insurance premium increases removed.
- Owners bear location risk.
- Emergency response remains a public function.

#### Rationale
The National Flood Insurance Program owed the Treasury \$22.525 billion after borrowing \$2 billion on February 10, 2025, according to the Congressional Research Service. Under FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0, about one-third of National Flood Insurance Program policyholders already pay full-risk premiums, and 9 percent will eventually need increases of more than 300 percent, according to the Government Accountability Office. Annual increases for most policyholders are limited to 18 percent by statute; GAO estimated it would take until 2037 for 95 percent of current policies to reach full-risk premiums, leaving a \$27 billion premium shortfall. The 18 percent cap delays full-risk pricing until 2037 and hides a 27 billion dollar shortfall, so it should be removed. Any assistance for policyholders comes only through opt-in funding, not taxpayer subsidies. The no-subsidy rule is set out in [[#Subsidies and Mandates]].

> - U.S. Government Accountability Office – [Flood Insurance: FEMA's New Rate-Setting Methodology Improves Actuarial Soundness but Highlights Need for Broader Program Reform (GAO-23-105977, July 31, 2023)](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105977)

## International Climate Agreements

>[!tldr] In short
> Awareness efforts are welcome. My contribution is the immediate ban on animal agriculture, a major human driver of climate change. The U.S. follows climate accords only when they match reasonable U.S. law and do not restrict Americans' freedom.

#### Position
International efforts to raise awareness of human-induced climate change can be supported. Animal agriculture, a major human driver of climate change, is banned and shut down immediately as a rights violation ([[#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]), so ending it serves sentient-rights protections and the climate at once. The United States is not beholden to any accord that goes against the freedom of its people and nation. Accords that align with reasonable U.S. conservation and regulatory laws for the public safety of sentient beings can be followed. Policy that infringes on rights-respecting citizens is not mandated.

> [!question]- "Does withdrawing from accords undermine global climate efforts?"
> Accords consistent with reasonable U.S. conservation and safety law can be followed. What is rejected is binding commitments that override the freedom of Americans.

#### Key Elements
- Support for awareness efforts.
- Animal agriculture, a major human driver, banned and shut down immediately.
- No accords that infringe American freedom or rights.
- Accords followed when consistent with reasonable U.S. conservation and safety law.

#### Rationale
Sovereignty applies to climate policy as to foreign policy. The Paris Agreement relies on nationally determined contributions, which shows that climate commitments can be set by each nation's own priorities.

## Energy Mix

>[!tldr] In short
> Fossil fuels stay where they work best while renewables grow through private innovation. There are no forced phase-outs.

#### Position
Fossil fuels remain for now while greater effort goes into renewables. Fossil fuels may continue in industries that benefit most from them, while other industries move to renewables that suit them better. Entire forced phase-outs are not supported; renewable technology advances through incentivization and innovation by private companies.

> [!question]- "Does keeping fossil fuels slow the transition?"
> Each industry uses the energy source that serves it best, and private innovation makes renewables competitive on their merits. Forced phase-outs impose costs before alternatives are ready.

#### Key Elements
- Fossil fuels remain where most beneficial.
- Greater effort into renewables.
- No forced phase-outs.
- Private innovation and incentivization drive renewable advancement.

#### Rationale
Fossil fuels still supply most U.S. energy, so abrupt phase-outs would disrupt the economy. Technology learning and competition have lowered the cost of utility-scale solar by about 85% since 2010, and unsubsidized solar and wind now compete on cost, which supports a transition led by innovation rather than mandate.

#### Rationale
A reliable, affordable energy supply underpins economic growth, so transition must not disrupt it. Nuclear power produces large amounts of low-carbon electricity with one of the lowest death rates per unit of energy of any source. Construction cost is the largest barrier to new reactors, so streamlined licensing works alongside standardized designs and market competition rather than replacing them. Competitive markets drive innovation more efficiently than mandates.

>[!tldr] In short
> Nuclear power should expand because it is proven safe, with strict safety standards.

#### Position
Nuclear power is expanded because it has been proven safe. Safety remains crucial, as it is everywhere else.

>[!example]- Documented accounts

It provides steady output that intermittent sources cannot, making it central to a gradual clean-energy transition.

## Nuclear Waste and Fuel Cycle

>[!tldr] In short
> Nuclear power expands, spent fuel is stored safely, recycling can develop, and storage sites go in low-density areas.

#### Position
Nuclear power is expanded, and spent fuel is managed under the same safety standards that make nuclear power safe. Storage and recycling technologies are allowed to develop under deregulation, and storage sites follow the development rule: low-density areas away from protected species.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **Finland:** Onkalo is designed to hold spent nuclear fuel deep in bedrock for 100,000 years. > [!question]- "Isn't waste the unsolved problem of nuclear power?"
> Spent fuel has been stored safely for decades. Allowing recycling and permanent storage to develop removes regulatory obstacles rather than technical ones.

#### Key Elements
- Expansion of nuclear power.
- Safety standards for spent fuel.
- Recycling and storage allowed to develop.
- Sites in low-density areas.

#### Rationale
The Nuclear Waste Policy Act of 1982 makes the federal government responsible for permanent disposal of spent fuel (42 U.S.C. § 10131). About 97,061 metric tons of commercial spent fuel were in storage at the end of 2024, 46,864 tons in dry storage and 50,197 tons in pools, according to a U.S. Department of Energy inventory. Siting follows [[#Conservation vs Development]].

## Subsidies and Mandates

>[!tldr] In short
> No green-tech subsidies unless taxpayers opt in, and no mandates. The market drives clean energy forward.

#### Position
Government subsidies for green technology mean taxpayer money, which is theft; the government subsidizes nothing unless taxpayers opt in. There are no mandates. Goals exist, and competition booms in the free market.

> [!question]- "Without subsidies, can clean technology compete?"
> Free-market competition drives advancement, and taxpayers who want to support green technology can opt in. Using mandatory taxes to pick winners is rejected.

#### Key Elements
- No government subsidies for green tech without taxpayer opt-in.
- Taxpayer funding without consent viewed as theft.
- No mandates (EVs, net-zero, and similar).
- Goals encouraged through free-market competition.

#### Rationale
Compelled funding of private industries violates property rights and distorts markets. Competition rewards technologies that deliver value at the lowest cost, which is how energy innovation advances most reliably. The Energy Information Administration counted \$183.3 billion (2022 dollars) in federal energy subsidies over FY 2016 to 2022, 46 percent of it for renewables, and 70 percent delivered through the tax code.

## Pollution and Liability

>[!tldr] In short
> Pollution that harms people violates their rights. It is limited to what public-health standards allow, polluters pay for the damage, and greenhouse gases count as pollution above the natural baseline, regulated on the site's gradual climate timeline.

#### Position
Pollution that harms others, or directly imposes a severe, non-consensual risk of serious harm on them, violates their [[Glossary#Negative rights|negative rights]]. It is regulated to a level that meets consumer and public-health standards, and polluters are liable for the harm they cause to people and property. Greenhouse gases count as pollution to the extent that emissions exceed the natural baseline; the natural contribution is not attributed to anyone. Greenhouse-gas rules follow the phased, non-disruptive transition set out in [[#Climate Change]]. Each environmental rule carries the burden of showing the harm it addresses.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **East Palestine, Ohio, 2023:** the NTSB found the vent-and-burn of hazardous tank cars after the Norfolk Southern derailment was "unnecessary." NTSB (2024) – [Failed Wheel Bearing Caused Norfolk Southern Train Derailment in East Palestine, Ohio](https://www.ntsb.gov/news/press-releases/Pages/NR20240625.aspx)

> [!question]- "Doesn't pollution regulation conflict with the framework's skepticism of regulation?"
> No. Preventing serious environmental harm is one of the four domains where business regulation is legitimate. The burden-of-proof rule still applies: a pollution rule must show the harm it prevents, and rules that cannot are removed.

> [!question]- "How can anyone be liable for diffuse greenhouse-gas harm?"
> Where harm cannot be traced to a specific emitter, emissions above the natural baseline are handled by regulation on the phased timeline rather than by individual lawsuits. Traceable harm to persons or property remains liable in court.

#### Key Elements
- Harmful pollution violates the negative rights of those harmed.
- Pollution is regulated to consumer and public-health standards.
- Polluters are liable for harm to persons and property.
- Greenhouse gases count as pollution above the natural baseline.
- Greenhouse-gas rules follow the phased transition in Climate Change.
- Each rule must show the harm it addresses.

#### Rationale
Axiom 6 treats the direct imposition of severe, non-consensual risk as crossing the [[Glossary#Rights forfeiture|forfeiture]] threshold, and property rights protect owners against physical invasion of their land and bodies. Air pollution is the clearest documented case. EPA's peer-reviewed second prospective study estimated that the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments would prevent over 230,000 early deaths in 2020, with central benefits exceeding costs by more than 30 to one. Climate has a natural baseline, and only the human-caused share above it is counted as pollution; the IPCC finds that share is essentially all recent warming. Rules with demonstrated effects meet the burden of proof in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Infrastructure and Regulation|Domestic Policy & Governance]].

## Regulation and Conservation

>[!tldr] In short
> Environmental rules protect sanctuary lands and herbivore populations, and pollution is regulated to keep people healthy.

#### Position
Environmental regulations exist on the specific grounds of conserving and maintaining sanctuary lands and herbivorous animal populations. Pollution and waste are regulated to a level that meets consumer and public health standards.

> [!question]- "Why protect herbivores specifically rather than all wildlife?"
> Under the framework, herbivores can live without causing harm to other sentient beings, so their populations and habitats receive priority protection. This follows from the sentientist ethic set out in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]].

#### Key Elements
- Regulations focused on sanctuary lands and herbivorous animal populations.
- Pollution and waste regulated to protect public health and consumer standards.

#### Rationale
Regulation is justified where it protects sentient beings from harm. Pollution harms public health, which grounds rules like the Clean Air Act. Conservation regulation centers on the populations the framework values most. About 12.9% of U.S. land and inland waters is currently protected and managed mainly for biodiversity, the baseline sanctuary policy builds from.

## Conservation vs Development

>[!tldr] In short
> Nature is preserved where rights are not violated. Mining and extraction go in quarries and lower-density places such as deserts, away from the habitat of protected species, and development is built alongside nature instead of destroying it.

#### Position
Nature is preserved so long as sentient rights are not violated. Resource extraction generally takes place in quarries and lower-density areas such as deserts, sited away from the habitat of protected species such as the desert tortoise. Technology is, for the most part, built in and alongside rights-respecting nature rather than demolishing it.

> [!question]- "Doesn't any development harm wildlife?"
> Development is directed toward low-sentient-density areas to minimize harm, and integration with nature is preferred over demolition. The aim is to minimize rights violations, not halt development.

#### Key Elements
- Preserve nature where sentient rights are not violated.
- Extraction preferred in low-sentient-density areas such as deserts and quarries.
- Technology integrated with rights-respecting nature rather than demolishing it.

#### Rationale
The sentientist framework weighs harm to sentient beings, so where development occurs matters. Concentrating extraction in areas with few sentient animals limits the harm of growth.

## Water Resources and Rights

>[!tldr] In short
> Water rights are property, pollution of water is a violation, and markets allocate water within health standards.

#### Position
Water rights are property rights, held, traded, and protected like other property. Polluting another's water is a rights violation under the pollution rule, with liability for the harm. Water is allocated through property and markets rather than political rationing.

> [!question]- "Won't markets leave people without water?"
> Markets direct water to its most valued uses and reward conservation. Public health standards for drinking water remain.

#### Key Elements
- Water rights as property.
- Pollution of water is a violation.
- Markets over rationing.
- Drinking-water health standards.

#### Rationale
Western states generally allocate water under prior appropriation, in which rights rest on beneficial use and seniority. Pollution liability is set out in [[#Pollution and Liability]].

## Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat

>[!tldr] In short
> Animal agriculture is banned outright and shuts down immediately, with no wind-down. Every animal alive at the shutdown is kept alive and given sanctuary for its natural life under the [[#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]], which also sets out what happens to the firms, the land and the workers. Federal grazing permits are revoked on day one, habitat rules protect sanctuary lands and herbivores, and lab-grown meat is the replacement ([[#Agriculture and Food Policy|Agriculture and Food Policy]]).

#### Position
Animal agriculture should be banned outright and shut down immediately. Farming sentient animals for food is a rights violation, so the ban is not phased in and there is no wind-down.

> [!note]- How this gets done: the Sanctuary Plan
> There is no wind-down. The shutdown, the care and adoption of every animal alive at that moment, the conversion, divestment or liquidation of the firms, the land, the workers, enforcement, and the Sanctuary Levy that pays for it are all set out once, in the [[#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]].

Habitat protection centers on sanctuary lands and herbivore populations. No grazing permits are issued on federal land, and existing permits are revoked on day one; they are not an honored commitment, and grazing does not displace protected herbivore habitat.

**My view:** I believe slaughterhouses and animal farming shouldn't exist in 2026, which follows my sentientist view. Animal agriculture should shut down immediately, with no transition, and lab-grown meat is the goal for replacing it.

> [!question]- "Doesn't this hurt ranchers?"
> Yes, ranching ends. A ranch that earns most of its revenue from animals converts fully to plant-based farming or cultivated-meat research, or is liquidated, and its owners receive no compensation; what is left after creditors goes to the sanctuaries. Keeping every animal alive comes first. There is no retraining or job-placement program: ranchers and farm workers find new jobs or can apply for sanctuary jobs under the worker rules in the [[#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]. Public sanctuary land serves the herbivore populations it protects.

> [!question]- "Animal agriculture is a huge industry. Won't ending it do serious economic damage?"
> The industry is large. U.S. cash receipts from animals and animal products were a record of about \$303.7 billion in 2025 and are forecast at \$287.3 billion in 2026, more than half of all \$540.3 billion in farm cash receipts, and the losses to producers, workers, and farm communities would be real. But economic value does not decide whether a practice violates rights. Britain did not weigh slavery's profitability against its wrong when it abolished slavery in 1833. It ended it, and the £20 million it raised, about 40% of government spending that year, went to compensating enslaved people's owners rather than to keeping the system. I draw the same line, with one difference: the industry gets no compensation, and what is left after creditors goes to the animals, in the sanctuaries. Regulatory barriers to lab-grown meat and plant-based food are cleared so replacements scale up as animal products leave the market ([[10. ### Sanctuary Plan

>[!tldr] In short
> All animal agriculture, wild-capture commercial fishing, aquaculture, commercial breeding mills and animal testing shut down immediately, with no wind-down. Every animal alive at that moment is kept alive. Most go to sanctuaries on converted feedlots and farms, run by the state in the emergency phase and then by licensed nonprofits, where they are sterilized, medicated, housed, fed and kept warm for their natural lives. Animals that are safe to keep can be adopted after strict vetting, and adoption is never a sale. Firms that earn most of their revenue from animal agriculture convert fully to cultivated-meat research or plant-based farming, or are liquidated; other firms divest only their animal-agriculture line. After creditors are paid, every dollar from liquidation and forced conversion goes to the sanctuaries, and shareholders get nothing. A family on a liquidated farm keeps its house and house lot. Land the sanctuaries need from owners outside the industry is bought at fair value. Sanctuary bonds cover the early years. A flat Sanctuary Levy on income above \$75,000 single or \$150,000 joint, never above 3%, repays the bonds and funds care. It ends when the national sanctuary population falls below 1% of its starting size and the bonds are repaid, and an endowment pays for the last animals. This breaks the property-rights part of my negative-rights view, and I accept that tradeoff.

#### Position
My plan is an immediate shutdown with no wind-down. On day one, all animal agriculture, wild-capture commercial fishing, aquaculture (including farmed shellfish, covered as a precaution), commercial breeding mills and animal testing stop. Nobody breeds, slaughters, catches or tests on another sentient animal after that day. Experimentation on offenders who have forfeited their rights continues under [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Animal Rights and Sentience Technology]]; it is not animal testing.

Keeping every animal alive comes first. No animal alive at the shutdown is killed. Because no new animals are born, the number in sanctuary falls every year, and the cost falls with it.

**My plan:**
1. **Immediate shutdown.** Everything listed above stops at once. Operating licenses, fishing licenses and quotas, and federal grazing permits are revoked on day one. They are not an honored commitment, because these practices violate rights.
2. **Every animal kept alive.** Every farmed, farmed-fish, breeding-mill and test animal alive at the shutdown is kept alive for its natural life.
3. **Farmed shellfish.** Farmed shellfish end with the rest of aquaculture, covered as a precaution: their sentience is low or uncertain, so, as with insects, they are protected rather than left out. Shellfish already in the water are left unharvested, and the farms are decommissioned: gear, lines and cages are removed, and no new seed is placed.
4. **Sanctuaries.** The sanctuaries sit on converted feedlots, barns, lots and farms. The state runs them in the emergency phase and then hands them, site by site, to licensed nonprofit operators ([[#Handover to Nonprofit Operators|Handover to Nonprofit Operators]]). No for-profit business receives taxpayer money.
5. **No breeding.** Sterilization is the default. An animal is kept separated instead only where sterilization is medically unsafe for that animal.
6. **Care.** Every sanctuary animal is medicated, housed, fed and kept warm for its natural life. Euthanasia follows the existing rule: only for the animal's own sake, on a veterinarian's finding, by a veterinary professional ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Animal Rights and Sentientism|Animal Rights and Sentientism]]).
7. **Adoption.** Animals that are safe to keep can be adopted after the vetting below. Breeding-mill animals are adopted out where possible, through the same vetting, and the rest go to sanctuary. Adopters pay all of an animal's care themselves, with no stipend. Adoption is never a purchase.
8. **Convert, divest or liquidate.** Every domestic company or farm that earns most of its revenue from animal agriculture or animal testing converts fully to cultivated-meat research or plant-based farming, or is liquidated. Every other domestic firm divests only its animal-agriculture or animal-testing line. This reaches farms, suppliers and lenders as well as companies. Shareholders and owners get no compensation. Workers' unpaid wages are paid first. I don't do that to be kind: a shutdown this large will bring backlash, unrest, resistance and disruption, and paying the wages owed reduces that so the shutdown can actually be carried out. After those wages and creditors are paid, all liquidation and forced-conversion proceeds go to the sanctuaries. A family on a liquidated farm keeps its house and house lot, which are not animal-agriculture assets.
9. **Land.** Feedlots, barns and land held by firms that convert or are liquidated pass to the sanctuaries without payment. Land the sanctuaries need from owners outside the industry, such as landlords who rented to a feedlot or crop farms that kept a few cattle, is bought at fair value. I don't pay them because they are owed it: paying fair value is a practical step to reduce backlash, legal challenges and delay, so the land is secured quickly. As the animals die and the land is no longer needed for them, it becomes protected habitat. It is not sold.
10. **Foreign firms.** The US operations of a foreign-owned animal-agriculture firm, such as Smithfield (owned by WH Group), are liquidated like a domestic firm's. The firm is then barred from operating within the United States or doing business with US-held companies.
11. **Funding.** Liquidation proceeds and sanctuary bonds cover the early years, so there is no tax spike. A flat Sanctuary Levy, never above 3%, repays the bonds and funds care. An endowment pays for the last animals.
12. **Workers.** There is no retraining or job-placement program. Workers find new jobs, or they can apply for sanctuary jobs under strict rules. I would be very worried about people who killed animals for a living moving straight into caring for them, so:
    - Anyone who killed animals for a living is barred from hands-on animal care at any sanctuary and from adopting. That covers slaughter and kill-floor workers, commercial fishing crews, fish-farm harvest staff, and lab staff who killed test animals.
    - Other former animal-agriculture workers may take care roles only after vetting at least as strict as an adopter's: a background check, a check of the animal-cruelty and animal-harm records, interviews and references, and a supervised probation period. Their work is then checked by unannounced welfare inspections of the animals in their care, and any violation means removal.
    - Workers who never handled animals, such as drivers, office staff and maintenance workers, may take sanctuary jobs that involve no animal care.
13. **Enforcement.** Inspections, the import bans on animal products and seafood, and the sentience-graded penalties in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Criminal Justice & Security]] apply to anyone who keeps operating after the shutdown.

**My view:** I accept that this plan breaks the property-rights part of my negative-rights view, through the Sanctuary Levy, compelled conversion and liquidation, and taking the industry's property with no compensation to its shareholders. That is my stated tradeoff, and it applies only to this plan.

#### Adoption and vetting
I worry that farm animals could be adopted by people who would kill them for meat at home, so every adoption is vetted first. Breeding-mill dogs, cats and other companion animals go through the same steps. Each step must be passed before an animal leaves a sanctuary:
1. **Background check.** Every adult in the household is checked against the national records of animal-harm marks, convictions and earlier adoption violations. Any animal-harm mark or conviction disqualifies the household, and so does any adult barred from animal care under point 12.
2. **Home and facility inspection.** An inspector checks the space, shelter, fencing, heating, water and access to a veterinarian against the needs of that species, before approval. The inspection sets how many animals the home may take.
3. **Signed no-slaughter commitment.** The adopter signs a commitment never to kill, sell, breed or use the animal for food or products. Euthanasia is allowed only under the veterinary rule above.
4. **Sterilization before release.** Every adopted animal is sterilized before it leaves the sanctuary. An animal for which sterilization is medically unsafe is placed only in a home that keeps it apart from animals it could breed with, and the inspection checks for that.
5. **ID and registry.** Every adopted animal carries a permanent ID and is entered in a national sanctuary registry, which records its guardian and where it lives. A move or a transfer to another guardian goes through the registry and the same vetting. A death must be reported to the registry with a veterinarian's certificate.
6. **Unannounced welfare checks.** Inspectors visit without notice, first within the opening months and then at least once a year for the animal's life.
7. **Return rule.** A guardian can return an animal to a state sanctuary at any time, with no penalty. An animal is removed and returned if a welfare check fails, the commitment is broken, or the guardian can no longer care for it.
8. **No sale, no stipend.** Adoption is never a purchase. No price is paid for an adopted animal, no adopted animal may be sold, and the adopter pays for its feed, housing and veterinary care.

Killing an adopted animal is treated under the existing murder rules in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty]] and the cruelty rules in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Animal Cruelty Law|Animal Cruelty Law]], graded by the animal's sentience like any other killing of a sentient animal. An unreported death is investigated as a possible killing.

#### Handover to Nonprofit Operators
I am skeptical of state-run sanctuaries. The state runs them at first only because a rights violation this atrocious, egregious and monumental was happening nationwide, so every sanctuary starts as a place of emergency. Once the emergency phase is over, the sanctuaries pass to private nonprofit operators while the animals are still alive. How much government stays involved follows one test: whatever is in the best interest of the victims, the animals.

1. **Emergency phase.** The state runs every sanctuary itself at first. The emergency phase ends site by site: each site passes to a licensed operator once that operator passes accreditation, and the state keeps running any site that has none. I expect most sites to transfer within one to three years.
2. **Sanctuary license.** Only nonprofits may hold a sanctuary license. The license sets the care standards, sterilization, the adoption vetting above and unannounced inspections. A licensed operator may not sell animals or animal products and may not breed any animal, and it hires care staff only under the worker rules in point 12 above.
3. **Site-by-site handover.** Each site passes to a licensed operator by contract. The state pays the operator a fixed amount per animal per year from the Sanctuary Levy. The payment stays steady and is not cut as donations come in, because a shortfall in donations would fall on the animals. Operators may take donations on top. An operator that fails loses the site, back to the state or to another licensed operator.
4. **Two layers of oversight.** An independent accreditation body accredits every operator, as the Global Federation of Animal Sanctuaries does for sanctuaries today, and state inspectors check every site as well. When animals are at risk, the state steps in.
5. **Land.** Operators get the right to use the land, never ownership, for as long as any animals remain on it. Sanctuary land cannot be sold. Once the animals no longer need it, it becomes protected habitat, and conservation trusts may hold it.
6. **The end.** When the levy ends, the Sanctuary Endowment pays the remaining operators until the last animal dies. After that, the sanctuaries become ordinary private charities under ordinary animal-welfare law. An operator that carries on as a private charity once its land becomes habitat needs land of its own. If the endowment ever runs short, the general-revenue backstop in [[#The Sanctuary Levy|The Sanctuary Levy]] still applies.

Contracting out work that private organizations can do is my general rule for government ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Public-Sector Size and Civil Service|Public-Sector Size and Civil Service]]). The per-animal payment is payment to a contractor for a service, the care the state would otherwise provide itself, not a subsidy. It is the same money the state would spend caring for each animal, so the handover does not change the levy.

#### What it costs
Most of these animals are young, because the industry kills them young. Natural lifespans run to about 15–20 years for cows, 10–12 for pigs and sheep, 6–8 for ducks and up to 8 for chickens. Meat chickens and turkeys are bred to grow so fast that their bodies give out early, so most would live only a short time even in sanctuary.

I estimate the cost in three cases. Each case adds up feed at 2026 USDA prices, veterinary care, medication, bedding and heat, caretaker wages at the BLS rate for farm animal workers, and one-time costs for sterilization and for converting housing. Each animal is kept until its natural death. Adopted animals would lower these costs, and I do not count them.

| Case | Year 1 | Year 5 | Year 10 | Total over the animals' lives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | \$155 billion | \$67 billion | \$5 billion | \$665 billion |
| Middle | \$349 billion | \$143 billion | \$50 billion | \$1.58 trillion |
| High | \$1.09 trillion | \$341 billion | \$162 billion | \$4.51 trillion |

In every case, cattle account for about three-quarters of the cost, because they are numerous, large and long-lived.

#### Liquidation, conversion and land
A liquidated firm is sold off by a court-appointed receiver. The money is paid out in this order: the receiver's costs, workers' unpaid wages and benefits, secured lenders against their collateral, taxes, and other creditors. Whatever is left, which would have gone to shareholders, goes to the sanctuaries instead. A firm that converts keeps the assets it uses in its new business and sells the assets that only served animals, and that money also goes to the sanctuaries after the lenders secured on those assets are paid. The animals themselves are not part of any estate; they go to sanctuary, and neither is a farm family's house and house lot (point 8 above). Lenders get no special protection: a loan the sale cannot cover is a loss, as in any failed business. A lender that earns most of its revenue from animal-agriculture loans is itself a covered firm, and every other lender divests the line by making no new loans to it.

The value left after creditors comes mostly from ranch and farm land, not from the meatpackers. Once the ban is in force, slaughter plants, poultry houses and brands built on animal products are worth little, and most large processors would owe more than their assets bring. Pasture also loses value without grazing. I assume forced sales bring about 40–60% of today's value for pasture, 70–85% for cropland, 5–15% for animal buildings and 30–45% for machinery.

#### The Sanctuary Levy
The Sanctuary Levy is a single flat rate on income above its own line: \$75,000 for single filers and heads of household, \$150,000 for married couples filing jointly. It phases in over the next \$10,000 (\$20,000 joint), as the flat tax does, so there is no cliff. Grandfathered Social Security benefits are not counted. Anyone at or below the line pays nothing. On IRS data for 2023 grown to 2026, that base is about \$6.49 trillion a year.

Liquidation proceeds and sanctuary bonds cover the early years, so there is no tax spike. The levy is held level for its whole run, which keeps the rate as low as possible. It is recalculated every year and may only be lowered. It is never above 3%. Once the bonds are repaid, it falls to the rate that covers that year's care alone. It is legally dedicated to sanctuary care, bond repayment and the closing endowment, and to nothing else.

| Case | Levy rate | Runs (shutdown in 2027) | Peak sanctuary bonds | Closing endowment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 0.66% | 2027–2036 | \$75 billion | \$8.4 billion |
| Middle | 1.71% | 2027–2040 | \$435 billion | \$12.5 billion |
| High | 3% (the cap) | 2027–2055 | \$2.48 trillion | none needed |

On the middle case, a single filer earning \$150,000 pays about \$1,200 a year, and a married couple earning \$200,000 pays about \$680. Single filers at or below \$75,000 and couples at or below \$150,000 pay nothing.

**When it ends.** The end date is not fixed. The levy ends when the national sanctuary population falls below 1% of its starting size and the sanctuary bonds are repaid. The population is counted in animal units rather than heads, using the head counts that define a large animal feeding operation under EPA rules: 1,000 cattle, 2,500 pigs, 10,000 sheep, 55,000 turkeys, 82,000 laying hens and 125,000 meat chickens each count as 1,000 units. So a cow counts as 1, a pig 0.4, a sheep or goat 0.1, a turkey about 0.018, a laying hen about 0.012 and a meat chicken 0.008. Counting this way means a few million short-lived birds cannot keep the levy alive, and the threshold tracks what the animals actually cost to keep. The sanctuaries start with about 139 million animal units, so the threshold is about 1.4 million. On my middle case it is crossed around year 14, when the animals still alive cost about \$2.5 billion a year.

**The 3% cap.** On the low and middle cases the cap never binds. On the high case, repaying the bonds by the 2044 threshold would need about 4.3%, so the levy stays at 3% past the threshold until the bonds are repaid, about 11 more years. The cap holds; the term stretches.

**The last animals.** In its final year the levy funds a Sanctuary Endowment equal to five years of care at the threshold level. The endowment is held in Treasury securities and pays for the last animals until the final one dies. Anything left over then goes to the debt. If it ever runs short, the remaining care comes from general revenue, as an ordinary cost of the sanctuaries. When the levy outlasts the animals, as on the high case, it pays their care directly and no endowment is needed.

**The bonds.** On all three cases the bonds are repaid in full by the levy. Sanctuary bonds sit outside the debt brake's path while they are repaid on schedule ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Fiscal Policy|Fiscal Policy]]).

> [!question]- "Why not let the industry wind down over ten years?"
> Because every year of wind-down means more animals bred and killed. With an immediate shutdown, no new animals are born. The cost falls every year as the animals alive today live out their lives.

> [!question]- "Doesn't taxing people and taking companies without compensation violate property rights?"
> Yes. I accept breaking the property-rights part of my negative-rights view for this plan, and I say so plainly. It is a stated exception in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Property Rights|Property Rights]] and [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Civil Asset Forfeiture|Civil Asset Forfeiture]]. The general rule still stands for everything else. Shareholders of firms built on a rights violation get no compensation; what their firms are worth after creditors goes to the animals those firms held.

> [!question]- "Why are landowners outside the industry paid?"
> A landlord who rented pasture to a feedlot, or a crop farm that kept a few cattle, did not run the industry and is not a covered firm. The sanctuaries buy what they need from them at fair value for the practical reason in point 9, not because the payment is owed.

> [!question]- "What stops someone adopting an animal and killing it?"
> The vetting above: a background check, a home inspection, a signed no-slaughter commitment, sterilization, a registry ID, and unannounced welfare checks for the animal's whole life. No one can buy an animal. Killing one is murder or cruelty under the existing rules.

> [!question]- "What happens to the workers?"
> There is no retraining or job-placement program. Workers find new jobs, or apply for sanctuary jobs under point 12. Anyone who killed animals for a living is barred from animal care, and other former animal-agriculture workers are hired for care only after strict vetting.

> [!question]- "Does this end experimentation on offenders?"
> No. "End all testing" means all animal testing. Experimentation on offenders who have forfeited their rights continues under [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Animal Rights and Sentience Technology]].

- Foreign-owned firms' US operations liquidated like domestic ones; the firms are then barred from operating in the US or doing business with US-held companies.
- Liquidation proceeds and sanctuary bonds cover the early years; the bonds sit outside the debt brake while repaid on schedule.
- A flat Sanctuary Levy on income above \$75,000 single or \$150,000 joint, with grandfathered Social Security benefits excluded: 1.71% on my middle case, never above 3%, held level, lowered whenever possible, and legally dedicated to sanctuary care. It ends when the sanctuary population falls below 1% of its starting animal units and the bonds are repaid.
- A closing Sanctuary Endowment pays for the last animals; any remainder goes to the debt.
- No retraining or job-placement program; workers find new jobs or apply for sanctuary jobs under strict rules: anyone who killed animals for a living (slaughter, kill floors, fishing crews, fish-farm harvest, labs) barred from hands-on care and from adopting; other former animal-agriculture workers hired for care only after vetting at least as strict as an adopter's, with supervised probation, unannounced checks and removal for any violation; workers who never handled animals limited to non-care jobs.
- Experimentation on offenders who have forfeited their rights continues.
- I accept breaking the property-rights part of my negative-rights view for this plan.

#### Rationale
Breeding stops on day one, so the number of animals can only fall. The cost is highest in the first year and shrinks as animals die of natural causes. In the middle case the cost per animal is about \$1,800 a year for a cow, \$920 for a pig and \$30–35 for a chicken. The industry's own remaining value pays first, through liquidation and conversion proceeds. Bonds absorb the early peak, so the levy stays level at about 1.71% of income above the levy line on my middle case, and the 3% cap holds even on the high case. Setting the levy line at \$75,000 single and \$150,000 joint keeps the cost off low- and middle-income households. Ending the levy at a population threshold, with an endowment for the tail, keeps people from paying a broad tax for a few remaining animals.

### Agriculture and Food Policy
*Moved from [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance]].*


>[!tldr] In short
> No farm subsidies without opt-in funding, food safety to health standards, and lab-grown meat allowed to compete.

#### Position
Farming receives no taxpayer subsidies without opt-in funding, consistent with the rule for every industry: no taxpayer money goes to farm, grazing, fishing or fish-farming businesses except through taxpayer opt-in, like any subsidy. Food safety is regulated to consumer and public health standards. Lab-grown meat is supported as the replacement for animal agriculture, which shuts down immediately ([[#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]), and regulation does not block it from competing.

> [!question]- "Won't farmers fail without subsidies?"
> Farms compete like other businesses. Opt-in funding remains available for those who choose to support agriculture, and consumers are protected by safety standards rather than by subsidies.

#### Key Elements
- No subsidies without opt-in funding.
- Food safety to consumer and public health standards.
- Lab-grown meat supported as the replacement for animal agriculture, which shuts down immediately, and allowed to compete.

#### Rationale
Direct government payments to farm producers were about \$54 billion in 2020, measured in 2024 dollars, largely pandemic assistance, according to USDA's Economic Research Service. The no-subsidy rule is set out in [[9. Energy & Environment#Subsidies and Mandates|Energy & Environment]], and lab-grown meat in [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Animal Rights and Sentience Technology|Bioethics, Progress, & Technology]].

## Commercial Fishing and Aquaculture

>[!tldr] In short
> Commercial fishing and fish farming of sentient animals are banned outright and shut down immediately, with no wind-down, under the [[#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]. Farmed fish alive at the shutdown are kept alive and given sanctuary, farmed shellfish end as a precaution, licenses and quotas are revoked on day one, and firms that earn most of their revenue from fishing or aquaculture convert fully to cultivated seafood or plant-based alternatives or are liquidated, with no compensation to shareholders. The ban covers shellfish, bycatch, and seafood imports. The best available science decides which fish are sentient, cultivated seafood is the replacement, and fish and the oceans fall within the future reordering of nature against predation.

#### Position
Commercial fishing and aquaculture of sentient animals are banned outright and shut down immediately. Catching or farming sentient animals for food is a rights violation, so the ban is not phased in and there is no wind-down.

Fish follow the same sentience gradient as the murder rules in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Criminal Justice & Security]]: killing higher-sentience fish is on the capital track, and killing lower-sentience fish is punished and counts as a mark against the offender.

> [!note]- How this gets done: the Sanctuary Plan
> There is no wind-down. Like land-based animal farming in [[#Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat|Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat]], wild-capture commercial fishing and aquaculture shut down immediately. No fishing operations, vessels, fish farms, or hatcheries operate after day one, and existing fishing licenses, quotas, and aquaculture licenses are revoked; they are not an honored commitment, because catching or farming sentient animals is a rights violation. Every farmed fish alive at the shutdown is kept alive and given sanctuary, with no breeding or stocking. Farmed shellfish end too, as a precaution: those already in the water are left unharvested, and the farms are decommissioned. Every domestic company, vessel owner, fish farm, supplier or lender that earns most of its revenue from fishing or aquaculture converts fully to cultivated-seafood research or plant-based alternatives, or is liquidated; others divest only that line. Shareholders get no compensation; after workers and creditors are paid, the proceeds go to the sanctuaries. Foreign firms may not operate in the United States or do business with US-held companies. There is no retraining or job-placement program; workers find new jobs or can apply for sanctuary jobs under the worker rules in the Sanctuary Plan. Inspections, the seafood import ban, bycatch bans, and the sentience-graded penalties in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Criminal Justice & Security]] apply to anyone who keeps operating. The full plan is in [[#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]].

Which fish are sentient follows the best available science. Shellfish and bivalves, such as oysters and mussels, are also covered by the ban as a precaution. Their sentience is low or uncertain, so, as with insects, they are protected at the low end of the sliding scale rather than left out ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sentience Determination|Sentience Determination]]). Bycatch and incidental kills in non-fishing marine activity are banned. Imports of wild-caught or farmed seafood are banned. Subsidies follow the opt-in rule in [[#Agriculture and Food Policy|Agriculture and Food Policy]]. The long-term replacement is cultivated seafood produced through private innovation. In the bioengineering future of predation abolishment and the reordering of nature against predation and suffering, fish and the oceans are classified within that framework.

> [!question]- "Are fish even sentient?"
> Sentience is determined by the best available scientific consensus, as for any being ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sentience Determination|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]]). Species that the best available science identifies as sentient are protected.

#### Key Elements
- Commercial fishing and aquaculture of sentient animals are banned outright and shut down immediately, with no wind-down; licenses and quotas revoked on day one ([[#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]]).
- Farmed fish alive at the shutdown are kept alive in sanctuary; farmed shellfish are left unharvested and the farms decommissioned.
- Firms that earn most of their revenue from fishing or aquaculture convert fully to cultivated seafood or plant-based alternatives or are liquidated, others divest the line; the industry gets no compensation or transition funding, and there is no retraining or job-placement program; workers find new jobs or can apply for sanctuary jobs under the worker rules in the [[#Sanctuary Plan|Sanctuary Plan]].
- Fish follow the sentience gradient: higher sentience is capital, lower sentience is punished and tracked.
- Shellfish and bivalves are covered by the ban as a precaution, given their low or uncertain sentience, as with insects.
- Bycatch and incidental kills in non-fishing marine activity are banned.
- Imports of wild-caught or farmed seafood are banned.
- No subsidies except through taxpayer opt-in ([[#Agriculture and Food Policy|Agriculture and Food Policy]]).
- Cultivated seafood is the long-term replacement, through private innovation.
- Sentience by scientific consensus decides which species are covered.
- Fish and the oceans fall within the future bioengineering framework of predation abolishment.

#### Rationale
Animal agriculture and aquaculture are abolished because they kill sentient beings that hold negative rights; farmed and wild-caught sentient fish fall under the same rule. Because every catch is a killing of a sentient being, the ban is the position. Subsidies follow the opt-in rule set out in [[9. Energy & Environment#Subsidies and Mandates|Subsidies and Mandates]]. Predation is treated as a rights violation ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Wild-Animal Predation|Wild-Animal Predation]]), and the oceans are included in the future reordering of nature against predation and suffering. The scale is significant: in 2022, U.S. commercial fishermen landed 8.4 billion pounds of seafood valued at \$5.9 billion, and U.S. aquaculture produced an estimated 663 million pounds valued at \$1.7 billion.
