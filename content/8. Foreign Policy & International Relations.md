## Core Principles
America First realism with selective Western imperialism. Prioritize U.S. sovereignty, security, cultural cohesion, and manufacturing strength. Engage forcefully against direct threats (especially Iran and extremist Islam) while avoiding open-ended nation-building. Participate in alliances and institutions only when they clearly serve American interests.

---

## Interventionism vs Non-Intervention

#### Position
The United States involves itself directly in areas of interest that strengthen the country's safety, such as intervention against Iran, a direct enemy and belligerent extremist force. Nation-building and regime change are viewed with skepticism because of their track record of efficacy. Western imperialism and colonialism that destroy ethically inferior cultures that are openly hostile are favored. Imperialism here means defeating and containing the threat, without governing the territory. Non-hostile cultures are left alone in the majority of cases.

> [!question]- "Doesn't favoring imperialism against hostile cultures contradict skepticism of nation-building?"
> No. Nation-building tries to reconstruct a society into a friendly democracy, which has repeatedly failed. Action against openly hostile cultures aims at defeating the threat, not at remaking the society in America's image. The line is drawn by hostility: hostile forces are confronted, non-hostile ones are left alone.

> [!question]- "Who decides that a culture is hostile, and by what test?"
> Conduct decides, not ethnicity or religion. A regime or movement is openly hostile when its governing institutions attack or sponsor attacks on the United States or commit systematic rights violations. Ethically inferior means governing norms that violate sentient rights. The President and Congress make the determination under their war powers.

#### Key Elements
- Direct intervention where it strengthens U.S. safety, particularly against Iran.
- Skepticism of nation-building and regime change.
- Western imperialism and colonialism favored against openly hostile, ethically inferior cultures.
- Non-hostile cultures generally left alone.

#### Rationale
Foreign policy exists to protect the security, prosperity, and continuity of the United States. Realist thinking holds that great powers should act when core interests are at stake and stay out when they are not. Recent efforts in Iraq, Afghanistan, and Libya showed that military victory is often achievable while building a stable, friendly state almost never is. Iran funds and directs proxy forces that have attacked U.S. troops and allies, pursues nuclear capability, and has plotted assassinations on U.S. soil, which makes it a concrete security problem rather than a distant one.

> [!info]- What is nation-building?
> Nation-building is the use of military and civilian power to construct or reconstruct a state's political institutions, economy, and security forces, usually after a war or regime change.

>[!tldr]- Summary
> The U.S. intervenes where its safety is at stake, especially against Iran. Nation-building is viewed skeptically because it usually fails. Openly hostile cultures may be confronted and defeated; non-hostile ones are left alone.

## Mexico and Latin America

#### Position
Drug cartels are treated as terrorist national-security threats and are destroyed by force. Joint operations with compliant Central and South American governments, including Mexico, are the preferred route. A government that refuses to act against cartels operating against the United States is treated as hostile, and war with that government is justified to eradicate the cartel problem. Action aims at defeating the threat, not at governing territory or rebuilding states. Immigrants from the region are vetted individually, and many Latin American families are judged compatible because of shared family and religious values.

> [!question]- "Wouldn't military action in Mexico risk a disastrous war with a neighbor?"
> Joint operations with a cooperating government come first, and most cartel action is meant to be carried out that way. War is reserved for a government that refuses to act against organizations killing Americans. Even then, the goal is to destroy the cartels, not to occupy or rebuild the country, in line with the skepticism of nation-building in [[#Interventionism vs Non-Intervention]].

#### Key Elements
- Cartels are terrorist national-security threats, destroyed by force.
- Joint operations with compliant governments are preferred.
- Refusal to act against cartels is treated as hostility; war is justified as a last resort.
- No occupation or nation-building.
- Immigration from the region goes through individual values vetting; many families are compatible.

#### Rationale
Cartel-supplied drugs kill Americans at a scale comparable to war. CDC provisional data show an estimated 80,391 drug overdose deaths in 2024, of which 48,422 involved synthetic opioids, mainly fentanyl. In 2025 the United States designated Cartel de Sinaloa, CJNG, MS-13, Tren de Aragua, and other groups as Foreign Terrorist Organizations, which aligns legal treatment with the threat. Domestic enforcement is set out in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Drug Policy|Criminal Justice & Security]], and the war position in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Drugs and Vice|Cultural & Social Issues]].

##### Sources
- Centers for Disease Control and Prevention – [U.S. Overdose Deaths Decrease Almost 27% in 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/pressroom/releases/20250514.html)
- Federal Register – [Foreign Terrorist Organization Designations of Tren de Aragua, MS-13, Cartel de Sinaloa, CJNG, and Others (2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/02/20/2025-02873/foreign-terrorist-organization-designations-of-tren-de-aragua-mara-salvatrucha-ms-13-cartel-de-sinaloa-cartel-de-jalisco-nueva-generacion-carteles-unidos-cartel-del-noreste-cartel-del-golfo-and-la-nueva-familia-michoacana)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Cartels are treated as terrorists and destroyed, ideally together with the local government. A government that refuses to help can face war, but the goal is to defeat the cartels, not to run the country. Many Latin American immigrants are seen as good fits because of shared values.

## Alliances and NATO

#### Position
The United States is not part of or a contributor to any alliance that erodes the nation's sovereignty or the best interests of its people. It joins alliances that increase the security and interests of the nation. Diplomacy and foreign relations are pursued, but national principles come first, and the United States does not capitulate to allies' demands or submit to any foreign tribunal.

> [!question]- "Does conditional membership weaken the credibility of alliances like NATO?"
> Alliances are valuable because they serve the security of their members. Membership on the condition that it serves American interests is the same calculation every ally makes. Credibility comes from strength and clear interests, not from unconditional commitment.

#### Key Elements
- Reject alliances that erode U.S. sovereignty or harm American interests.
- Join alliances that increase U.S. security and interests.
- Maintain diplomacy.
- Never capitulate to allies' demands or submit to foreign tribunals.

#### Rationale
The federal government's first duty is to its own citizens, and alliances are tools toward that end. NATO's collective-defense commitment under Article 5 serves U.S. security where it deters common threats. Sovereignty means U.S. actions answer to U.S. law and voters, not to foreign bodies.

##### Sources
- NATO – [Collective defence and Article 5](https://www.nato.int/cps/en/natohq/topics_110496.htm)
- Cornell LII – [U.S. Constitution, Article II](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articleii)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> The U.S. joins alliances only when they make the country safer and never gives up its sovereignty. It stays diplomatic but does not bow to allies or foreign courts.

## Trade and Economics

#### Position
American manufacturing is bolstered wherever possible. Every import brought into the country has a deal structure under which the United States also exports to that partner, keeping trade mutually beneficial. Tariffs are good in theory and questionable in practice. Domestic trade is prioritized and incentivized through tax and regulatory relief only, such as the payroll tax cuts and full expensing in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Taxation|Domestic Policy & Governance]]. Outsourced labor is subject to regulations and restrictions only where it involves a country determined to be a national security threat to the United States; China is one example. China is treated as a dangerous rival, and trade with it continues under these same conditions.

> [!question]- "Do restrictions on outsourcing conflict with the framework's free-market position?"
> No. Free markets are supported at home, and outsourcing restrictions are limited to countries determined to be national security threats to the United States, such as China. Reciprocity requirements and these narrow restrictions protect the industrial base the country needs to defend itself. The broad work of making U.S. labor cheaper is done through across-the-board payroll tax cuts and full expensing, set out in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Taxation|Domestic Policy & Governance]]. Tariffs are approached with caution because their practical effects are mixed.

> [!question]- "Don't Americans pay for tariffs?"
> Mostly, yes. Studies of the 2018 tariffs found they passed almost completely into the prices Americans paid for imported goods, so the cost fell on U.S. buyers rather than on foreign exporters. That is why tariffs are treated as good in theory, as leverage and as protection for strategic industries, and questionable in practice. The preferred tools for supporting American industry are payroll tax cuts and full expensing.

#### Key Elements
- Prioritize and incentivize American manufacturing.
- Reciprocal export arrangements for imports.
- Tariffs good in theory, questionable in practice.
- Prioritize domestic trade; regulate and restrict outsourced labor only where it involves a country determined to be a national security threat (China is one example).
- China treated as a dangerous rival; trade continues on reciprocal terms.

#### Rationale
A manufacturing base is a strategic asset in war and crisis. The United States runs a large goods trade deficit with China, and the FBI identifies China's intelligence and economic espionage as a major threat. Reciprocity keeps trade beneficial to both sides rather than one-directional. The practical case against broad tariffs is measured: the 2018 tariffs passed through almost completely to U.S. import prices, with the full cost falling on domestic buyers and a loss in U.S. real income of about \$1.4 billion a month by the end of 2018.

##### Sources
- U.S. Census Bureau – [Trade in Goods with China](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/balance/c5700.html)
- FBI – [The China Threat](https://www.fbi.gov/investigate/counterintelligence/the-china-threat)
- Amiti, Redding & Weinstein, NBER – [The Impact of the 2018 Trade War on U.S. Prices and Welfare (2019)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25672)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> American manufacturing comes first, imports should come with matching exports, and tariffs are treated cautiously. Outsourcing is restricted only where it involves a country judged to be a national security threat, such as China. China is a dangerous rival, and trade with it continues only on reciprocal terms.

## Immigration and Borders

#### Position
The best society is culturally cohesive in its values. Diversity, though interesting and positive in places, is associated with lower social trust, most clearly among neighbors, at least in the short to mid term, though the measured effect is small, and in the framework's judgment culture mashing can lead to tribalism, racism, and clashes of ideals, principles, and shared beliefs. A nation is strongest when its populace is culturally similar in beliefs, ethics, and values, so immigration is not treated as a virtue or as necessarily protected. Immigrants who are accepted are vetted and selected to fit the shared Western value structure, and cultural assimilation is paramount. Border security is significantly higher than it is now, with lethal force used when necessary against violent or armed crossings. Illegal immigrants are removed forcefully; incentives for voluntary deportation may be used but are not relied on. Birthright citizenship is abolished by amendment, going forward only, without affecting anyone who obtained citizenship under prior law.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the Fourteenth Amendment guarantee birthright citizenship?"
> Yes, as currently interpreted, which is why abolition is proposed through constitutional amendment. The Supreme Court read the Citizenship Clause to cover children born on U.S. soil in United States v. Wong Kim Ark. The change applies only after the amendment, leaving existing citizens' status untouched.

> [!question]- "Isn't lethal force at the border excessive?"
> It is limited to when it is necessary against violent or armed crossings, the same as force against any attack. Peaceful illegal entrants are removed, not killed.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the economy need immigrant labor?"
> Immigrant labor does help the economy and the budget, and the framework accepts that cost. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that the 2022–2026 immigration surge would reduce federal deficits by about \$0.9 trillion over 2024 to 2034. The position rests on social cohesion, not on economics: a shared culture of values is treated as worth more than the fiscal gain. Labor needs are met first through the domestic workforce, with lower payroll taxes and full expensing making American labor cheaper, and immigration that remains is selected through values vetting and assimilation.

> [!question]- "Immigrants commit less crime than natives. Why prioritize removal?"
> The data do show lower crime among undocumented immigrants: in Texas arrest records, which check the immigration status of every arrestee, undocumented immigrants had considerably lower felony arrest rates than native-born citizens. Removal does not rest on crime rates. It rests on the rule of law and on sovereignty: a nation decides who enters and stays, and entering or remaining illegally is itself the violation of that law.

#### Key Elements
- Cultural cohesion in values viewed as the strongest foundation for society.
- Immigration not inherently virtuous or protected.
- Strict vetting for fit with Western values; assimilation paramount.
- Much stronger border security, with lethal force when necessary against violent or armed crossings.
- Forceful removal of illegal immigrants; incentives secondary.
- Prospective abolition of birthright citizenship by amendment.

#### Rationale
Shared values make trust and cooperation possible, which is why the framework favors cohesion over diversity. That choice has a fiscal cost the framework accepts: CBO estimated that the recent immigration surge would cut federal deficits by about \$0.9 trillion over 2024 to 2034. Federal law already sets grounds for inadmissibility, so vetting for values extends an existing power. No study shows that values screening predicts later conduct; the vetting rests on the principle that a nation may choose members who share its values. The measured link between diversity and lower trust is real but small on average, strongest for trust in neighbors. About 9% of U.S. births in 2023 (roughly 320,000) were to unauthorized or temporary-status mothers. Enforcement positions are set out in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Border Security and Immigration Enforcement|Criminal Justice & Security]].

> [!info]- What is birthright citizenship?
> Birthright citizenship is automatic citizenship for anyone born on a country's soil, regardless of the parents' status. In the United States it rests on the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause.

## Removal of Non-Citizen Residents and Loss of Citizenship

#### Position
Lawful residence in the United States for a non-citizen is conditional on self-support, integration, and obeying the law. The rule covers green card holders and visa holders, including those already living in the country, and applies only to conduct after it takes effect. A covered non-citizen is removed when a court finds, by clear and convincing evidence, any of the following:
- **Public benefits:** receiving cash welfare, SNAP, housing assistance, non-emergency Medicaid, or the last-resort allowance. Emergency medical care does not count, and neither do benefits received by a U.S.-citizen child.
- **Failure to integrate:** not passing the English and civics test used for naturalization within two years of admission.
- **Crime:** conviction of any criminal offense above a basic infraction. Traffic tickets and similar infractions are excluded; theft and anything more serious leads to removal, with zero tolerance. Removal follows the sentence and never replaces it: the offender first serves the full punishment set out in criminal law, and capital crimes are punished as capital crimes.

Opinions, beliefs, and speech are never grounds for removal. Each case requires written notice, a hearing before a judge, the chance to present evidence, and review in federal court ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Due Process and Rule of Law|Due Process and Rule of Law]]). Refugees and asylees are not exempt. They are removed swiftly, but to a safe third country rather than to a country where they would face persecution or torture; the framework owes them safety, not more.

Terrorism, material support to a designated terrorist organization, and treason are never handled by deportation instead of punishment, whoever commits them, citizen or not. They are prosecuted as crimes and treated as attempted murder is: where guilt meets the capital evidentiary standard, the sentence is death ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Mandatory Death Penalty]]; [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Evidentiary Standard|Evidentiary Standard]]); where it is proven beyond a reasonable doubt but falls short of that standard, the offender is held in secure confinement under compelled labor ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor|Compelled Labor]]). Any knowing support for a designated terrorist organization counts, not only an attack. For a capital sentence, the support must be proven by a documented money trail or communications that meet the capital evidentiary standard. Deportation would only move the threat elsewhere and let it return; punishment ends it.

A dual national convicted of one of these offenses also forfeits U.S. citizenship as an added penalty alongside the sentence, never in place of it. The rule applies equally to dual nationals by birth and by naturalization, applies only to conduct after it takes effect, and never leaves anyone stateless. The law names offenses, not religions. Because the Supreme Court holds that citizenship cannot be taken without the citizen's consent, the forfeiture is enacted by constitutional amendment. The removal rules for non-citizens are enacted by statute.

> [!question]- "Isn't 'failing to integrate' too vague to enforce?"
> It would be if it meant attitudes or lifestyle, so it does not. The only test is the one the country already uses for new citizens: federal law requires naturalization applicants to show "an understanding of the English language" and knowledge of U.S. history and government. A resident who cannot pass that test within two years of admission has not met the condition. Broader standards, such as hostility to the country's way of life, are left out because they cannot be measured and would turn removal into a penalty for speech ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Free Speech and Censorship|Free Speech and Censorship]]).

> [!question]- "Isn't removal for any theft too harsh?"
> Residence is a privilege extended on condition of obeying the law, and a resident who steals has broken that condition. Basic infractions such as traffic tickets are excluded so that the rule reaches crimes against people and property, not paperwork. Current law already makes many non-citizens deportable for crimes; this rule removes the gaps and the discretion, with a conviction and a court finding required in every case. The rule applies the same way to a long-term green-card holder with citizen family: residence was granted on the same condition, and the rule reaches only conduct after enactment, so every resident knows the terms in advance. Family members who are citizens keep their citizenship and may choose to stay or to leave.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the Constitution forbid stripping citizenship?"
> For involuntary loss, yes, which is why this part requires an amendment. In Afroyim v. Rusk (1967) the Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment defines "a citizenship which a citizen keeps unless he voluntarily relinquishes it," and in Vance v. Terrazas (1980) it required proof of intent to give up citizenship. In Trop v. Dulles (1958) a plurality held that stripping citizenship as punishment was cruel and unusual, noting that "statelessness is not to be imposed as punishment for crime." The amendment answers the first point directly and the second by limiting the rule to dual nationals, so no one is left without a country. The forfeiture is added to the criminal sentence, death or secure confinement, and never replaces it. Current law already revokes citizenship obtained by fraud, where naturalization was "illegally procured or ... procured by concealment of a material fact."

> [!question]- "Can lawful residents be deported for using benefits or committing crimes without an amendment?"
> Yes, with full due process. The Supreme Court upheld deporting long-time legal residents in Harisiades v. Shaughnessy (1952), treating deportation as a civil matter rather than punishment. In Zadvydas v. Davis (2001) it held that "the Due Process Clause applies to all persons within the United States, including aliens, whether their presence is lawful, unlawful, temporary, or permanent," which is why every case here goes through a hearing, the clear-and-convincing standard, and court review. Existing law already points this way: a non-citizen who becomes "a public charge" within five years of entry from causes not shown to have arisen after entry is deportable, and most qualified non-citizens are barred from federal means-tested benefits for their first five years.

> [!question]- "Isn't it unfair to people who paid taxes and lost a job or fell ill?"
> The rule applies only to benefits received after it takes effect, so no one is removed for past use that was lawful when it happened. Emergency care is never counted, and a U.S.-citizen child keeps any benefit the child qualifies for without putting the parent's residence at risk. Residents know the condition in advance and can protect themselves through work, savings, private insurance, or sponsors. The last-resort allowance is not available to non-citizens ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Welfare and Entitlements|Welfare and Entitlements]]); a resident who needs long-term support can return to the country of citizenship.

> [!question]- "Doesn't removing refugees violate the ban on returning people to persecution?"
> Not when they go to a safe third country. Federal law bars removal to a country where a person's "life or freedom would be threatened" because of race, religion, nationality, social group, or political opinion, though that bar has exceptions for serious criminals and security threats. Regulations implementing the Convention Against Torture bar removal where it is "more likely than not" that the person would be tortured, with no such exceptions. Removal to a third country that will receive the person meets both obligations. Federal law already lists additional countries to which a person may be removed when the first choice will not accept them.

> [!question]- "Isn't death for material support far beyond current law?"
> Yes. Under 18 U.S.C. § 2339B, knowingly providing material support to a designated foreign terrorist organization carries up to 20 years in prison, or any term of years or life "if the death of any person results." The framework goes further on principle: money and communications are what let a terrorist organization kill, so knowing support is treated as taking part in its attempts at murder. Because the penalty is death, the proof must be a documented money trail or communications that meet the capital evidentiary standard, with no conviction on inference alone. Cases proven beyond a reasonable doubt but short of that standard go to secure confinement under compelled labor.

> [!question]- "The immigration courts are already overwhelmed. How can they handle removal for every theft?"
> They cannot as they stand. At the end of August 2026 the immigration courts had 3,092,988 pending cases, even after closing 899,741 cases so far in fiscal year 2026, and new removal grounds would add to that load. The framework therefore pairs the rules with capacity: dedicated removal courts, separate from the asylum backlog, hear these cases; they are funded with enough judges, interpreters, and public defenders to decide cases within 180 days from notice; and for crime cases, the removal hearing is joined to the end of the criminal sentence, since the conviction already establishes the ground. A deadline without the judges to meet it would trade due process for speed, which the framework does not allow.

> [!question]- "Doesn't this create two classes of citizens?"
> No. Every offender, citizen or not, faces the same criminal punishment for terrorism or treason: death where the capital standard is met, secure confinement under compelled labor otherwise. The only difference is the added forfeiture of citizenship for dual nationals, which exists for dual nationals alone so that no one becomes stateless.

> [!question]- "Why the focus on Islamist terrorism if the law is religion-neutral?"
> Because the data put it first, though not alone. The Global Terrorism Index 2026 found that four jihadist organizations, the Islamic State, JNIM, the Pakistani Taliban, and al-Shabaab, caused 3,869 deaths in 2025, 70 per cent of all terrorism deaths worldwide. In the West, 527 of 990 terrorism deaths over the past two decades, 53 per cent, occurred in religiously motivated attacks, and almost all of those were caused by jihadist groups or individuals. The same report records the counter-trend: religious attacks were just over ten per cent of the 1,449 Western attacks since 2007, 30 per cent had a political motivation and 32 per cent no single clear ideology, politically motivated attacks rose by almost 20 per cent in 2025, and the high-profile attacks that year included the assassination of Charlie Kirk. A law that names offenses rather than faiths covers every one of these threats.

#### Key Elements
- Covers green card holders and visa holders, including current residents, for conduct after enactment only.
- Removal on a court finding, by clear and convincing evidence, of benefits received, failure to integrate, or a criminal conviction.
- Benefits that count: cash welfare, SNAP, housing assistance, non-emergency Medicaid, and the last-resort allowance; emergency care and a U.S.-citizen child's benefits excluded.
- Failing to integrate defined only as not passing the naturalization English and civics test within two years of admission.
- Any criminal offense above a basic infraction leads to removal; traffic tickets and similar infractions excluded.
- No removal for opinions, beliefs, or speech.
- Refugees and asylees not exempt; removed swiftly to a safe third country, never to persecution or torture.
- Removal follows any criminal sentence and never replaces it.
- Terrorism, material support, and treason, by citizens or non-citizens, punished as attempted murder is: death at the capital evidentiary standard, otherwise secure confinement under compelled labor; never deportation instead of punishment.
- Any knowing support for a designated terrorist organization is capital when proven by a documented money trail or communications meeting the capital evidentiary standard.
- Dual nationals convicted of these offenses also forfeit citizenship as an added penalty; religion-neutral in law, prospective only, never leaving anyone stateless.
- Citizenship forfeiture by constitutional amendment; residency rules by statute.

#### Rationale
The framework treats immigration as a privilege the nation grants to those who fit its shared values, with assimilation paramount ([[#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]; [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Language and Assimilation|Language and Assimilation]]). Federal law already reflects the self-support condition. A non-citizen who becomes a public charge within five years of entry is deportable, qualified non-citizens who entered on or after August 22, 1996 are generally barred from federal means-tested benefits for five years, and federal housing assistance is limited to listed categories of non-citizens. The public-charge rules for admission have shifted with administrations: a 2019 rule stopped being in effect on March 9, 2021, and a 2022 rule similar to the 1999 guidance took effect on December 23, 2022. Writing the condition into statute, with a court finding by clear and convincing evidence in every case, makes it stable. The safe-third-country rule keeps the United States within its obligations against returning people to persecution or torture without granting refugees more than safety. Terrorism is different in kind. An attack meant to kill is an attempt at murder, and the framework treats attempted murder as capital ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Terrorism and Domestic Extremism|Terrorism and Domestic Extremism]]); deporting a terrorist would only move the threat abroad, where it can plan again, while execution or secure confinement ends it. Citizenship forfeiture is kept as an added penalty for dual nationals so that an offender's tie to the country ends with the crime. The Court's reading of the Fourteenth Amendment in Afroyim means involuntary loss requires an amendment, and the framework's concern for rights means the amendment must require a criminal conviction and must never create statelessness. The offenses are religion-neutral, but the emphasis on Islamist terrorism follows the data: jihadist groups caused 70 per cent of terrorism deaths worldwide in 2025 and most terrorism deaths in the West over two decades, while political and mixed-ideology attacks are more numerous in the West and rising. Removal for belief or speech is excluded because the framework protects expression even when it is hostile.

## Israel and the Middle East

#### Position
Israel is supported as the first line of Western defense against the barbaric Islamic Republic and the forces of terror in the region. Extremist Islam is eradicated. More force is used toward Iran, and the current administration is judged too soft. Iran's people are spared, but the Islamic regime is obliterated along with all allies of state-sponsored, anti-Western terror. Destroying the regime is not followed by nation-building or occupation. Israel is supported as far as it serves U.S. security, toward completely erasing the stain of extremist Islam from the world.

> [!question]- "Can the Iranian regime be destroyed without harming its people?"

#### Key Elements
- Strong support for Israel as the front line against Iran and extremist Islam.
- Greater force toward Iran; current policy judged too soft.
- Obliterate the Islamic regime and its state-sponsored terror allies while sparing civilians.
- Eradicate extremist Islam as a global force.

#### Rationale
Iran funds, arms, and directs proxy forces that attack U.S. troops, shipping, and allies, and it has plotted assassinations on U.S. soil. Israel confronts these forces directly, which serves U.S. interests. Destroying the regime removes the source of the threat rather than managing its symptoms.

>[!tldr]- Summary

## Sanctions and Economic Statecraft

#### Position
Economic tools serve national security. Trade is reciprocal, and restrictions apply to adversaries and national-security threats such as China, Russia, and Iran, including limits on outsourcing and technology transfer. Sanctions are used where they weaken an enemy's capacity to threaten the United States, alongside military deterrence rather than in place of it.

> [!question]- "Do sanctions actually work?"
> Not alone. They are one tool that raises an adversary's costs and limits its access to technology and finance, used together with deterrence. Across 204 sanctions episodes studied by the Peterson Institute, sanctions were at least partly successful in 34%, succeeding about half the time for modest policy goals but in about 31% of attempts at regime change or democratization.

#### Key Elements
- Economic tools serve security.
- Reciprocal trade.
- Restrictions on adversaries.
- Sanctions alongside deterrence.

#### Rationale
The International Emergency Economic Powers Act authorizes the President to regulate economic transactions to deal with an unusual and extraordinary foreign threat once a national emergency is declared (50 U.S.C. § 1701). Under the emergency-powers rule in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Emergency Powers|Authority & Civil Liberties]], such declarations lapse unless the legislature renews them.

##### Sources
- Cornell LII – [50 U.S.C. § 1701: Unusual and extraordinary threat](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/50/1701)
- Hufbauer, Schott, Elliott & Oegg, Peterson Institute – [Economic Sanctions Reconsidered, 3rd ed., ch. 6 (2007)](https://www.piie.com/publications/chapters_preview/4075/06iie4075.pdf)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Sanctions and trade limits target adversaries like China, Russia, and Iran, and work alongside military deterrence.

## Global Institutions

#### Position
Participation in global institutions follows the position on alliances: the United States contributes only to those that benefit it and serve its best interests. It may contribute, but it is not held to any standard, tribunal, or global court for its sovereign actions and does not capitulate to any international law. The United States follows its own law and what is best for its people and shared values, and no globalist bureaucrats or courts dictate its conduct.

> [!question]- "Does rejecting international law isolate the United States?"
> No. The United States can cooperate with any institution that serves its interests. What it rejects is surrendering authority over its own actions to foreign bodies that answer to no American voter.

#### Key Elements
- Participate only in institutions that benefit the U.S.
- Never submit to international tribunals or global courts.
- U.S. law and national interest above international law and global bureaucracy.

#### Rationale
Sovereignty means accountability to one's own citizens. The United States is not a party to the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court. Institutions such as the UN operate under a charter that lets members participate without surrendering domestic authority.

##### Sources
- International Criminal Court – [About the Court](https://www.icc-cpi.int/about/the-court)
- United Nations – [UN Charter](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> The U.S. joins global institutions only when they help it and never submits to international courts or law over its own.

## Foreign Aid

#### Position
Foreign spending must advance the security and long-term interests of the United States. Assistance that serves that purpose, such as support for allies against shared threats or efforts that counter adversaries, is judged under the same national-interest test as alliances and institutions. Charitable aid that serves no American interest is redistribution abroad and is funded only through taxpayer opt-in or private giving.

> [!question]- "Won't cutting aid cede influence to China and other rivals?"
> Aid that counters adversaries or secures American interests passes the national-interest test and can continue. What does not pass is charity carried out with compelled tax money. Americans who want to fund humanitarian work abroad remain free to do so, through opt-in programs or private giving.

> [!question]- "Doesn't cutting aid cost lives?"
> It can. The State Department credits PEPFAR, the U.S. HIV/AIDS program, with saving 26 million lives since 2003. The framework does not deny that. It rests on principle: compelled taxes do not fund charity abroad any more than at home. Programs like PEPFAR continue where they pass the national-interest test, and otherwise through opt-in funding and private giving, which remain open to everyone who wants to keep them going.

#### Key Elements
- Foreign spending must serve U.S. security and long-term interests.
- Interest-serving assistance is judged under the alliance and institution test.
- Purely charitable foreign aid is funded only by opt-in or private giving.

#### Rationale
The state's primary duty is to its own citizens, as set out in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]], and the framework rejects mandatory redistribution at home. Applying the same rule abroad keeps policy consistent. Foreign aid accounts for about 1% or less of the federal budget, and U.S. global health funding was \$12.4 billion in FY2024. The amounts are small relative to the budget, so the question is one of principle: whether compelled taxes should fund charity abroad. The opt-in model is defined in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Welfare and Entitlements|Domestic Policy & Governance]].

##### Sources
- KFF – [The U.S. Government and Global Health](https://www.kff.org/global-health-policy/health-policy-101-the-u-s-government-and-global-health/)
- U.S. Department of State – [PEPFAR Latest Global Results & Projections Factsheet (Dec. 2024)](https://www.state.gov/pepfar-latest-global-results-factsheet-dec-2024)
- KFF – [10 Key Facts About the U.S. Global Health Response](https://www.kff.org/global-health-policy/10-key-facts-about-the-u-s-global-health-response/)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Foreign aid must serve America's own security and interests. Aid that is purely charitable is paid for only by people who choose to give, not by required taxes.

## Military Space

The Outer Space Treaty is kept as the baseline for peaceful civil use, interpreted to allow defensive systems, with renegotiation or exit from clauses that block missile defense or hostile-denial. Human spaceflight stays fully privatized, the only funded government space program is military under the Space Force and the Department of Defense, which may contract private firms, and there are no business subsidies except taxpayer opt-in.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the Outer Space Treaty ban weapons in space?"

## Laws of War

#### Position
Civilians are absolutely protected and get full rights protections. Prisoners of war get basic protections and treatment similar to how the United States treats its own prisoners, including labor, but foreign captives are held in a separate military setup. Labor is the preferred placement for them. Geneva compliance is refused only where the Convention conflicts with the framework's compelled-labor rule: a prisoner whom a military tribunal finds, under due process, took part in hostilities against the United States has crossed the forfeiture threshold in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#General Rules of Forfeiture|Section 1]] and may be assigned compelled labor on the same terms as domestic prisoners, beyond the Convention's limits on the kinds of work and its pay requirements. A rights violator past that line loses the corresponding protections. A prisoner who has not crossed it keeps every protection the Convention gives.

> [!question]- "Doesn't departing from Geneva on labor invite enemies to mistreat captured Americans?"
> Civilians remain absolutely protected, and prisoners of war receive basic protections and treatment similar to how the United States treats its own prisoners. The only departure is compelled labor for prisoners a military tribunal finds took part in hostilities; every other prisoner keeps every protection the Convention gives. The standard is the same one applied at home, not a lower one invented for foreigners. Reciprocity is a practical consideration, but the framework does not let a treaty set the terms; U.S. law and the rights framework do.

#### Key Elements
- Civilians absolutely protected, with full rights protections.
- Prisoners of war get basic protections.
- Geneva compliance refused only where it conflicts with the compelled-labor rule, for prisoners a military tribunal finds took part in hostilities, under the Section 1 forfeiture threshold.
- Prisoners of war who have not crossed the forfeiture threshold keep full Convention protections.
- Labor is the preferred placement for prisoners of war.
- Treatment similar to U.S. treatment of its own prisoners, including labor.
- Foreign captives held in a separate military setup.

#### Rationale
The Third Geneva Convention of 1949, to which the United States is a party, states that "prisoners of war must at all times be humanely treated" (Article 13), that they "shall not be interned in penitentiaries" except in their own interest (Article 22), and that the detaining power "may utilize the labour of prisoners of war who are physically fit" (Article 49). The framework departs from the Convention only where its labor limits conflict with the compelled-labor rule, and only for prisoners past the forfeiture threshold. Labor by prisoners of war rests on two grounds. Their detention as captured combatants permits labor, as the Convention itself does for physically fit prisoners (Article 49), and labor is assigned after a military tribunal finds, under due process, that the prisoner took part in hostilities against the United States, consistent with the forfeiture rule in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#General Rules of Forfeiture|Section 1]]. Compelled labor for domestic prisoners is set out in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor|Criminal Justice & Security]].

##### Sources
- Yale Law School Avalon Project – [Convention (III) relative to the Treatment of Prisoners of War (1949)](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/geneva03.asp)
- International Committee of the Red Cross – [The Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their Additional Protocols](https://www.icrc.org/en/law-and-policy/geneva-conventions-and-their-commentaries)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Civilians are absolutely protected. Prisoners of war keep their Geneva rights, except where those conflict with the compelled-labor rule, and are held in a separate military setup, but those a tribunal finds took part in hostilities can be put to compelled labor like domestic prisoners.

## Nuclear Weapons and WMD

#### Position
Nuclear proliferation is unfortunate but can never be reversed, since progress will always be made with all technologies. Mutual deterrence is the only way to prevent catastrophe. Enemies are militarily prevented from advancing nuclear capability when the United States can do so safely.

> [!question]- "Isn't preventive military action against proliferation destabilizing?"
> Action is taken only when it can be done safely. Allowing hostile regimes to acquire nuclear weapons is far more destabilizing, because deterrence depends on rational actors and some enemies are openly committed to destroying the West.

#### Key Elements
- Proliferation cannot be reversed.
- Mutual deterrence as the safeguard against catastrophe.
- Military prevention of enemy proliferation when safely possible.
- Strong U.S. nuclear posture.

#### Rationale
Nine states possess nuclear weapons, and the knowledge cannot be removed from the world. Deterrence has prevented nuclear war among major powers since 1945. Preventing hostile regimes such as Iran from acquiring weapons keeps deterrence workable.

> [!info]- What is mutual deterrence?
> Mutual deterrence is the condition in which each side can inflict unacceptable retaliation on the other, so that neither has an incentive to strike first.

##### Sources
- Arms Control Association – [Nuclear Weapons: Who Has What at a Glance](https://www.armscontrol.org/factsheets/Nuclearweaponswhohaswhat)
- United Nations Office for Disarmament Affairs – [Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT)](https://www.un.org/disarmament/wmd/nuclear/npt/)

>[!tldr]- Summary
> Nuclear weapons cannot be uninvented, so deterrence is the main safeguard. Enemies are stopped from getting them by military force when it can be done safely.

- Containment through alliances.
- Preventive action only when safe.
- No occupation or nation-building.
