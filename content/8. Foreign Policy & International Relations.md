## Core Principles
America First realism with selective Western imperialism. Prioritize U.S. sovereignty, security, cultural cohesion, and manufacturing strength. Engage forcefully against direct threats (especially Iran and extremist Islam) while avoiding open-ended nation-building. Participate in alliances and institutions only when they clearly serve American interests.

## Interventionism vs Non-Intervention

>[!tldr] In short
> The U.S. intervenes where its safety is at stake, especially against Iran. Nation-building is viewed skeptically because it usually fails. Openly hostile cultures may be confronted and defeated; non-hostile ones are left alone.

#### Position
The United States involves itself directly in areas of interest that strengthen the country's safety, such as intervention against Iran, a direct enemy and belligerent extremist force. Nation-building and regime change are viewed with skepticism because of their track record of efficacy. Western imperialism and colonialism that destroy ethically inferior cultures that are openly hostile are favored. Imperialism here means defeating and containing the threat, without governing the territory. Non-hostile cultures are left alone in the majority of cases. I believe that when U.S. action takes control of a foreign country's assets or revenue, they are held in trust for its people, with public audits, spending only for their benefit, a fixed end date unless Congress renews it, and creditors' claims settled in court.

> [!question]- "Doesn't favoring imperialism against hostile cultures contradict skepticism of nation-building?"
> No. Nation-building tries to reconstruct a society into a friendly democracy, which has repeatedly failed. Action against openly hostile cultures aims at defeating the threat, not at remaking the society in America's image. The line is drawn by hostility: hostile forces are confronted, non-hostile ones are left alone.

> [!question]- "Who decides that a culture is hostile, and by what test?"
> Conduct decides, not ethnicity or religion. A regime or movement is openly hostile when its governing institutions attack or sponsor attacks on the United States or commit systematic rights violations. Ethically inferior means governing norms that violate sentient rights. The President and Congress make the determination under their war powers. In real time, the President may act alone only to repel an attack under way or imminent, or to protect U.S. forces and citizens; that is the immediate response already allowed in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Emergency Powers|Emergency Powers]]. A sustained campaign to defeat and contain a regime or movement needs Congress's authorization within 30 days, or it ends on a fixed date. That is shorter than the 60 days plus 30 for withdrawal in today's War Powers Resolution. When the evidence of attack or sponsorship is incomplete, the default is defensive action only: interception, protection of forces, sanctions, and intelligence gathering. A regime or movement is designated openly hostile, and offensive action begins, once the attack or sponsorship is attributed to its governing institutions on evidence the President certifies to Congress.

#### Key Elements
- Direct intervention where it strengthens U.S. safety, particularly against Iran.
- Skepticism of nation-building and regime change.
- Western imperialism and colonialism favored against openly hostile, ethically inferior cultures.
- Non-hostile cultures generally left alone.
- Real time: the President acts alone only to repel an attack under way or imminent; a sustained campaign needs Congress's authorization within 30 days; incomplete evidence means defensive action only until attribution is certified to Congress.
- Foreign assets or revenue under U.S. control are held in trust for that country's people: public audits, spending only for their benefit, a fixed end date unless Congress renews it, creditors' claims settled in court.

#### Rationale
Foreign policy exists to protect the security, prosperity, and continuity of the United States. Realist thinking holds that great powers should act when core interests are at stake and stay out when they are not. Recent efforts in Iraq, Afghanistan, and Libya showed that military victory is often achievable while building a stable, friendly state almost never is. Iran funds and directs proxy forces that have attacked U.S. troops and allies, pursues nuclear capability, and has plotted assassinations on U.S. soil, which makes it a concrete security problem rather than a distant one.

> [!info]- What is nation-building?
> Nation-building is the use of military and civilian power to construct or reconstruct a state's political institutions, economy, and security forces, usually after a war or regime change.

## Alliances and NATO

>[!tldr] In short
> The U.S. joins alliances only when they make the country safer and never gives up its sovereignty. It stays diplomatic but does not bow to allies or foreign courts.

#### Position
The United States is not part of or a contributor to any alliance that erodes the nation's sovereignty or the best interests of its people. It joins alliances that increase the security and interests of the nation. Diplomacy and foreign relations are pursued, but national principles come first, and the United States does not capitulate to allies' demands or submit to any foreign tribunal. I believe the United States takes territory only with the consent of its people and their government, by purchase or treaty. With allies, security needs are met first through basing and defense agreements. Threats and tariffs against a friendly country are a last resort, justified only when the security concern is overwhelming and truly justified.

> [!question]- "Does conditional membership weaken the credibility of alliances like NATO?"
> Alliances are valuable because they serve the security of their members. Membership on the condition that it serves American interests is the same calculation every ally makes. Credibility comes from strength and clear interests, not from unconditional commitment.

#### Key Elements
- Reject alliances that erode U.S. sovereignty or harm American interests.
- Join alliances that increase U.S. security and interests.
- Maintain diplomacy.
- Never capitulate to allies' demands or submit to foreign tribunals.
- Territory acquired only with the consent of its people and government, by purchase or treaty; threats or tariffs against a friendly country only as a last resort for an overwhelming, truly justified security need.

#### Rationale
The federal government's first duty is to its own citizens, and alliances are tools toward that end. NATO's collective-defense commitment under Article 5 serves U.S. security where it deters common threats. Sovereignty means U.S. actions answer to U.S. law and voters, not to foreign bodies.

## Trade and Economics

>[!tldr] In short
> American manufacturing comes first, imports should come with matching exports, and tariffs are treated cautiously. Outsourcing is restricted only where it involves a country judged to be a national security threat, such as China. China is a dangerous rival, and trade with it continues only on reciprocal terms.

#### Position
American manufacturing is bolstered wherever possible. Every import brought into the country has a deal structure under which the United States also exports to that partner, keeping trade mutually beneficial. Tariffs are good in theory and questionable in practice. Domestic trade is prioritized and incentivized through tax and regulatory relief only, such as full expensing and the long-run payroll tax cuts in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Taxation|Domestic Policy & Governance]]. Outsourced labor is subject to regulations and restrictions only where it involves a country determined to be a national security threat to the United States; China is one example. China is treated as a dangerous rival, and trade with it continues under these same conditions. I believe tariffs are taxes and belong to Congress. The President may impose them only under clear, narrow statutory authority, for a stated national-security or leverage purpose, and for a fixed period.

> [!question]- "Do restrictions on outsourcing conflict with your free-market position?"
> No. Free markets are supported at home, and outsourcing restrictions are limited to countries determined to be national security threats to the United States, such as China. Reciprocity requirements and these narrow restrictions protect the industrial base the country needs to defend itself. The broad work of making U.S. labor cheaper is done through full expensing now and across-the-board payroll tax cuts in the long run, as the transition levy steps down, set out in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Taxation|Domestic Policy & Governance]]. Tariffs are approached with caution because their practical effects are mixed.

> [!question]- "Don't Americans pay for tariffs?"
> Mostly, yes. Studies of the 2018 tariffs found they passed almost completely into the prices Americans paid for imported goods, so the cost fell on U.S. buyers rather than on foreign exporters. That is why tariffs are treated as good in theory, as leverage and as protection for strategic industries, and questionable in practice. The preferred tools for supporting American industry are full expensing and, in the long run, payroll tax cuts.

#### Key Elements
- Prioritize and incentivize American manufacturing.
- Reciprocal export arrangements for imports.
- Tariffs good in theory, questionable in practice.
- Prioritize domestic trade; regulate and restrict outsourced labor only where it involves a country determined to be a national security threat (China is one example).
- China treated as a dangerous rival; trade continues on reciprocal terms.
- U.S. investment that funds a threat country's military, surveillance or weapons technology is restricted in the same way as outsourcing to it.
- Tariffs set by Congress; executive tariffs only under clear, narrow statutory authority, for a stated purpose and a fixed period.

#### Rationale
A manufacturing base is a strategic asset in war and crisis. The United States runs a large goods trade deficit with China, and the FBI identifies China's intelligence and economic espionage as a major threat. Reciprocity keeps trade beneficial to both sides rather than one-directional. The practical case against broad tariffs is measured. The 2018 tariffs passed through almost completely to U.S. import prices, with the full cost falling on domestic buyers and a loss in U.S. real income of about \$1.4 billion a month by the end of 2018.

## Immigration and Borders

>[!tldr] In short
> A culturally similar society is seen as strongest, so immigration is limited to people who fit Western values and assimilate, with American labor needs as a secondary criterion. I want less immigration from Muslim-majority countries. Being Muslim carries a presumption against admission, which an individual applicant can overcome only with verified evidence of conduct and record. The border is heavily secured, with lethal force when necessary, and illegal immigrants are removed by force. Birthright citizenship ends by amendment for future births.

#### Position
The best society is culturally cohesive in its values. Diversity, though interesting and positive in places, is associated with lower social trust, most clearly among neighbors, at least in the short to mid term, though the measured effect is small. In my judgment culture mashing can lead to tribalism, racism, and clashes of ideals, principles, and shared beliefs. A nation is strongest when its populace is culturally similar in beliefs, ethics, and values, so immigration is not treated as a virtue or as necessarily protected. Immigrants who are accepted are vetted and selected to fit the shared Western value structure, and cultural assimilation is paramount. Values and assimilation are the primary criteria; American labor needs are a secondary criterion. I want less immigration from Muslim-majority countries. Being Muslim carries a rebuttable presumption of incompatibility: the default is refusal, overcome only by clear and convincing individual evidence of conduct and record, never by self-declaration alone ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Islam and Extremism|Islam and Extremism]]). Border security is significantly higher than it is now, with lethal force used when necessary to protect it. Illegal immigrants are removed forcefully; incentives for voluntary deportation may be used but are not relied on. Birthright citizenship is abolished by amendment, going forward only, without affecting anyone who obtained citizenship under prior law. I believe refugee resettlement is a privilege granted in the national interest, under the same values vetting as any immigrant. Persecution gives a right not to be returned to it, not a right to be resettled here, and the ceiling is set to the vetting capacity and the national interest.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the Fourteenth Amendment guarantee birthright citizenship?"
> Yes, as currently interpreted, which is why abolition is proposed through constitutional amendment. The Supreme Court read the Citizenship Clause to cover children born on U.S. soil in United States v. Wong Kim Ark. The change applies only after the amendment, leaving existing citizens' status untouched.

> [!question]- "Isn't lethal force at the border excessive?"
> No. I want the border protected, with lethal force when necessary, the same as force against any attack. Illegal entrants who are caught are removed by force.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the economy need immigrant labor?"
> Supporters of high immigration point to the budget: the Congressional Budget Office estimated that the 2022–2026 immigration surge would reduce federal deficits by about \$0.9 trillion over 2024 to 2034. That gain does not decide admission. The position rests on social cohesion, not on economics: a shared culture of values is treated as worth more than the fiscal gain. Labor needs are met first through the domestic workforce, with full expensing and, in the long run, lower payroll taxes making American labor cheaper, and immigration that remains is selected first through values vetting and assimilation, then by American labor needs.

> [!question]- "How does an applicant overcome the presumption?"
> By evidence of conduct and record, not by declaration. Every immigrant goes through individual values vetting. Two groups start with the burden on them: Muslim applicants, and applicants from countries where Islamist movements govern or where survey data show majority support for state-enforced religious law. To overcome it, the applicant must show by clear and convincing evidence all five of the following:
> 1. **Constitution over religious law:** acceptance that the Constitution and civil law override any religious law, including in family, inheritance, and criminal matters.
> 2. **Equal rights:** acceptance of equal legal standing for women and for non-Muslims and nonbelievers.
> 3. **Freedom to leave the faith:** acceptance that anyone may leave or change religion, or criticize it, without penalty.
> 4. **No violence:** rejection of violence for religious or political ends, with no membership in, support for, or funding of a designated terrorist or Islamist organization.
> 5. **A verifiable record:** a record of conduct that matches 1–4 and can be checked.
>
> **Evidence that counts:** criminal and security record checks; public-data review of the applicant's own posts, publications, and affiliations, within the public-data limits in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Surveillance and Digital Technology|Surveillance and Digital Technology]]; memberships, donations, and employers; prior lawful residence and compliance with the law in a country that meets these standards; and sworn references who can be checked. A structured interview and a sworn statement are required, but they are never enough on their own. The statement is made under the perjury rules in [[4. Religion & Secularism#Oath of Office and Perjury|Oath of Office and Perjury]], so a lie found later is fraud, which is conduct and a ground for removal.
>
> **Who decides:** a consular or USCIS officer trained for values vetting decides first. A denial gets one review by a second officer, on the written record. The applicant carries the burden throughout. Being Muslim sets where the burden starts. It is never the test itself, and an applicant of any faith who fails 1–5 is refused.
>
> Passing vetting admits a person; it does not lift the excluded-ideologies amendment, which bars adherents of Islam, as it bars adherents of Nazism, communism, and socialism, from public office and every taxpayer-funded position ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Oath of Office and Perjury|Oath of Office and Perjury]]). Nor does passing vetting lift the mosque bar: no new mosques may be built, and existing mosques stay capped with no expansion, relocation, or rebuilding ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Religious Freedom|Religious Freedom]]).

> [!question]- "Immigrants commit less crime than natives. Why prioritize removal?"
> The data do show lower crime among undocumented immigrants: in Texas arrest records, which check the immigration status of every arrestee, undocumented immigrants had considerably lower felony arrest rates than native-born citizens. Incarceration is not the same as offending, but the national record points the same way: immigrants have had lower incarceration rates than the U.S.-born for 150 years, and by 2020 they were 60% less likely to be incarcerated, and 30% less likely than U.S.-born whites, a gap that has widened since 1960 and that the authors find is not explained by immigration policy or by immigrants' observable characteristics. Removal does not rest on crime rates. It rests on the rule of law and on sovereignty: a nation decides who enters and stays, and entering or remaining illegally is itself the violation of that law.

#### Skilled-Worker Visas (H-1B)
I believe skilled-worker visas follow the same order as all immigration: every applicant passes the same individual values vetting first, and American labor needs are a secondary criterion after values and assimilation, met first through the domestic workforce.

I judge the H-1B program by what it does now, not by the exceptional people it admitted decades ago. The firm-level lottery evidence (data above) finds that each added H-1B visa crowded out about 1.5 other workers at the winning firm, which is why American labor needs are met first through the domestic workforce. Critics cite the city-level finding that foreign STEM workers raised wage growth for college-educated natives. That is an average across cities, and it does nothing for the workers displaced at the firms that win the visas. As Palmer Luckey, Anduril's founder, put it, "Immigration policy must be driven by the present, not the past." X – [Palmer Luckey, 22 Oct 2026](https://x.com/PalmerLuckey/status/2113159728188854654)

#### Key Elements
- Cultural cohesion in values viewed as the strongest foundation for society.
- Immigration not inherently virtuous or protected.
- Strict vetting for fit with Western values; assimilation paramount.
- American labor needs as a secondary selection criterion, after values and assimilation.
- Less immigration from Muslim-majority countries; a rebuttable presumption against Muslim applicants, overcome only by verified conduct and record (acceptance of constitutional supremacy over religious law, equal rights for women and non-Muslims, freedom to leave the faith, rejection of violence), not by declaration.
- Much stronger border security, with lethal force when necessary.
- Forceful removal of illegal immigrants; incentives secondary.
- Prospective abolition of birthright citizenship by amendment.
- Refugee resettlement is a national-interest privilege under the same values vetting; persecution gives a right not to be returned to it, not a right to resettlement.

#### Rationale
Shared values make trust and cooperation possible, which is why I favor cohesion over diversity. Critics cite the fiscal gain from immigration, which CBO put at about \$0.9 trillion in lower deficits over 2024 to 2034; cohesion outweighs it. Federal law already sets grounds for inadmissibility, so vetting for values extends an existing power. Critics note that no study shows values screening predicts later conduct. The vetting does not need one: it rests on the principle that a nation may choose members who share its values. The measured link between diversity and lower trust is real but small on average, strongest for trust in neighbors. About 9% of U.S. births in 2023 (roughly 320,000) were to unauthorized or temporary-status mothers. Enforcement positions are set out in [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Border Security and Immigration Enforcement|Border Security and Immigration Enforcement]].

> [!info]- What is birthright citizenship?
> Birthright citizenship is automatic citizenship for anyone born on a country's soil, regardless of the parents' status. In the United States it rests on the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause.

### Border Security and Immigration Enforcement

>[!tldr] In short
> Illegal immigrants are removed by force, the border is heavily fortified, and lethal force is allowed when necessary. Sanctuary cities are abolished. ICE grows, and its officers wear body cameras.

#### Position
Illegal immigrants are removed from the country by force. The border is heavily secured and fortified, and border crossings are defended with lethal force when necessary. Sanctuary cities are abolished at the federal level. ICE is expanded in size, with one reform: all officers wear body cameras.

>[!example]- Documented accounts
> These are individual reported events, not statistics; they illustrate the position and do not measure a trend.
> - **Record crossings:** CBP recorded 302,034 southwest border encounters in December 2023. U.S. Customs and Border Protection (2024) – [CBP Releases December 2023 Monthly Update](https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/national-media-release/cbp-releases-december-2023-monthly-update)
> - **Laken Riley:** Jose Antonio Ibarra, an undocumented Venezuelan immigrant, was found guilty of murdering the Georgia nursing student and sentenced to life. BBC News (2024) – [Laken Riley: Jose Ibarra sentenced to life in prison for US nursing student's murder](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c23829939rjo)

> [!question]- "Can the federal government abolish sanctuary cities without violating states' rights?"
> Abolishing sanctuary jurisdictions is treated as enforcing a federal responsibility that local governments have no authority to obstruct. Immigration and border control are federal powers, as the Supreme Court affirmed in Arizona v. United States. Federal law already bars state and local governments from restricting their officials from sharing immigration-status information with federal authorities under 8 U.S.C. § 1373.

#### Key Elements
- Forceful removal of illegal immigrants.
- Heavily fortified and secured borders.
- Lethal force authorized to defend border crossings when necessary.
- Federal abolition of sanctuary cities.
- Expansion of ICE, with body cameras required for all officers.

#### Rationale
A nation that cannot control who enters cannot enforce its own laws or decide who joins the political community, and the high-trust society I aim for depends on enforced law. Unlawful entry is a violation of the nation's law, and removal is the remedy. Sanctuary policies place local politics above federal law and shield people the law requires to be removed. Expanding ICE gives enforcement the capacity it lacks, and the body-camera requirement applies the same accountability standard set for police. ICE and CBP publish enforcement and encounter statistics that document the scale of the task.

> [!info]- What is a sanctuary city?
> A sanctuary city or jurisdiction is a local government that limits cooperation with federal immigration enforcement, for example by refusing to honor ICE detainer requests or restricting information sharing with federal authorities.

## Investor Visas

>[!tldr] In short
> Status is never sold. Investors pass the same vetting as everyone else; afterward, investment with a proven lawful source and audited job creation may count as one factor.

#### Position
Immigration status is never sold. An investor goes through the same individual values vetting and civics requirements as every other applicant ([[#Immigration and Borders]]). Investment may count as one factor for an economic visa only after vetting is passed, and only with proof that the money was lawfully obtained and audited proof that the promised jobs were created. A payment or gift to the government buys no place in line.

> [!question]- "Investors bring capital and jobs. Why not welcome them?"
> They can be welcome, on the same terms as everyone else. The objection to selling status is that wealth says nothing about whether a person shares the values I admit for, and it opens a path for money of unknown origin. The record bears this out. The Government Accountability Office found that the EB-5 investor program faced fraud risks unique to it, including difficulty verifying that invested funds were lawfully obtained and schemes to defraud investors, and that the government's reports of the program's economic benefits were not reliable. Investment is a real benefit, so it may count once vetting is passed; it is never a substitute for vetting.

#### Key Elements
- No immigration status for money alone; payments or gifts buy no place in line.
- Investors pass the same values vetting and civics requirements as everyone else.
- After vetting, investment may count as one factor for an economic visa.
- Lawful source of funds proven; job creation audited.

#### Rationale
Admission is a privilege granted to those who fit the shared value structure ([[2. Political and Social Character#Western Values and Compatibility|Western Values and Compatibility]]). Selling status replaces that test with wealth, and the EB-5 record shows the fraud risks that come with it.

## Removal of Non-Citizen Residents and Loss of Citizenship

>[!tldr] In short
> Green card and visa holders are removed, after a court finding by clear and convincing evidence, if they take cash welfare, SNAP, housing aid, non-emergency Medicaid, or the [[Glossary#Last-resort allowance|last-resort allowance]], fail the naturalization English and civics test within two years, or are convicted of any crime above a basic infraction. Refugees go to a safe third country, never to persecution or torture. No one is removed for opinions or speech. Terrorism, material support, and treason are punished, not deported: death where the capital standard is met, secure confinement under compelled labor otherwise, for citizens and non-citizens alike. A constitutional amendment adds [[Glossary#Rights forfeiture|forfeiture]] of citizenship for dual nationals so convicted, never leaving anyone stateless and never applied retroactively.

#### Position
Lawful residence in the United States for a non-citizen is conditional on self-support, integration, and obeying the law. The rule covers green card holders and visa holders, including those already living in the country, and applies only to conduct after it takes effect. A covered non-citizen is removed when a court finds, by clear and convincing evidence, any of the following:
- **Public benefits:** receiving cash welfare, SNAP, housing assistance, non-emergency Medicaid, or the last-resort allowance. Emergency medical care does not count, and neither do benefits received by a U.S.-citizen child.
- **Failure to integrate:** not passing the English and civics test used for naturalization within two years of admission.
- **Crime:** conviction of any criminal offense above a basic infraction. Traffic tickets and similar infractions are excluded; theft and anything more serious leads to removal, with zero tolerance. Removal follows the sentence and never replaces it: the offender first serves the full punishment set out in criminal law, and capital crimes are punished as capital crimes.

Opinions, beliefs, and speech are never grounds for removal. Each case requires written notice, a hearing before a judge, the chance to present evidence, and review in federal court ([[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Due Process and Rule of Law|Due Process and Rule of Law]]). Refugees and asylees are not exempt. They are removed swiftly, but to a safe third country rather than to a country where they would face persecution or torture; I owe them safety, not more. I believe a third country counts as safe only after review of that person's case, not on diplomatic assurances alone. Each person gets written notice naming the country and a hearing on any fear claim before removal.

Terrorism, material support to a designated terrorist organization, and treason are never handled by deportation instead of punishment, whoever commits them, citizen or not. They are prosecuted as crimes and treated as attempted murder is: where guilt meets the [[Glossary#Capital evidentiary standard|capital evidentiary standard]], the sentence is death ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty|Mandatory Death Penalty]]; [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Evidentiary Standard|Evidentiary Standard]]); where it is proven beyond a reasonable doubt but falls short of that standard, the offender is held in secure confinement under compelled labor ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor|Compelled Labor]]). Any knowing support for a designated terrorist organization counts, not only an attack. For a capital sentence, the support must be proven by a documented money trail or communications that meet the capital evidentiary standard. Deportation would only move the threat elsewhere and let it return; punishment ends it.

A dual national convicted of one of these offenses also forfeits U.S. citizenship as an added penalty alongside the sentence, never in place of it. The rule applies equally to dual nationals by birth and by naturalization, applies only to conduct after it takes effect, and never leaves anyone stateless. The law names offenses, not religions. Because the Supreme Court holds that citizenship cannot be taken without the citizen's consent, the forfeiture is enacted by constitutional amendment. The removal rules for non-citizens are enacted by statute.

> [!question]- "Isn't 'failing to integrate' too vague to enforce?"
> It would be if it meant attitudes or lifestyle, so it does not. The only test is the one the country already uses for new citizens: federal law requires naturalization applicants to show "an understanding of the English language" and knowledge of U.S. history and government. A resident who cannot pass that test within two years of admission has not met the condition. Broader standards, such as hostility to the country's way of life, are left out because they cannot be measured and would turn removal into a penalty for speech ([[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Free Speech and Censorship|Free Speech and Censorship]]).

> [!question]- "Isn't removal for any theft too harsh?"
> Residence is a privilege extended on condition of obeying the law, and a resident who steals has broken that condition. Basic infractions such as traffic tickets are excluded so that the rule reaches crimes against people and property, not paperwork. Current law already makes many non-citizens deportable for crimes; this rule removes the gaps and the discretion, with a conviction and a court finding required in every case. The rule applies the same way to a long-term green-card holder with citizen family: residence was granted on the same condition, and the rule reaches only conduct after enactment, so every resident knows the terms in advance. Family members who are citizens keep their citizenship and may choose to stay or to leave.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the Constitution forbid stripping citizenship?"
> For involuntary loss, yes, which is why this part requires an amendment. In Afroyim v. Rusk (1967) the Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment defines "a citizenship which a citizen keeps unless he voluntarily relinquishes it," and in Vance v. Terrazas (1980) it required proof of intent to give up citizenship. In Trop v. Dulles (1958) a plurality held that stripping citizenship as punishment was cruel and unusual, noting that "statelessness is not to be imposed as punishment for crime." The amendment answers the first point directly and the second by limiting the rule to dual nationals, so no one is left without a country. The forfeiture is added to the criminal sentence, death or secure confinement, and never replaces it. Current law already revokes citizenship obtained by fraud, where naturalization was "illegally procured or ... procured by concealment of a material fact."

> [!question]- "Can lawful residents be deported for using benefits or committing crimes without an amendment?"
> Yes, with full due process. The Supreme Court upheld deporting long-time legal residents in Harisiades v. Shaughnessy (1952), treating deportation as a civil matter rather than punishment. In Zadvydas v. Davis (2001) it held that "the Due Process Clause applies to all persons within the United States, including aliens, whether their presence is lawful, unlawful, temporary, or permanent," which is why every case here goes through a hearing, the clear-and-convincing standard, and court review. Existing law already points this way. A non-citizen who becomes "a public charge" within five years of entry from causes not shown to have arisen after entry is deportable. Most qualified non-citizens are barred from federal means-tested benefits for their first five years.

> [!question]- "Isn't it unfair to people who paid taxes and lost a job or fell ill?"
> The rule applies only to benefits received after it takes effect, so no one is removed for past use that was lawful when it happened. Emergency care is never counted, and a U.S.-citizen child keeps any benefit the child qualifies for without putting the parent's residence at risk. Residents know the condition in advance and can protect themselves through work, savings, private insurance, or sponsors. The last-resort allowance is not available to non-citizens ([[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Welfare and Entitlements|Welfare and Entitlements]]); a resident who needs long-term support can return to the country of citizenship.

> [!question]- "Doesn't removing refugees violate the ban on returning people to persecution?"
> Not when they go to a safe third country. Federal law bars removal to a country where a person's "life or freedom would be threatened" because of race, religion, nationality, social group, or political opinion, though that bar has exceptions for serious criminals and security threats. A separate statute, section 2242 of the Foreign Affairs Reform and Restructuring Act of 1998, makes it "the policy of the United States" not to return anyone to a country "in which there are substantial grounds for believing the person would be in danger of being subjected to torture," and its regulations bar removal where torture is "more likely than not," with no such exceptions. That statute takes its terms and its regulations from the Convention Against Torture, so the law ought to write the torture bar into the Immigration and Nationality Act on its own terms, using the federal criminal code's definition of torture, so that it holds whether or not the United States stays in the Convention ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#^treaty-exit|treaty exit]]). Removal to a third country that will receive the person meets both bars. Federal law already lists additional countries to which a person may be removed when the first choice will not accept them.

> [!question]- "Isn't death for material support far beyond current law?"
> Yes. Under 18 U.S.C. § 2339B, knowingly providing material support to a designated foreign terrorist organization carries up to 20 years in prison, or any term of years or life "if the death of any person results." I go further on principle. Money and communications are what let a terrorist organization kill, so knowing support is treated as taking part in its attempts at murder. Because the penalty is death, the proof must be a documented money trail or communications that meet the capital evidentiary standard, with no conviction on inference alone. Cases proven beyond a reasonable doubt but short of that standard go to secure confinement under compelled labor.

> [!question]- "The immigration courts are already overwhelmed. How can they handle removal for every theft?"
> They cannot as they stand. At the end of August 2026 the immigration courts had 3,092,988 pending cases, even after closing 899,741 cases so far in fiscal year 2026, and new removal grounds would add to that load. The United States therefore ought to pair the rules with capacity: dedicated removal courts, separate from the asylum backlog, hear these cases. They are funded with enough judges, interpreters, and public defenders to decide cases within 180 days from notice. For crime cases, the removal hearing is joined to the end of the criminal sentence, since the conviction already establishes the ground. A deadline without the judges to meet it would trade due process for speed, which the United States ought not to allow.

> [!question]- "Doesn't this create two classes of citizens?"
> No. Every offender, citizen or not, faces the same criminal punishment for terrorism or treason: death where the capital standard is met, secure confinement under compelled labor otherwise. The only difference is the added forfeiture of citizenship for dual nationals, which exists for dual nationals alone so that no one becomes stateless.

> [!question]- "Why the focus on Islamist terrorism if the law is religion-neutral?"
> Because the data put it first, though not alone. The Global Terrorism Index 2026 found that four jihadist organizations, the Islamic State, JNIM, the Pakistani Taliban, and al-Shabaab, caused 3,869 deaths in 2025, 70 per cent of all terrorism deaths worldwide. In the West, 527 of 990 terrorism deaths over the past two decades, 53 per cent, occurred in religiously motivated attacks, and almost all of those were caused by jihadist groups or individuals. The same report records the counter-trend. Religious attacks were just over ten per cent of the 1,449 Western attacks since 2007, 30 per cent had a political motivation and 32 per cent no single clear ideology, politically motivated attacks rose by almost 20 per cent in 2025, and the high-profile attacks that year included the assassination of Charlie Kirk. A law that names offenses rather than faiths covers every one of these threats.

#### Key Elements
- Covers green card holders and visa holders, including current residents, for conduct after enactment only.
- Removal on a court finding, by clear and convincing evidence, of benefits received, failure to integrate, or a criminal conviction.
- Benefits that count: cash welfare, SNAP, housing assistance, non-emergency Medicaid, and the last-resort allowance; emergency care and a U.S.-citizen child's benefits excluded.
- Failing to integrate defined only as not passing the naturalization English and civics test within two years of admission.
- Any criminal offense above a basic infraction leads to removal; traffic tickets and similar infractions excluded.
- No removal for opinions, beliefs, or speech.
- Refugees and asylees not exempt; removed swiftly to a safe third country, never to persecution or torture.
- Removal follows any criminal sentence and never replaces it.
- Terrorism, material support, and treason, by citizens or non-citizens, punished as attempted murder is: death at the capital evidentiary standard, otherwise secure confinement under compelled labor; never deportation instead of punishment.
- Any knowing support for a designated terrorist organization is capital when proven by a documented money trail or communications meeting the capital evidentiary standard.
- Dual nationals convicted of these offenses also forfeit citizenship as an added penalty; religion-neutral in law, prospective only, never leaving anyone stateless.
- Citizenship forfeiture by constitutional amendment; residency rules by statute.
- Citizenship obtained by fraud is revoked as current law provides (8 U.S.C. § 1451): only by a federal court, on clear, unequivocal and convincing evidence, and only for concealment of a material fact or willful misrepresentation. This corrects a grant that was never lawful and is separate from the forfeiture amendment.
- A third country counts as safe only after review of the person's case, not on assurances alone; written notice naming the country and a hearing on any fear claim come before removal.

#### Rationale
I treat immigration as a privilege the nation grants to those who fit its shared values, with assimilation paramount ([[#Immigration and Borders|Immigration and Borders]]; [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Language and Assimilation|Language and Assimilation]]). Federal law already reflects the self-support condition. A non-citizen who becomes a public charge within five years of entry is deportable, and qualified non-citizens who entered on or after August 22, 1996 are generally barred from federal means-tested benefits for five years. Federal housing assistance is limited to listed categories of non-citizens. The public-charge rules for admission have shifted with administrations: a 2019 rule stopped being in effect on March 9, 2021, and a 2022 rule similar to the 1999 guidance took effect on December 23, 2022. Writing the condition into statute, with a court finding by clear and convincing evidence in every case, makes it stable. The design adapts two pledges from Britain's Restore Britain party: to deport a legally resident foreign national who "lives in social housing, claims benefits," "fails to integrate," or "commits crime" (Restore Britain – [Reverse Mass Immigration](https://www.restorebritain.org.uk/immigration_border_control)), and to strip citizenship from "dual-national Islamic extremists" (Restore Britain – [Make Britain Safe Again](https://www.restorebritain.org.uk/law_order_criminal_justice)). The United States ought to narrow both: the benefits are listed, integration is a two-year test, every removal needs a court finding, and the citizenship penalty follows a conviction under religion-neutral offenses and never leaves anyone stateless ([[11. Modern Political Parties#United Kingdom|United Kingdom]]). The safe-third-country rule keeps the United States within its obligations against returning people to persecution or torture without granting refugees more than safety. Terrorism is different in kind. An attack meant to kill is an attempt at murder, and I treat attempted murder as capital ([[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Terrorism and Domestic Extremism|Terrorism and Domestic Extremism]]); deporting a terrorist would only move the threat abroad, where it can plan again, while execution or secure confinement ends it. Citizenship forfeiture is kept as an added penalty for dual nationals so that an offender's tie to the country ends with the crime. The Court's reading of the Fourteenth Amendment in Afroyim means involuntary loss requires an amendment, and my concern for rights means the amendment must require a criminal conviction and must never create statelessness. The offenses are religion-neutral, but the emphasis on Islamist terrorism follows the data. Jihadist groups caused 70 per cent of terrorism deaths worldwide in 2025 and most terrorism deaths in the West over two decades, while political and mixed-ideology attacks are more numerous in the West and rising. Removal for belief or speech is excluded because I protect expression even when it's hostile.

## Mexico and Latin America

>[!tldr] In short
> Cartels are treated as terrorists and destroyed, ideally together with the local government. A government that refuses to help can face war, but the goal is to defeat the cartels, not to run the country. Many Latin American immigrants are seen as good fits because of shared values.

#### Position
Drug cartels are treated as terrorist national-security threats and are destroyed by force. Joint operations with compliant Central and South American governments, including Mexico, are the preferred route. A government that refuses to act against cartels operating against the United States is treated as hostile, and war with that government is justified to eradicate the cartel problem. Action aims at defeating the threat, not at governing territory or rebuilding states. Immigrants from the region are vetted individually, and many Latin American families are judged compatible because of shared family and religious values. I believe a foreign leader who directs or shelters cartels or terror groups against the United States may be captured to stand trial in U.S. courts. That targets the person driving the threat and is not nation-building, and the United States does not then govern the country.

> [!question]- "Wouldn't military action in Mexico risk a disastrous war with a neighbor?"
> Joint operations with a cooperating government come first, and most cartel action is meant to be carried out that way. War is reserved for a government that refuses to act against organizations killing Americans. Even then, the goal is to destroy the cartels, not to occupy or rebuild the country, in line with the skepticism of nation-building in [[#Interventionism vs Non-Intervention]].

#### Key Elements
- Cartels are terrorist national-security threats, destroyed by force.
- Joint operations with compliant governments are preferred.
- Refusal to act against cartels is treated as hostility; war is justified as a last resort.
- No occupation or nation-building.
- Immigration from the region goes through individual values vetting; many families are compatible.
- Foreign leaders who direct or shelter cartels or terror groups against the United States may be captured for trial in U.S. courts, without the United States governing the country.

#### Rationale
Cartel-supplied drugs kill Americans at a scale comparable to war. CDC provisional data show an estimated 70,735 drug overdose deaths in 2025, of which 38,506 involved synthetic opioids, mainly fentanyl, down from about 81,300 in 2024 and a peak of about 112,400 in the 12 months ending August 2023. In 2025 the United States designated Cartel de Sinaloa, CJNG, MS-13, Tren de Aragua, and other groups as Foreign Terrorist Organizations, which aligns legal treatment with the threat. Domestic enforcement is set out in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Drug Policy|Drug Policy]], and the war position in [[7. Cultural & Social Issues#Drugs and Vice|Cultural & Social Issues]].

## Israel and the Middle East

>[!tldr] In short
> I back Israel as the first line of Western defense against the Islamic Republic of Iran and the forces of terror in the region. The Iranian regime and its allies in state-sponsored, anti-Western terror, among them Hezbollah, the Houthis, Hamas, and Palestinian Islamic Jihad, ought to be destroyed, sparing ordinary Iranians, and extremist Islam eliminated. Hamas is condemned as a terrorist organization. I believe a two-state solution is not viable and that Gaza should likely be dissolved. I believe a negotiated middle ground on the West Bank would be best but is unlikely; failing that, its armed threat should be forcibly removed and the territory walled off from Israel entirely. Palestinian civilians should not be targeted, the hostages should be returned, and I believe the war in Gaza is categorically not a genocide. I believe Israel defends negative rights against an ideology that rejects them.

#### Position
We should be supporting Israel as the first line of Western defense against the barbaric Islamic Republic and the forces of terror in the region. Extremist Islam should be eradicated. More force should be used toward Iran, and I judge the current administration too soft. Iran's people should be spared, but the Islamic regime should be obliterated along with all allies of state-sponsored, anti-Western terror. Destroying the regime is not followed by nation-building or occupation. Israel should be supported as far as it serves U.S. security, toward completely erasing the stain of extremist Islam from the world. I believe Israel defends negative rights against an ideology, [[2. Political and Social Character#Islamism|Islamism]], that rejects them.

Those allies include Hezbollah, the Houthis (Ansarallah), Palestinian Islamic Jihad, and Iraqi militias such as Kata'ib Hezbollah. Hezbollah and Palestinian Islamic Jihad have been U.S.-designated Foreign Terrorist Organizations since 1997, Kata'ib Hezbollah since 2009, and the Houthis again since March 2025, and the State Department reports that Iran backs every one of them. Hamas is another. It has been a U.S.-designated Foreign Terrorist Organization since 1997, Iran has supported it materially for decades, and on October 7, 2023 it led an attack that killed more than 1,200 people in Israel. Hamas is condemned as a terrorist organization, and its organized violent wing treated as an enemy force, consistent with how Islamism is ranked on this site ([[2. Political and Social Character#Islamism|Islamism]]). I believe October 7 was an attempt at ethnic cleansing, an atrocity committed on ethnic and religious grounds. The West Point Combating Terrorism Center describes the attack as Hamas's first to explicitly target children and the elderly, and notes that Hamas attacks were meant to make civilians leave the land it claims; a UN Commission of Inquiry found that civilians were deliberately targeted.

I believe a two-state solution is not viable, and Gaza should likely be dissolved, since it has proven time and again hostile and uncooperative toward Israel. Palestinian civilians should not be targeted; I believe Hamas is the one endangering them, by operating among them. Israel warns before strikes, sends aid that I believe Hamas often steals or diverts, and arranges medical care for civilians. I believe the war is categorically not a genocide, and I believe Palestinian reporting and the UN are biased against Israel. I do not think it out of the realm of possibility that UN, USAID, and certain neutral-party statistics have been ideologically driven or manipulated to align with the genocide claim, since aid and diplomatic groups have an interest in ending armed conflict. All hostages should be returned to Israel, and I believe Israel gave Hamas many chances at a deal that Hamas refused. I believe large shares of Palestinians openly support Hamas and October 7 and are hostile and racist toward Jews and Israel, while Israel is not racist toward Arabs: Arab citizens hold seats in its parliament and positions of power in its government.

I believe the West Bank likely cannot be assimilated into Israel, given widespread anti-Israeli sympathy there: in an October 2025 poll, 59% of West Bank respondents called Hamas's decision to launch October 7 correct. I believe a negotiated middle ground would be best but is unlikely. Failing that, the West Bank's armed threat should be forcibly removed and the territory walled off from Israel entirely, separated by hard security, with violence met by force against those who commit it. I also believe Israel would be justified in annexing the land outright, provided the armed threat is removed. People in the West Bank who choose to join Israel and assimilate properly should be accepted; those who refuse and remain hostile, as found individually by a court or tribunal under the conduct test in [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#^hostile-forfeit|Hostile conduct, removal, and entry]], should be forced out. Under current polling, the West Bank and Gaza belong on the entry-ban list ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#^hostile-forfeit|Hostile conduct, removal, and entry]]). Given how widely hostility runs there, I believe hostile conduct of that kind amounts to a security threat, and a nation's security and duty to its citizens override the negative rights of foreign security threats ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sovereignty and Borders|Sovereignty and Borders]]). I believe international law should be disregarded here, since a nation follows its own laws, not foreign tribunals, and a nation should exit any treaty that ties its hands against such threats ([[#Global Institutions|Global Institutions]]). I prefer separation to indefinite control because a perpetual security state is costly and dangerous and not in Israel's best interest.

Israel is a democratic Western society, mostly, and Palestine is not. I believe Palestine is incompatible with the West and, by extension, an enemy of the United States. Israel is the barrier against barbarians who want the destruction of the West and the largest force in the region against Islamism, so we must aid and support it as necessary.

I am skeptical of politically charged alignment with the Palestinian cause. Left-wing politicians and progressives have rallied behind "Free Palestine" as a cause, and I believe a lot of misinformation and propaganda spread by Hamas itself is eaten up by pro-Palestinian audiences in Europe and the United States. That is not to discount Israeli propaganda either.

> [!question]- "Can the Iranian regime be destroyed without harming its people?"
> The United States ought to make sparing the Iranian people a requirement while targeting the regime and its terror allies. The distinction between a hostile government and the population it rules is central: the enemy is the regime and its proxies, not Iranians.

> [!question]- "What is the history of the conflict?"

Jewish immigration rose sharply after 1933. The Arab Revolt of 1936–39 followed, and in 1937 the Peel Commission judged the Mandate unworkable and recommended partition. |

Israel declared independence on May 14, 1948, and Arab states invaded. Armistices in 1949 left Egypt holding Gaza and Jordan the West Bank. About 750,000 Palestinians became refugees (the Nakba); Israel then estimated no more than 550,000 and Arab states 750,000–800,000. Historians debate how much of the flight was expulsion and how much was flight from fighting. |

About 586,000 went to Israel between 1948 and 1972, and most of the rest to France, Britain, and the Americas. Causes are disputed: Israel points to riots, anti-Jewish laws, and confiscations, while Arab governments have said the Jews left by choice. |

UN Resolution 242 called for withdrawal from territories occupied in exchange for peace. The Arab League's Khartoum resolution answered: "no peace with Israel, no recognition of Israel, no negotiations with it." |

> My view: I believe this history shows Palestinian and Arab leaders rejecting partition and recognition several times, which is part of why I believe a two-state solution is not viable.

> [!question]- "What happened on October 7, 2023?"
> Hamas led an attack from the Gaza Strip into Israel, beginning with a barrage of more than 3,000 rockets. More than 1,200 Israelis and foreign nationals were killed, including 46 U.S. citizens, and Hamas and other groups seized some 251 hostages. The Congressional Research Service notes allegations of sexual violence during the assault. The State Department designated Hamas a Foreign Terrorist Organization on October 8, 1997. Iran has provided material support to Hamas for decades.
>
> Rocket fire on this scale was not new, but the war multiplied it. In the 2014 Gaza war, Palestinian armed groups fired 4,881 rockets and 1,753 mortars toward Israel, according to the UN Commission of Inquiry. In the 11-day May 2021 conflict, Israel said about 4,360 rockets were fired from Gaza, about 680 of which fell short inside the Strip (Reuters). For October 7 itself, the IDF puts the barrage at 5,000 or more. By the war's first anniversary, the IDF counted about 26,240 rockets, missiles, and drones fired at Israel: 13,200 from Gaza, 12,400 from Lebanon, 400 from Iran in two direct attacks in April and October 2024, 180 from Yemen, and 60 from Syria (Reuters). By October 2026 its count had reached about 31,540: about 10,200 from Gaza, 20,000 from Lebanon, 1,200 from Iran, 90 from Yemen, and 50 from Syria. These are IDF figures; the 2026 Gaza total is lower than the 2024 one, which suggests a change in counting method, and the IDF does not count launches that fell short or failed to reach Israel, which it says number in the thousands.
>

BBC (2024) – [Surviving October 7th: We Will Dance Again](https://www.bbc.com/mediacentre/proginfo/2024/39/surviving-october-seventh-we-will-dance-again)

> [!question]- "Is it a genocide?"
> I believe it is categorically not a genocide. Israel has the military power to level Gaza if that were its aim, and I believe the numbers do not reflect genocide or ethnic cleansing. I believe Gaza's population did not dip anywhere near the proportions other genocides do relative to the population. The Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics estimated about 2.13 million people at the end of 2025, about 10.6% below its pre-war projection, a figure that counts deaths, departures, and fewer births.
>
> | Case | Killed | Targeted population | Share killed |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Holocaust, European Jews (1933–1945) | about 6 million | about 9.5 million (1933) | about 2 in 3 |
> | Rwanda, Tutsi (1994) | more than 500,000 | Tutsi minority | about 3 in 4 in the one province studied in detail |
> | Cambodia, Khmer Rouge (1975–1979), all groups | about 1.7 million | whole population | about 21% |
> | Srebrenica, Bosnian Muslims (1995) | 7,000–8,000 men and boys | about 40,000 | about 1 in 5 |
> | Gaza, health ministry count reported by UN OCHA (to October 2026) | 74,250 | about 2.2 million before the war | about 3.4% |
> | Gaza, Lancet estimate (October 2023 to June 2024 only) | 64,260 | about 2.2 million before the war | about 2.9% |
>
> The Gaza figures include combatants, and the Lancet estimate covers only the first nine months. The Armenian genocide is left out for lack of a neutral demographic estimate I could verify. Under the Genocide Convention, genocide turns on intent to destroy a group "in whole or in part," not on a set share killed; international tribunals require that the targeted part be substantial, judged by its size relative to the group and other factors.
>
> Others disagree, and the legal question is unresolved. South Africa brought a genocide case against Israel at the International Court of Justice in December 2023. The Court ordered provisional measures in January, March, and May 2024 but has not ruled on the merits; it set deadlines for further written pleadings running to May 2029, and Israel has objected to the Court's jurisdiction. In September 2025, a UN Commission of Inquiry concluded that Israel had committed genocide in Gaza, a finding Israel rejects.
>
> No independent count separates combatants from civilians. A peer-reviewed study in The Lancet, by researchers at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine, estimated 64,260 traumatic-injury deaths from October 2023 to June 2024, about 41% more than the Gaza health ministry reported; 59% of deaths with known age and sex were women, children, and older people, and the study did not estimate how many were combatants. A household survey published in The Lancet Global Health in 2026 estimated about 75,200 violent deaths through January 5, 2025, about 3.4% of the pre-war population, and found 56% were women, children, or people over 64; a re-analysis in the same journal found interviewer-level deviations and said dropping two outlying teams lowers the estimate by about 16,000. A 2024 modeling study in Frontiers in Public Health estimated that combatants were 12.7% of deaths in 2023, an estimate built on health ministry data and Israeli claims. Israel's military has claimed about 17,000 Hamas fighters killed by October 2024.
>
> Some statistics have been revised or disputed. In May 2024 the UN switched its breakdown of Gaza deaths from the Hamas-run Government Media Office, which put women and children at 69%, to health ministry data on identified bodies, where they were 52%, while saying the overall toll had not changed. In June 2024 the IPC's Famine Review Committee found a famine analysis by FEWS NET, the USAID-funded early-warning network, not plausible given uncertain and non-convergent evidence, and a separate review that month found the evidence did "not indicate that Famine is currently occurring," while warning of a high risk. In August 2025 the Committee did confirm famine, "with reasonable evidence," in Gaza Governorate as of August 15, with about 514,000 people in catastrophic conditions and famine projected to spread to Deir al-Balah and Khan Younis; the IPC later added an addendum clarifying the malnutrition data it used after questions were raised about its methods. Israel's COGAT rejected the finding as relying on "partial and unreliable sources, many of them affiliated with Hamas." In December 2025, after the October ceasefire improved aid access, the IPC classified no area of Gaza in famine, though it put the whole Strip in Emergency (Phase 4) and about 1.6 million people in Crisis or worse. Cutting the other way, the Lancet capture-recapture study and the Lancet Global Health household survey both estimated more violent deaths than the health ministry reported, not fewer.
>
> My view: I believe Palestinian reporting and the UN are biased against Israel, and I weigh their findings with that in mind. I do not think it out of the realm of possibility that UN, USAID, and certain neutral-party statistics have been ideologically driven or manipulated to align with the genocide claim, since aid and diplomatic groups have an interest in ending armed conflict.

> [!question]- "Is a two-state solution viable?"
> I believe it is not, and that Gaza should likely be dissolved, since it has proven time and again hostile and uncooperative toward Israel. Palestinian opinion is divided: in an October 2026 poll by the Palestinian Center for Policy and Survey Research, 53% of Palestinians supported a two-state solution on the 1967 lines and 44% opposed it.

> [!question]- "Do Palestinians support Hamas and October 7?"
> Many do. In polls by the Palestinian Center for Policy and Survey Research, the share of Palestinians calling Hamas's decision to launch the October 7 attack correct was 71% in March 2024 and 53% in October 2025, 59% in the West Bank and 44% in Gaza. In the October 2025 poll, nearly all respondents said they did not believe Hamas committed the atrocities against civilians shown in international media. Support for Hamas as a party has since fallen, to 19% in October 2026. Before the war, an Arab Barometer survey taken from September 28 to October 8, 2023 found 21% support for Hamas; it rose to 43% by early December 2023 but never reached a majority in the West Bank or Gaza. In a 2009 Pew Research Center survey, 97% of Palestinians held an unfavorable view of Jews; no newer neutral survey on that question was found. I believe this support reflects open hostility and racism toward Jews and Israel. ^pcpsr-oct7

> [!question]- "What does Hamas say it wants?"
> Its founding 1988 charter opens with the quote "Israel will exist and will continue to exist until Islam will obliterate it," states that "there is no solution for the Palestinian question except through Jihad," quotes a hadith calling on Muslims to kill Jews hiding "behind stones and trees," and cites the forged "Protocols of the Elders of Zion." Its 2017 policy document says its conflict "is with the Zionist project not with the Jews because of their religion," rejects "any alternative to the full and complete liberation of Palestine, from the river to the sea," refuses to recognize Israel, and calls a state on the 1967 lines only "a formula of national consensus." On October 24, 2023, Hamas official Ghazi Hamad said on Lebanese television that October 7 "is just the first time, and there will be a second, a third, a fourth." I believe these words support my view that Hamas is condemned as a terrorist organization.

> [!question]- "Who funds Hamas?"
> The State Department says Iran gives Hamas money, weapons, and training, up to about \$100 million a year in combined support to Palestinian militant groups. It also found "no evidence that Iran knew about" October 7 in advance. CRS cites a 2024 estimate that Hamas's yearly revenue tops \$1 billion, about \$360 million of it from taxes on goods entering Gaza, with foreign sources including Iran and private entities in Qatar, Turkey, and other states.

> [!question]- "How has Hamas governed Gaza?"
> Hamas has held no election since 2006. CRS calls it Gaza's "de facto authoritarian ruler." The State Department reported that as of November 30, 2023 Hamas courts had sentenced 25 people to death, 13 for "collaboration," and carried out executions without the PA approval required by Palestinian law. It also reported that from January to September 2023 Hamas detained 60 Palestinians for political affiliation, criticism of Hamas, or suspected collaboration, and reported violence against LGBTQI+ people. During the 2014 war, Hamas's armed wing claimed the firing-squad executions of 21 alleged collaborators.

> [!question]- "What about the occupation and settlements?"
> In a July 2024 advisory opinion, the International Court of Justice found, 11 votes to 4, that Israel's continued presence in the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and Gaza is unlawful and must end "as rapidly as possible," and, 14 to 1, that Israel must stop new settlement activity and evacuate settlers. Advisory opinions are not binding; Vice-President Sebutinde dissented on every point, and Judges Tomka, Abraham, and Aurescu dissented on the core findings. The UN human rights office, citing the Israeli group Peace Now, put the settler population in 2023 at about 465,000 in the West Bank and 230,000 in East Jerusalem. UN OCHA counted 509 Palestinians killed by Israeli forces and settlers in the West Bank and East Jerusalem in 2023, the deadliest year since its records began in 2005, and before October 7 counted 26 Israelis killed in the West Bank. Forcible transfer of a civilian population is prohibited under the Fourth Geneva Convention (Article 49) and the Rome Statute.
>
> My view: Forcing hostile residents out conflicts with Article 49 of the Fourth Geneva Convention and the Rome Statute, and the Court found Israel's presence unlawful. Neither changes my position. The opinion is advisory and drew dissents on its core findings, and I believe a nation follows its own laws, not foreign tribunals ([[#Global Institutions|Global Institutions]]). Nor do I want indefinite control: a perpetual security state is costly and dangerous and not in Israel's best interest, so I prefer a negotiated middle ground and, failing that, separation behind hard security, with force aimed at those who commit violence. Residents who choose to join Israel and assimilate are accepted; only those who refuse and remain hostile are forced out, because given how widely hostility runs there I treat hostile conduct as a security threat ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sovereignty and Borders|Sovereignty and Borders]]; [[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#^hostile-forfeit|Hostile conduct, removal, and entry]]).

> [!question]- "Does the Palestinian Authority reward attacks?"
> The 2018 Taylor Force Act bars most U.S. economic aid to the Palestinian Authority until it ends payments to people jailed for, or killed while committing, attacks and to their families. In April 2026 the State Department said it could not certify that the payments had ended. Citing Israel's foreign minister, it said the PA paid \$156 million in 2025, \$126 million to prisoners and released prisoners and \$30 million to families of attackers who died, now through a new welfare agency. On schooling, a 2021 EU-commissioned study of PA textbooks by Germany's Georg Eckert Institute found they met UNESCO standards on themes such as human rights but also contained "anti-Semitic narratives and glorifications of violence." It found no direct calls for violence against Israelis and said 2020 revisions reduced some escalatory content.

> [!question]- "Why are you skeptical of the Free Palestine movement?"
> I am skeptical of politically charged alignment with the Palestinian cause. Left-wing politicians and progressives have rallied behind "Free Palestine" as a cause, and I believe a lot of misinformation and propaganda spread by Hamas itself is eaten up by pro-Palestinian audiences in Europe and the United States. That is not to discount Israeli propaganda either. Some documented cases support the concern about Hamas. After the October 17, 2023 blast at Gaza's al-Ahli hospital, Hamas officials blamed an Israeli airstrike and cited about 500 dead. A U.S. intelligence assessment judged Israel was not responsible and estimated deaths at the low end of 100 to 300, and the New York Times said its early coverage "relied too heavily on claims by Hamas." In May 2024 the UN changed its breakdown of Gaza deaths from figures of Hamas's Government Media Office, which put women and children at 69%, to health ministry data on identified bodies, where they were 52%; the UN said the overall toll had not fallen and that women and children were still about 60% if the identified breakdown is applied to all deaths. Misinformation and covert influence run both ways: Meta removed an Israeli network, which it linked to the Tel Aviv firm STOIC, of 510 fake Facebook accounts that targeted U.S. and Canadian audiences and U.S. lawmakers, and a separate Iranian network posing as Israelis. U.S. opinion has split along party lines: in Gallup's February 2026 poll, 65% of Democrats sympathized more with the Palestinians and 17% with the Israelis, while 70% of Republicans sympathized more with the Israelis; overall, Americans sided with the Palestinians 41% to 36%, the first time in Gallup's series since 2001. Pew found in September 2025 that 84% of Americans viewed Hamas unfavorably, including wide majorities of both parties, so sympathy for Palestinians does not mean support for Hamas. In Western Europe, YouGov's May 2025 EuroTrack found more people sided with the Palestinians than the Israelis in all six countries polled: Britain 32% to 14%, Spain 33% to 15%, Italy 31% to 7%, Denmark 28% to 18%, France 24% to 18%, and Germany 18% to 17%, with a fifth to two-fifths unsure in each. On U.S. campuses, the Crowd Counting Consortium at Harvard and the University of Connecticut logged more than 3,700 days of pro-Palestine protest at 525 schools from October 7, 2023 to late May 2024, with property damage, mostly graffiti, on 33 of them and police injuries on 8, and 3,645 arrests; it found protesters were injured by police or counter-protesters about as often as protesters caused property damage. ACLED found 97% of U.S. student demonstrations from October 7, 2023 to May 3, 2024 were peaceful, meaning they involved no serious violence or property damage. The protests were nonviolent for the most part, sure, but I believe they were often aggressive and fascistic against others. In one documented case, a federal judge found in August 2024 that "Jewish students were excluded from portions of the UCLA campus because they refused to denounce their faith," noting that UCLA did not dispute it, and ordered the university not to allow campus areas to be closed to Jewish students, including over their beliefs about Israel (Frankel v. Regents of the University of California).

> [!question]- "Is Israel racist against Arabs?"
> I believe it is not. Arab citizens make up about 21% of Israel's population (2.15 million at the end of 2025), vote, and sit in the Knesset, and in 2021 the Arab party Ra'am became the first independent Arab party to join an Israeli governing coalition. In 2022 Khaled Kabub became the first Muslim to hold a permanent seat on Israel's Supreme Court, which has had an Arab justice since 2003; all earlier Arab appointees were Christian. In 2021/22 about 60,600 Arab students made up 18% of students in Israeli higher education, against 21% of the population, up 133% over the decade. Large gaps remain: the OECD found that Arab-Israelis' hourly wages are about half those of non-Haredi Jewish men, and attributed the gap to education, language, location, and "partly also possible discrimination," citing audit studies that found discrimination against Arab-Israelis in hiring for some services and in rental housing. The State Department reported that planning for Arab areas lags far behind Jewish municipalities, leaving about 50,000 Arab families in unpermitted homes, and that the 2018 Nation-State Law downgraded Arabic from an official language. The OECD found Arab-Israeli women have the lowest employment rates of any group, and that gaps in schooling, Hebrew fluency, and distance from jobs explain much of the wage gap. In medicine, a 2024 peer-reviewed study found that in 2023 Arab citizens made up 25% of Israel's employed physicians, up from 8% in 2010, 27% of nurses and dentists, and 49% of pharmacists, compared with 16% of all employment.
>
> Military and police service is uneven. Druze and Circassian men are conscripted; Bedouin, other Muslim, and Christian Arab citizens may volunteer. An IDF colonel who heads the minorities department told The Jerusalem Post in 2025 that Druze enlistment had reached 85% since October 7, that Bedouin service was just over 60%, and that Christian Arab enlistment had tripled in a year, but that Muslim soldiers still number only a few dozen and Christian soldiers a few hundred. Calcalist, citing IDF and national civil service data, reported that in the 2022–23 service year 481 Arab citizens enlisted in the IDF (433 Muslims and Bedouin, 48 Christians) and 3,319 joined civilian national service. In the Israel Police, ministry data reported in 2026 show 3,294 new officers in 2025, of whom 96 were Muslim, 2 Bedouin, and 12 Arab Christian, down from 165, 7, and 59 in 2023, even as overall recruitment rose. These counts exclude the Druze.

> [!question]- "Does Israel protect civilians in Gaza?"
> I believe Palestinian civilians should not be targeted and that Hamas endangers them by operating among them. A 2019 case study by the NATO Strategic Communications Centre of Excellence found that Hamas has used human shields since 2007, launching rockets and placing military infrastructure in or near residential and commercial areas. An analysis in the West Point Combating Terrorism Center's CTC Sentinel reports that Hamas dug its tunnel network near and under schools, mosques, and UN facilities. Israel has issued evacuation orders and warnings before strikes, including "roof knocking," though a 2009 UN fact-finding mission questioned how effective such warnings are when there is little time or no safe place to go. Israel allows aid into Gaza, and patients are evacuated for treatment abroad through Israeli crossings: the World Health Organization counted more than 10,700 medical evacuations by the end of 2025. I believe Hamas often steals or diverts aid. The U.S. evidence is mixed: the USAID Inspector General is investigating credible allegations of Hamas diversion and theft, while an internal USAID review of 156 reported incidents of loss or theft of U.S.-funded aid found no evidence that Hamas benefited. The UN's tracking system reported that 88% of UN aid trucks entering Gaza from May 19 to August 22, 2025 were intercepted, taken "either peacefully by hungry people or forcefully by armed actors," without attributing the losses to Hamas. Israel's military has claimed, based on its intelligence, that Hamas diverted up to 25% of aid; Hamas denies it, and neither claim has been independently verified.
>
> U.S. intelligence, in an assessment declassified in January 2024, said it was confident that Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad used Gaza's al-Shifa hospital complex to command forces and hold at least a few hostages, and that they largely evacuated it days before Israeli troops entered; the evidence was not released. In 2014, UNRWA reported finding weapons hidden in one of its vacant Gaza schools and condemned it as a flagrant violation of its premises; a UN board of inquiry later identified them as a mortar tube and mortar rounds.
>
> On UNRWA, the UN's oversight office reviewed Israel's allegations against 19 of its roughly 13,000 Gaza staff and found that evidence, if authenticated, could indicate nine may have taken part in October 7; they were fired. An independent review led by former French foreign minister Catherine Colonna found UNRWA's neutrality rules the most elaborate in the UN system but in need of improvement, and that Israel had not provided evidence for its broader claim that many UNRWA employees belong to terrorist groups. After the Gaza Humanitarian Foundation began distributing food in May 2025, the UN human rights office counted at least 1,373 Palestinians killed while seeking food by July 31, 859 of them near its sites, most by the Israeli military.

> All hostages should be returned to Israel. Hamas released the last 20 living hostages on October 13, 2025 under a U.S.-brokered ceasefire, and the remains of the last deceased hostage, Ran Gvili, were recovered on January 26, 2026, leaving no hostages in Gaza. I believe Israel gave Hamas many chances at a deal that Hamas refused.

> [!question]- "What has the war cost Gaza?"
> As of October 2024, more than 41,000 Palestinians in Gaza had been killed, according to the Hamas-controlled health ministry, whose count does not separate combatants from civilians. About 90% of Gaza's roughly 2.1 million residents had been displaced, most facing acute shortages of food, water, and medical care. Israel claimed to have killed about 17,000 of Hamas's estimated 25,000 to 30,000 fighters, and at least 346 Israeli soldiers had died in ground operations. These figures are from the Congressional Research Service as of October 2024. By October 2026 the health ministry count reported by UN OCHA had reached 74,250, still without separating combatants (see the table under "Is it a genocide?").

> [!question]- "How much military aid does the U.S. give Israel?"
> Under the 2016 Memorandum of Understanding covering FY2019 to FY2028, the U.S. pledged, subject to appropriation, \$38 billion in military aid: \$33 billion in Foreign Military Financing grants and \$5 billion for missile defense, or about \$3.3 billion and \$500 million a year. After October 7, Congress added emergency aid, including \$3.5 billion in Foreign Military Financing and \$5.2 billion for missile defense and the Iron Beam laser system in April 2024. Israel is the largest cumulative recipient of U.S. foreign assistance since World War II. I support this aid as far as it serves U.S. security ([[8. #### Key Elements
- Strong support for Israel as the front line against Iran and extremist Islam.
- Greater force toward Iran; current policy judged too soft.
- Obliterate the Islamic regime and its state-sponsored terror allies while sparing civilians.
- Hamas condemned as a terrorist organization and treated, with Hezbollah, the Houthis, Palestinian Islamic Jihad, and Iraqi militias such as Kata'ib Hezbollah, as one of Iran's terror allies.
- October 7 judged an attempt at ethnic cleansing.
- Two-state solution judged not viable; Gaza should likely be dissolved.
- West Bank: a negotiated middle ground preferred but judged unlikely; failing that, its armed threat forcibly removed and the territory walled off from Israel, separated by hard security; annexation also judged justified once the armed threat is removed; residents who assimilate accepted, those individually found hostile by conduct removed ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#^hostile-forfeit|conduct test]]); complete entry ban from the West Bank and Gaza under the ban-list rule (regime or dominant factions endorse genocidal attacks, or 5%+ open support; lifted below 1% sustained with no endorsing authority; reviewed yearly); international law disregarded.
- Skepticism of politically charged "Free Palestine" alignment, which I believe spreads Hamas propaganda in the West.
- Palestinian civilians not to be targeted; all hostages returned to Israel.
- The war in Gaza judged not a genocide.
- U.S. military aid to Israel supported as far as it serves U.S. security.
- Eradicate extremist Islam as a global force.

#### Rationale
Iran funds, arms, and directs proxy forces that attack U.S. troops, shipping, and allies, and it has plotted assassinations on U.S. soil. Its proxies have killed and attacked Americans for more than four decades. Hezbollah's 1983 suicide truck bombing of the U.S. Marine barracks in Beirut killed 241 U.S. service members. In 2023 Iran-aligned militias in Iraq and Syria, including Kata'ib Hezbollah, attacked U.S. forces with drones Iran supplied, and since 2023 the Houthis have launched hundreds of attacks on commercial ships in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden and on the U.S. service members defending them. Hamas is among the groups it has supported, and its October 7 attack killed more than 1,200 people in a single day. Israel confronts these forces directly, which serves U.S. interests. Destroying the regime removes the source of the threat rather than managing its symptoms.

### Muslim Brotherhood

#### Position
The United States ought to designate as a terrorist organization every Muslim Brotherhood chapter shown to support Islamist terrorism or to organize violence, and keep the remaining chapters under close watch, designating any chapter as soon as the evidence shows it has crossed that line. The Brotherhood's ideology and network have produced one terrorist group after another: Egyptian Islamic Jihad, which assassinated President Anwar Sadat and merged into al-Qa'ida; Gama'a al-Islamiyya, whose spiritual leader was imprisoned in the United States for his role in the 1993 World Trade Center bombing; HASM and Liwa al-Thawra in Egypt; Palestinian Islamic Jihad; and Hamas, whose 1988 charter calls it a wing of the Brotherhood. In recent years its chapters have fired rockets alongside Hezbollah, fielded fighters trained by Iran's Revolutionary Guard, and built rockets and drones. A legal political wing does not exempt a chapter that is shown to be dangerous. Executive Order 14362 set this chapter-by-chapter process in motion, and four chapters have been designated so far: Lebanon, Egypt, Jordan and Sudan ([[12. Current Administration#Muslim Brotherhood chapters designated|Muslim Brotherhood chapters designated]]). I would only designate the dangerous chapters, while watching the rest; those chapters, like the Islamist terror groups they back, are among the enemies met with proactive eradication ([[4. Religion & Secularism#Islam and Extremism|Islam and Extremism]]).

> [!question]- "Aren't some Brotherhood chapters peaceful political parties?"
> Defenders point out that some chapters run as legal political parties and say they have renounced violence. Jordan's Islamic Action Front won 31 of 138 seats in the 2024 parliamentary election. Its secretary general calls it an independent party with "no relationship with any other organisational body" and says it is "committed to order, the law, and the provisions of the constitution," and the Jordanian Brotherhood says it is "committed to its peaceful approach." A party front does not shield a chapter with a record like Jordan's. In April 2025 Jordan's intelligence service arrested 16 people over plots involving explosives, automatic weapons, rocket manufacture, a rocket hidden ready to launch, and a drone project. A week later Jordan outlawed the Brotherhood, closed its offices, seized its assets and raided the Islamic Action Front's headquarters, and the interior minister said members had tried to remove and destroy documents from the Brotherhood's headquarters. In January 2026 the Treasury found that Jordanian Brotherhood members, working with entities abroad, had manufactured rockets, explosives and drones, run recruitment operations and raised money illegally, and designated the chapter for material support to a designated terrorist group. Jordan's chapter had a party that won seats and a peaceful public line while its members were building rockets, and that is exactly why the chapters not yet designated stay under close watch: designation follows as soon as the evidence does. Other chapters keep armed wings of their own. The Lebanese chapter reactivated its armed wing, the al-Fajr Forces, after October 7, 2023 and fired rockets into northern Israel in coordination with Hezbollah. The Sudanese chapter runs an armed wing, the al-Baraa Bin Malik Brigade, whose fighters have carried out mass executions of civilians and summarily executed people based on race, ethnicity or perceived affiliation with opposition groups. The governments closest to the movement treat it as a terror threat: Egypt declared it a terrorist group in December 2013, Saudi Arabia in March 2014, and the UAE listed "the Muslim Brotherhood (MB) organisation and groups" as terrorist in November 2014.

#### Key Elements
- Every Brotherhood chapter shown to support Islamist terrorism, or to organize violence, designated as a terrorist organization.
- The remaining chapters kept under close watch and designated as soon as evidence shows they cross that line.
- A legal party or civic front does not shield a chapter shown to be dangerous.
- The movement judged by its record: the terrorist groups that grew out of it, from Egyptian Islamic Jihad and Gama'a al-Islamiyya to HASM, Palestinian Islamic Jihad and Hamas.

#### Rationale
The Brotherhood's record is wider than any one group. The 9/11 Commission found that Osama bin Laden "relies heavily" on the Egyptian writer Sayyid Qutb, "a member of the Muslim Brotherhood" executed in 1966, who taught that all Muslims as he defined them must take up arms and that any Muslim who rejects his ideas is "just one more nonbeliever worthy of destruction." The State Department records that the Egyptian Brotherhood produced Egyptian Islamic Jihad, which assassinated President Anwar Sadat in 1981 and, led by Ayman al-Zawahiri, merged with al-Qa'ida in 2001; Gama'a al-Islamiyya, whose spiritual leader served a life sentence for his role in the 1993 World Trade Center bombing; HASM, whose 2019 car bomb outside a Cairo hospital killed at least 20 people; and Liwa al-Thawra, which assassinated the commander of the Egyptian army's Ninth Armored Division in 2016 and bombed a police training center in Tanta in 2017. Palestinian Islamic Jihad and Hamas also emerged from the Brotherhood; Hamas's 1988 charter states that it "is one of the wings of Moslem Brotherhood in Palestine." The United States has designated every one of these groups as terrorist.

The record of each chapter is what decides its designation, and the designated chapters have such records. The Lebanese chapter's al-Fajr Forces fired rockets into Israel in coordination with Hezbollah after October 7, 2023; in July 2025 the Lebanese Army dismantled a covert military training camp that included its militants; and its secretary general has pushed for a more formal alignment with Hezbollah's axis. The Sudanese chapter has contributed upwards of 20,000 fighters to Sudan's war, many trained and supported by Iran's Revolutionary Guard. Executive Order 14362 states that a senior leader of the Egyptian chapter called on October 7 for violent attacks on U.S. partners and interests, and the Treasury found that in mid-2025 the Egyptian chapter took funding from a terrorist group's military wing to destabilize the Egyptian government and that, in 2024, would-be fighters needed Brotherhood connections in Egypt to reach Gaza. Jordanian Brotherhood leaders, the order states, have long given material support to Hamas's militant wing. In the United States, a federal jury in Dallas in 2008 convicted the Holy Land Foundation and five of its leaders of funneling about \$12.4 million to a designated terrorist organization; the government's evidence showed that the foundation was the chief fundraising arm of a committee the Brotherhood set up in the U.S. for that purpose. Each of these findings is tied to a named chapter or network; a chapter with no such record yet is watched, not designated.

## Sanctions and Economic Statecraft

>[!tldr] In short
> Sanctions and trade limits target adversaries like China, Russia, and Iran, and work alongside military deterrence.

#### Position
Economic tools serve national security. Trade is reciprocal, and restrictions apply to adversaries and national-security threats such as China, Russia, and Iran, including limits on outsourcing and technology transfer. Sanctions are used where they weaken an enemy's capacity to threaten the United States, alongside military deterrence rather than in place of it.

> [!question]- "Do sanctions actually work?"
> Not alone. They are one tool that raises an adversary's costs and limits its access to technology and finance, used together with deterrence. Across 204 sanctions episodes studied by the Peterson Institute, sanctions were at least partly successful in 34%, succeeding about half the time for modest policy goals but in about 31% of attempts at regime change or democratization.

#### Key Elements
- Economic tools serve security.
- Reciprocal trade.
- Restrictions on adversaries.
- Sanctions alongside deterrence.

#### Rationale
The International Emergency Economic Powers Act authorizes the President to regulate economic transactions to deal with an unusual and extraordinary foreign threat once a national emergency is declared (50 U.S.C. § 1701). Under the emergency-powers rule in [[3. Authority & Civil Liberties#Emergency Powers|Authority & Civil Liberties]], such declarations lapse unless the legislature renews them.

## Global Institutions

>[!tldr] In short
> The U.S. joins global institutions only when they help it and never submits to international courts or law over its own.

#### Position
Participation in global institutions follows the position on alliances: the United States contributes only to those that benefit it and serve its best interests. It may contribute, but it is not held to any standard, tribunal, or global court for its sovereign actions and does not capitulate to any international law. The United States follows its own law and what is best for its people and shared values, and no globalist bureaucrats or courts dictate its conduct. Treaties the United States has ratified remain binding as U.S. law until it exits them through its own constitutional procedure. The United States stays in the UN and other bodies where participation benefits it. The rejection of foreign tribunals applies to every nation's sovereignty, not only America's. I believe the United States does not recognize or cooperate with the International Criminal Court. Sanctions on its officials are kept for concrete acts against Americans or allied personnel, such as warrants or arrests, not for the court's existence or general investigations.

> [!question]- "Does rejecting international law isolate the United States?"
> No. The United States can cooperate with any institution that serves its interests. What it rejects is surrendering authority over its own actions to foreign bodies that answer to no American voter.

> [!question]- "Doesn't this let the U.S. ignore treaties it signed?"
> No. A treaty the United States has ratified is U.S. law and is followed until the country exits it through its own procedure. What is rejected is a foreign court or body claiming authority over U.S. actions.

Some ratified treaties conflict with my positions, and the United States ought to exit them before those positions take effect. The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, ratified by the United States in 1992, says in article 7 that "no one shall be subjected without his free consent to medical or scientific experimentation," and article 4(2) allows no derogation from it. The U.S. reservation to article 7 limits only "cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment" to what the Fifth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments forbid; it does not reach the experimentation clause. The U.S. reservation to article 6 keeps capital punishment for anyone "other than a pregnant woman" under "existing or future laws," but the Human Rights Committee reads article 6 to allow death only for crimes "involving intentional killing" and to bar new capital offenses after ratification. So the United States ought to withdraw from the Covenant through its own constitutional procedure before the experimentation track and the new capital offenses take effect. While the Covenant has no withdrawal clause and the Committee holds that no state may leave it, the United States still ought to leave, because no nation is bound forever to a foreign committee's reading of a treaty it chose to join. The Convention Against Torture can be denounced under its article 31, effective one year after notice, and the United States ought to denounce it too if its definition of torture is read to reach the experimentation track. Leaving the Convention does not lift the bar on removing anyone to torture, which the law ought to hold in statute. ^treaty-exit

#### Key Elements
- Participate only in institutions that benefit the U.S.
- Never submit to international tribunals or global courts.
- U.S. law and national interest above international law and global bureaucracy.
- Ratified treaties binding as U.S. law until exited through U.S. procedure.
- Withdraw from the ICCPR before the experimentation track and new capital offenses take effect; denounce the Convention Against Torture under its article 31 if its torture definition is read to reach the experimentation track.
- Rejection of foreign tribunals applies to every nation's sovereignty.
- No recognition of or cooperation with the ICC; sanctions on its officials only for concrete acts against Americans or allied personnel.

#### Rationale
Sovereignty means accountability to one's own citizens. The United States is not a party to the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court. Institutions such as the UN operate under a charter that lets members participate without surrendering domestic authority. The American Service-Members' Protection Act of 2002 (22 U.S.C. 7421 et seq.) bars U.S. cooperation with the International Criminal Court and authorizes the President to use "all means necessary and appropriate" to free U.S. and allied personnel it detains.

## World Health Organization

>[!tldr] In short
> The United States is not bound by the WHO. It cooperates on specific health work, such as surveillance data sharing, where Americans clearly benefit, with voluntary funding tied to results.

#### Position
The United States is not a member bound by the World Health Organization's rules, regulations, or pandemic agreements, consistent with [[#Global Institutions]]. It may cooperate with the WHO and other health bodies case by case through specific agreements, such as disease-surveillance data sharing, where the benefit to Americans is clear. Any funding is voluntary, targeted to named programs, and tied to published results. Domestic defense against pandemics, natural or engineered, is the government's own duty ([[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Pandemics and Biosecurity|Pandemics and Biosecurity]]).

> [!question]- "Doesn't leaving the WHO weaken the world's early warning against pandemics, including for Americans?"
> Supporters of membership point to disease surveillance: disease does not stop at borders, and the WHO coordinates reporting and response among nearly every country. Cooperation does not require membership. The United States ought to keep that benefit by cooperating on surveillance and data sharing through specific agreements, without being bound to the organization's rules. Membership has not guaranteed good performance. The Independent Panel for Pandemic Preparedness and Response found that the COVID-19 outbreak likely met the criteria for a global emergency at the WHO committee's first meeting on January 22, 2020, but the declaration came only on January 30.

> [!question]- "How much does the United States pay?"
> More than any other country. The United States contributed \$1.284 billion in the 2022–2023 biennium, 15.6% of the WHO's revenue. Most of it was voluntary and earmarked; the assessed contribution has been set at the 22% maximum share of member assessments.

#### Key Elements
- Not bound by WHO rules, regulations, or pandemic agreements.
- Case-by-case cooperation through specific agreements, such as surveillance data sharing.
- Funding voluntary, targeted to named programs, and tied to published results.
- Domestic pandemic defense remains a core government duty.

#### Rationale
The United States ought to take part in global institutions only where they serve the national interest, and never submit to their authority. Health cooperation serves that interest when it is specific and measured; open-ended membership and funding do not guarantee results, as the 2020 delay showed.

## Foreign Aid

>[!tldr] In short
> Foreign aid must serve America's own security and interests. Aid that is purely charitable is paid for only by people who choose to give, not by required taxes.

#### Position
Foreign spending must advance the security and long-term interests of the United States. Assistance that serves that purpose, such as support for allies against shared threats or efforts that counter adversaries, is judged under the same national-interest test as alliances and institutions. Charitable aid that serves no American interest is redistribution abroad and is funded only through taxpayer opt-in or private giving.

> [!question]- "Won't cutting aid cede influence to China and other rivals?"
> Aid that counters adversaries or secures American interests passes the national-interest test and can continue. What does not pass is charity carried out with compelled tax money. Americans who want to fund humanitarian work abroad remain free to do so, through opt-in programs or private giving.

> [!question]- "Doesn't cutting aid cost lives?"
> It can. The State Department credits PEPFAR, the U.S. HIV/AIDS program, with saving 26 million lives since 2003. I do not deny that. It rests on principle: compelled taxes do not fund charity abroad any more than at home. Programs like PEPFAR continue where they pass the national-interest test, and otherwise through opt-in funding and private giving, which remain open to everyone who wants to keep them going.

#### Key Elements
- Foreign spending must serve U.S. security and long-term interests.
- Interest-serving assistance is judged under the alliance and institution test.
- Purely charitable foreign aid is funded only by opt-in or private giving.

#### Rationale
The state's primary duty is to its own citizens, as set out in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework|Ethical and Philosophical Framework]], and I reject mandatory redistribution at home. Applying the same rule abroad keeps policy consistent. Foreign aid accounts for about 1% or less of the federal budget, and U.S. global health funding was \$12.4 billion in FY2024. The amounts are small relative to the budget, so the question is one of principle: whether compelled taxes should fund charity abroad. The opt-in model is defined in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance#Welfare and Entitlements|Domestic Policy & Governance]].

## Military Spending and Projection

>[!tldr] In short
> Current high military spending is fine and can rise if needed. Homeland defense comes first, but global reach and swift strikes are part of protecting it.

#### Position
Current military spending is very high and is acceptable. If more firepower is needed or a visible shortfall appears in campaigns, it is raised, though the empirics of military spending are beyond what is asserted here. Homeland defense always comes first, but global power is often bound up with homeland protection. Offensive deterrence and swift strikes go hand in hand with protecting the country. Spending stays within the balanced-budget and spending-restraint goals in [[5. Domestic Policy & Governance|Domestic Policy & Governance]].

> [!question]- "Why maintain global power projection if homeland defense comes first?"

#### Key Elements
- Current high spending acceptable.
- Increase if capability gaps appear.
- Homeland defense first.
- Global power projection and offensive deterrence as extensions of homeland defense.

#### Rationale
The United States has the largest military budget in the world, and that capacity underwrites deterrence. Projection and rapid strike capability prevent distant threats from becoming close ones.

## Military Space

>[!tldr] In short
> The Outer Space Treaty stays as the civil baseline but is read to allow defense, with clauses that block missile defense renegotiated or exited. Defensive space weapons are allowed; first-strike weapons are disfavored. Civil spaceflight stays private, the only funded government space program is military, and business subsidies are opt-in only.

#### Position
The Outer Space Treaty is kept as the baseline for peaceful civil use, interpreted to allow defensive systems, with renegotiation or exit from clauses that block missile defense or hostile-denial. Defensive space weapons, such as missile defense and defensive anti-satellite systems, are allowed; offensive first-strike space weapons are disfavored. Human spaceflight stays fully privatized, the only funded government space program is military under the Space Force and the Department of Defense, which may contract private firms, and there are no business subsidies except taxpayer opt-in.

> [!question]- "Doesn't the Outer Space Treaty ban weapons in space?"
> It bans nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction in orbit and military bases on celestial bodies. It is kept as the civil baseline and interpreted to allow defensive systems; clauses that block missile defense are renegotiated or exited.

#### Key Elements
- Outer Space Treaty as baseline for peaceful civil use.
- Interpreted to allow defensive systems.
- Renegotiate or exit clauses that block missile defense or hostile-denial.
- Defensive space weapons allowed; offensive first-strike disfavored.
- Civil spaceflight private; the only funded government space program is military, under Space Force / DoD.
- No business subsidies except taxpayer opt-in.

#### Rationale
This follows my view that military power serves the defense of the country ([[#Military Spending and Projection|Military Spending and Projection]]), opt-in subsidies, and human judgment over force, as in [[#Cyber Warfare and Autonomous Weapons|Cyber Warfare and Autonomous Weapons]]. Article IV of the Outer Space Treaty bars placing nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction in orbit and bars military bases and weapons testing on celestial bodies. Article XVI allows any party to withdraw on one year's written notice. Privatized civil spaceflight is set out in [[10. Bioethics, Progress, & Technology#Space and Exploration|Space and Exploration]].

## Laws of War

>[!tldr] In short
> Civilians are absolutely protected, except foreign actors who have forfeited protection as set out in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sovereignty and Borders|Sovereignty and Borders]] ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#^hostile-forfeit|conduct test]]). Prisoners of war keep their Geneva rights, except where those conflict with the compelled-labor rule, and are held in a separate military setup. But those a tribunal finds took part in hostilities can be put to compelled labor like domestic prisoners.

#### Position
Civilians are absolutely protected and get full rights protections, except foreign actors who have forfeited protection as set out in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sovereignty and Borders|Sovereignty and Borders]] ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#^hostile-forfeit|conduct test]]). Prisoners of war get basic protections and treatment similar to how the United States treats its own prisoners, including labor, but foreign captives are held in a separate military setup. Labor is the preferred placement for them. Geneva compliance is refused only where the Convention conflicts with my compelled-labor rule: a prisoner whom a military tribunal finds, under due process, took part in hostilities against the United States has crossed the forfeiture threshold in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#General Rules of Forfeiture|Section 1]] and may be assigned compelled labor on the same terms as domestic prisoners, beyond the Convention's limits on the kinds of work and its pay requirements. A rights violator past that line loses the corresponding protections. A prisoner who has not crossed it keeps every protection the Convention gives.

> [!question]- "Doesn't departing from Geneva on labor invite enemies to mistreat captured Americans?"
> Civilians remain absolutely protected, except foreign actors who have forfeited protection by hostile conduct ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#^hostile-forfeit|Hostile conduct, removal, and entry]]), and prisoners of war receive basic protections and treatment similar to how the United States treats its own prisoners. The only departure is compelled labor for prisoners a military tribunal finds took part in hostilities; every other prisoner keeps every protection the Convention gives. The standard is the same one applied at home, not a lower one invented for foreigners. Reciprocity is a practical consideration, but the United States ought not to let a treaty set the terms; U.S. law and the rights framework do.

> [!question]- "If civilians are absolutely protected and beliefs are never grounds for removal, how can hostile West Bank residents be forced out, and their population barred from entry? Isn't that ethnic cleansing?"
> The two rules govern different people. Inside the United States, opinions, beliefs, and speech are never grounds for removal ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#Removal of Non-Citizen Residents and Loss of Citizenship|Removal of Non-Citizen Residents]]). That rule protects people the government governs. Abroad, a civilian keeps full protection unless he forfeits it by his own conduct against the threatened nation ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sovereignty and Borders|Sovereignty and Borders]]). Hostility means conduct, not identity: membership in, or material support for, an armed group that targets civilians; public calls for genocide or ethnic cleansing; or taking part in or organizing violence against civilians. Ethnicity, religion, ordinary speech, and private opinion alone are never hostility.
>
> **Why the hostile are forced out.** A person found hostile has forfeited his protection against the nation he threatens, and that nation has the right and the duty to defend its people from him. Removal is the remedy that fits the threat. He has shown, by his own conduct, that he will act to harm the nation's civilians. Letting him stay keeps an active threat among the people it targets, and indefinite detention would be a costly, permanent security state, the very thing separation is meant to end. Removal ends the threat at its source without taking his life. Once a court or tribunal finds him hostile, he is forced out. A later offer to assimilate does not reopen the case, because a promise from someone already proven hostile is not a security guarantee. The nation's security and its duty to its citizens override the negative rights of a foreign security threat, so forcing him out wrongs no one.
>
> **Why entry is barred to the whole population.** No foreigner has a right to enter another nation; entry is a privilege the nation grants on its own terms ([[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#Sovereignty and Borders|Sovereignty and Borders]]). The United States, and any nation such a population threatens, ought to keep a ban list and impose a complete entry ban, by nationality or place of origin, on two kinds of place. The first is any place whose regime or dominant factions wage or celebrate attacks that target civilians for genocide or ethnic cleansing. The second is any place where reliable polling shows 5% or more of the population openly supporting such attacks. When the people who hold power or set the norm openly back genocidal attacks, screening cannot reliably sort the hostile from the rest, because hostility is common, rewarded, and easy to conceal, and one wrong admission can cost lives. The line sits at 5% because even one person in twenty openly backing the slaughter of civilians is a risk no nation's citizens should be made to carry; waiting for a quarter or a majority would mean acting only after the hostility is overwhelming. The West Bank and Gaza qualify on both counts: Hamas, a dominant faction, waged October 7 and celebrates it, and support for the attack runs far above 5% ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#^pcpsr-oct7|PCPSR polling]]).
>
> The list is reviewed every year. A place comes off only when both conditions are met: reliable polling shows open support below 1% in consecutive annual reviews, and no governing authority or dominant faction there still endorses such attacks. The lift point sits far below the trigger so that a small or passing dip does not reopen entry; support must fall to a level where hostility is rare enough that individual screening can reliably catch it again, and the people in power must have stopped endorsing it. The ban is tied to measured support and to what the authorities there do, not to ethnicity or religion, and it applies to any place that crosses the same line.
>
> **Why none of this is ethnic cleansing.** Ethnic cleansing drives people from their homes because of who they are. The entry ban removes no one from anywhere; it declines to admit people the nation has no duty to admit, on a measured security risk that applies equally to any place that crosses the same line. Forced exit applies only to individuals a court or military tribunal finds hostile by clear and convincing evidence, the removal standard, not the capital one. Residents who are not found hostile and choose to assimilate stay, whatever their ethnicity or faith. October 7 targeted people for being Jewish; this targets no one for being Palestinian. ^hostile-forfeit

#### Key Elements
- Civilians absolutely protected, with full rights protections, except foreign actors who forfeit protection by hostile conduct ([[8. Foreign Policy & International Relations#^hostile-forfeit|conduct test]]).
- Prisoners of war get basic protections.
- Geneva compliance refused only where it conflicts with the compelled-labor rule, for prisoners a military tribunal finds took part in hostilities, under the Section 1 forfeiture threshold.
- Prisoners of war who have not crossed the forfeiture threshold keep full Convention protections.
- Labor is the preferred placement for prisoners of war.
- Treatment similar to U.S. treatment of its own prisoners, including labor.
- Foreign captives held in a separate military setup.

#### Rationale
The Third Geneva Convention of 1949, to which the United States is a party, states that "prisoners of war must at all times be humanely treated" (Article 13), that they "shall not be interned in penitentiaries" except in their own interest (Article 22), and that the detaining power "may utilize the labour of prisoners of war who are physically fit" (Article 49). I depart from the Convention only where its labor limits conflict with the compelled-labor rule, and only for prisoners past the forfeiture threshold. Labor by prisoners of war rests on two grounds. Their detention as captured combatants permits labor, as the Convention itself does for physically fit prisoners (Article 49). Labor is assigned after a military tribunal finds, under due process, that the prisoner took part in hostilities against the United States, consistent with the forfeiture rule in [[1. Ethical and Philosophical Framework#General Rules of Forfeiture|Section 1]]. Compelled labor for domestic prisoners is set out in [[6. Criminal Justice & Security#Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor|Criminal Justice & Security]].

## Nuclear Weapons and WMD

>[!tldr] In short
> Nuclear weapons cannot be uninvented, so deterrence is the main safeguard. Enemies are stopped from getting them by military force when it can be done safely.

#### Position
Nuclear proliferation is unfortunate but can never be reversed, since progress will always be made with all technologies. Mutual deterrence is the only way to prevent catastrophe. Enemies are militarily prevented from advancing nuclear capability when the United States can do so safely.

> [!question]- "Isn't preventive military action against proliferation destabilizing?"
> Action is taken only when it can be done safely. Allowing hostile regimes to acquire nuclear weapons is far more destabilizing, because deterrence depends on rational actors and some enemies are openly committed to destroying the West.

#### Key Elements
- Proliferation cannot be reversed.
- Mutual deterrence as the safeguard against catastrophe.
- Military prevention of enemy proliferation when safely possible.
- Strong U.S. nuclear posture.

#### Rationale
Nine states possess nuclear weapons, and the knowledge cannot be removed from the world. Deterrence has prevented nuclear war among major powers since 1945. Preventing hostile regimes such as Iran from acquiring weapons keeps deterrence workable.

> [!info]- What is mutual deterrence?
> Mutual deterrence is the condition in which each side can inflict unacceptable retaliation on the other, so that neither has an incentive to strike first.

## North Korea and Nuclear Rogue States

>[!tldr] In short
> North Korea's arsenal is deterred and contained. Preventive action is taken only when it can be done safely.

#### Position
Deterrence is the main safeguard against states that already hold nuclear weapons, such as North Korea. Their arsenals cannot be uninvented, so the United States maintains a credible deterrent and alliances that contain them. Preventive military action against a nuclear program is taken when it can be done safely; where a state already holds deliverable weapons, deterrence and containment govern.

> [!question]- "Why not strike North Korea's arsenal?"
> Preventive action applies when it can be done safely. Against an established arsenal, a strike risks nuclear retaliation, so deterrence is the safer course.

#### Key Elements
- Deterrence as the main safeguard.
- Containment through alliances.
- Preventive action only when safe.
- No occupation or nation-building.

#### Rationale
SIPRI estimates that North Korea holds about 50 nuclear warheads and enough fissile material for up to 90. The preventive-action rule is set out in [[#Nuclear Weapons and WMD]].
