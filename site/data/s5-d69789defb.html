Core Principles

I stand for limited government, individual responsibility, low taxation, and maximum cultural and economic freedom, with only minimal safety nets. The state ought to prioritize free markets, personal choice, and fiscal restraint. Opt-in social programs and basic public services are acceptable to me. Mandatory redistribution is not, beyond the narrow exceptions named in Welfare and Entitlements. Minimal necessary taxes should fund core services: K-12 education, police, fire, emergency services, infrastructure upkeep, and the bare-minimum safety floor defined in Welfare and Entitlements. K-12 is funded by each state from its general revenue at about $10,000 per child, as set out in Education.

Economic System

tldrIn short

I believe markets work better than the alternatives for making people richer over time. Countries and periods that protected private property and let prices and competition guide most decisions produced higher living standards, less extreme poverty, and more innovation than systems that put the state in charge of most production and prices. The big historical examples (Soviet bloc vs. the West, North vs. South Korea, Venezuela’s collapse, China’s growth after it allowed more markets) all point in the same direction.

Government still has a limited, important job. It should handle defense, basic law and order, courts, and core infrastructure, and keep a few clear rules against force, fraud, and serious safety dangers. Beyond that, when government tries to heavily manage the economy or pick winners with subsidies, the results are usually wasteful and favor the politically connected.

Corporate subsidies are a bad deal. Taxpayers currently spend large amounts supporting specific companies. The track record shows these programs rarely deliver the promised broad benefits and often just reward lobbying. I would end them.

No minimum wage. The research on job losses is genuinely mixed, but my position does not rest on it: a minimum wage dictates the terms of a voluntary contract, so I would abolish it. Low pay should be topped up through the tax return, and hardship handled through the safety net.

Owner income. Royalties and lease payments the nation earns as an owner of federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum should not be paid out as a citizen dividend. Spread across every adult they would come to only tens of dollars a year. I would send them first to the earnings-only top-up for low earners, with any remainder paying down the debt.

Bottom line: Markets should do most of the heavy lifting - they have the best record at creating widespread prosperity. Government ought to stay focused on the basics and on stopping clear harm. I'd avoid special favors for corporations and set no wage floor, topping up low pay through the tax return instead.

Position

Market-based systems grounded in secure property rights and price signals have produced the largest sustained gains in material living standards, poverty reduction, and innovation in recorded history. Comparative evidence favors these systems over socialist or heavily state-directed alternatives. Historical cases of sustained socialist organization produced chronic shortages, lower productivity, and eventual stagnation or collapse relative to more market-oriented peers. Hybrid systems that preserved significant private ownership and price signals performed better than those that suppressed them.

The state’s economic role should remain limited to core public goods - national defense, domestic public safety, courts, basic infrastructure, a bare-minimum safety net, and basic K-12 funding averaging about $10,000 per child, paid by each state from its general revenue - plus a narrow set of evidence-based rules against force, fraud, and severe safety failures. I reject broad economic management, industrial planning, and taxpayer-funded corporate subsidies. There should be no minimum wage. Wages ought to be set by voluntary agreement, and hardship is covered by the bare-minimum safety net (Labor and Unions). Income the nation earns as an owner, such as royalties and lease payments on federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum, should go first to funding the earnings-only top-up (Welfare and Entitlements), and any remainder automatically to reducing the national debt (Fiscal Policy), with no annual legislative division. It should not be paid out as a citizen dividend.

question"Why is there no citizen dividend?"

Owner income is too small to matter as a payment: spread across every adult, it comes to tens of dollars a year. Put first toward the earnings-only top-up, with any remainder going to debt reduction, the same money supports work and lowers the burden on future taxpayers. Because it is income the nation earns as an owner, using it this way takes nothing from any citizen through taxation.

question"Don't markets leave some people behind?"

Markets do not guarantee equal outcomes, and I do not claim they do. A minimal floor for basic necessities should be funded by minimal necessary taxes, and anyone who wants to fund more can opt in to do so. What I reject is mandatory redistribution beyond that floor and state direction of the economy, because the historical record shows those approaches produce shortages and stagnation that leave far more people behind. As markets spread after 1990, the share of the world living in extreme poverty (under $3.00 a day) fell from 43.4% in 1990 to 10.1% in 2024, according to the World Bank. Hunger has fallen too but has not ended: the UN estimated that 8.2% of the world, about 673 million people, faced hunger in 2024, with the share above 20% in Africa. In 2023, the poorest 10% in the most economically free quarter of countries had incomes 7.8 times higher than the poorest 10% in the least free quarter. Extreme poverty affected 2% of people in the freest quarter compared with 52% in the least free. These Fraser Institute figures are cross-country correlations, not measured effects of economic freedom.

Rationale

Comparative historical evidence shows that societies with greater economic freedom and stronger market institutions have achieved higher long-run growth, faster poverty reduction, and greater consumer abundance than those that suppressed private property and price signals. Targeted safety and anti-fraud rules address real market failures, but regulatory scope expands easily and is prone to capture; therefore the burden of proof remains on further intervention. Corporate subsidies distort capital allocation and reward political connection more reliably than economic value.

Federal owner income is real but small. In FY2024 the Office of Natural Resources Revenue disbursed $16.45 billion from energy and mineral production on federal and Tribal lands and federal offshore areas, of which $6.32 billion went to the U.S. Treasury and $1.18 billion to Tribes and individual Indian mineral owners, whose property it is. Excluding the Tribal share and spread across about 267 million adults, federal owner income comes to roughly $24 to $57 per adult per year: too small to matter as a payment, but useful toward the top-up and the debt. The top-up costs about $26 billion a year on my estimate (Welfare and Entitlements), far more than the $6.32 billion the Treasury received from federal energy and mineral revenue in FY2024. So at current levels all owner income goes to the top-up, general revenue covers the rest, and debt reduction receives owner income only once it exceeds the top-up's cost.

Defenders of the minimum wage cite well-known studies, notably Card & Krueger, that find little or no employment loss from moderate increases. Other research and several meta-analyses find negative employment effects, particularly for teenagers and the least skilled, with the size of the effect varying by the magnitude of the increase and local labor-market conditions. The weight of evidence does not support large or rapid increases as costless, and the government ought to abolish the minimum wage on rights grounds, not on the employment evidence alone (Labor and Unions).

Socialism

tldrIn short

Socialism in its core sense, social or state ownership of the means of production and planning in place of markets, is antithetical to sentientism and negative rights, and a terrible idea: it takes property, forces redistribution, compels labor and speech, and suppresses dissent. Social democracy is different: the Nordic countries are market economies with large welfare states. I share the goal of a floor for basic needs but fund only a minimal one and leave the rest to markets and voluntary giving.

Position

The word "socialism" covers very different systems, and I judge each by what it does to individual rights. In its core sense — social or state ownership of the means of production and planning in place of markets (Stanford Encyclopedia: "the bulk of the means of production is under social, democratic control") — three features are incompatible with my framework: (1) collective or state ownership of productive property imposed by force, including expropriation; (2) central planning that replaces prices and voluntary exchange; (3) compulsory redistribution beyond a minimal floor for basic needs. Each takes property or overrides consent from people who have harmed no one.

Social democracy keeps private ownership and markets and funds a large welfare state through taxes. The Nordic countries are in this category (Denmark's PM, 2015: "Denmark is far from a socialist planned economy. Denmark is a market economy."). Social democracy shares only the third feature, so I judge it as too large a compulsory state, not as socialism in the core sense (Nordic Social Democracy).

Socialism in the core sense is antithetical to sentientism and negative rights: it seizes property, forces redistribution, compels labor and speech, and suppresses dissent. Cases, calculation-problem argument, and country comparisons live in the Q&As, Rationale, and Comparisons — not repeated here. Shared ground I keep: a bare-minimum safety floor, voluntary cooperatives, and voluntary giving. Flat tax, named minimal welfare/top-up floor, and the time-limited Sanctuary Levy are not excluded socialism (Socialism (excluded)).

My view: The right of citizens to simply be left alone is incomprehensible to socialists who are convinced they have a mandate to rearrange other people's lives.

question"Isn't socialism just about fairness and helping people who are struggling?"

The government ought to fund a bare-minimum safety floor for basic needs through minimal necessary taxes, and anyone may give more voluntarily (Welfare and Entitlements). The disagreement is about means. Taking ownership of productive property, or compelling redistribution beyond that floor, uses force against people who violated no one's rights. The record of planned economies also suggests it leaves the poor worse off, not better.

question"The Nordic countries are successful and socialist. Doesn't that disprove the critique?"

No, because they are not socialist in the core sense. Their own leaders describe them as market economies, and the Heritage Foundation's 2025 Index of Economic Freedom ranks Denmark 7th, Norway 9th, Sweden 12th, and Finland 13th in the world. What they add is high taxes and a large welfare state. I reject that compulsory scale too, but it's a different argument from the one against planning and state ownership. Their success tends to support markets with private property, which they kept.

question"Venezuela failed because of oil prices and U.S. sanctions, not socialism."

They were shocks, not the cause. Oil prices fell sharply in 2014, and ECLAC links part of the 2020 drop to tighter U.S. sanctions on the public sector. But the IMF found that "economic mismanagement accelerated and amplified the economic collapse," and price controls were producing shortages of staples in 2007, during the oil boom. The IMF calls the result the largest collapse for a country not at war in almost half a century. The policies made the economy fragile before the shocks arrived.

question"Isn't Cuba's poverty caused by the U.S. embargo?"

The embargo raises costs, but it is not the cause of Cuba's poverty. It has blocked most U.S. trade and investment since the early 1960s. Its extraterritorial provisions, such as the 1996 Helms-Burton Act, also raise costs for foreign firms and banks that deal with Cuba. But the embargo does not stop Cuba from trading with the rest of the world. The European Union describes itself as Cuba's main trade partner and largest foreign investor, and in the 1990s Spain, Canada, and China were among its leading partners. U.S. law has also allowed agricultural exports to Cuba since 2000. Through 1990 the Soviet Union provided more than $65 billion in aid and price subsidies, and the economy still shrank by roughly a third when that aid ended. Analysts of the 1990s crisis named the "inefficiencies of centrally planned economies" as its underlying cause. The embargo makes a weak economy worse. It does not explain why the economy is weak.

question"Doesn't Cuba have excellent health care and literacy?"

Cuba reports high literacy, long life expectancy, and low infant mortality. These figures come from a government that controls statistics and the press, so researchers treat some of them with caution. A 2017 study in Demography found patterns in Cuba's records of fetal and newborn deaths consistent with misclassification. It estimated Cuba's infant mortality at about 9.0 to 10.1 per 1,000 live births, against the officially reported 5.8. Even taking the official figures at face value, I do not accept good services as justification for taking property and forbidding exit.

question"Couldn't modern computers solve the planning problem?"

The argument does not depend on computing power. Hayek's point was that the knowledge an economy runs on is "dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess," much of it about local conditions and changing preferences that are never written down. Mises argued that without market prices for capital goods, planners cannot compare the costs of different uses of resources. Prices gather that information because people trade with their own resources. A computer can process data faster, but it cannot produce the prices that come from voluntary exchange.

question"Is market socialism, with worker cooperatives competing in markets, also rejected?"

Not if it is voluntary. Workers may form cooperatives, own firms jointly, and compete in markets under my freedom of association. What I reject is making that model compulsory, by forbidding private ownership or seizing firms to convert them.

question"Don't a tiny few seize the wealth that workers produce?"

Some large fortunes are inherited, and some are helped by political favors. That is why I reject taxpayer-funded corporate subsidies and would tax an heir's inherited gains when the assets are sold (carryover basis). But most of the largest American fortunes were built, not seized: Forbes classed 71% of its 2026 list of the 400 richest Americans as self-made, counting someone as self-made when they built most of the fortune themselves, even from a comfortable start. Those fortunes came from selling something millions of people chose to buy. A wage is the price a worker and an employer both accept. When the state sets a higher price than an employer can pay, the job does not happen (Labor and Unions). A fair share is what free people agree to, not what a planner decides. question"Can the rise in hospital, college, and housing costs be directly associated with regulation, progressive tax policy, and the states involved?"

I believe it can be strongly associated with regulation and other government intervention, and with state policy especially in housing. I would not pin it directly on progressive tax rates.

Mark Perry's "Chart of the Century" plots BLS consumer prices from January 2000 to December 2023. Hospital services rose 243.7%, college tuition and fees 184.7%, and housing 96.2%, against overall inflation of 82.4%. Televisions fell 97.9%, toys 73.5%, and computer software 72.4%. That is an earlier cut of the same BLS pattern I already calculate through September 2026 above.

The association with government is not just a coincidence of labels. In healthcare, third-party payment (insurers and public programs) means the patient often never sees the price, so nobody in the transaction has a strong reason to hold it down (Healthcare). In college, federal lending and subsidies raise demand without freeing supply, and schools exposed to higher federal loan limits raised tuition (data in Higher Education). In housing, rules that restrict building and entry keep supply tight (Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness). The pattern matches the degree of government involvement: prices rose where the state pays, subsidizes, or regulates most heavily, and fell where firms compete for customers.

Progressive tax policy is a weaker and more indirect link. A progressive income tax does not set hospital charges, tuition, or rents. It can affect where people live, how much they invest, and what they have left after tax, but that is not the same as directly causing those sector prices to rise.

State differences are clearest in housing. Stricter zoning and other supply limits, which are common in more progressive coastal areas, keep housing scarce and expensive. Places that allow more building see more moderate prices. Healthcare and higher education also vary by state rules and subsidies, but the housing contrast is the sharpest.

This is not the only cause. Labor-intensive services like hospital care, college, and child care can get more expensive even without new regulation, because wages in those fields have to keep up with the rest of the economy even when productivity grows slowly (Baumol's cost disease). Quality changes, technology, and demand also matter. Those caveats do not erase the main pattern: the sectors with the heaviest government involvement are the ones that ran far ahead of inflation.

Rationale

I protect property and voluntary exchange as extensions of the right not to be subjected to force (Political Philosophy). Socialism in the core sense removes both by design. It also faces a problem of knowledge that holds even under good intentions. Mises argued in 1920 that without market prices for the means of production, a planning authority cannot calculate which uses of resources are more or less valuable. Hayek added in 1945 that the relevant knowledge "never exists in concentrated or integrated form," and that prices are how a market economy uses it. The record of planned economies matches this reasoning (Economic System).

Venezuela is the most recent large case. Under Hugo Chávez the state took over the Orinoco oil projects of ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips in 2007. An international arbitration tribunal later found the Conoco takeover unlawful and awarded the company about $8.7 billion. Price controls on about 400 basic products, imposed from 2003, were followed by recurring shortages of milk, meat, and sugar. The IMF estimates that real GDP contracted by more than 75% between 2013 and 2021, which it calls "the single largest economic collapse for a non-conflict country in almost half a century." Hyperinflation began in late 2017, and ECLAC reported annualized inflation still at 1,198% in November 2021. By late 2025, UNHCR and the R4V platform counted nearly 7.9 million Venezuelan refugees and migrants worldwide.

Cuba is the longest-running case in the hemisphere. In 1960 the revolutionary government nationalized the banks, the foreign oil refineries, and the property of U.S. nationals. The U.S. Foreign Claims Settlement Commission later found 5,911 of those claims compensable, worth about $1.9 billion in principal. The state still owns most means of production and employs a majority of the workforce, according to the Congressional Research Service. Basic food has been rationed through the libreta ration book for decades. When Soviet aid ended, official statistics show GDP fell 34.8% from 1989 to 1993. Recent years have brought a second crisis. In 2021 the official consumer price index rose 77%, and economists estimate true inflation at 174% to 700%. In September 2024 the government cut the subsidized daily bread ration from 80 to 60 grams for lack of flour. In October 2024 the national grid collapsed, leaving the whole island without power. U.S. Customs and Border Protection recorded 224,607 encounters with Cuban nationals in fiscal 2022 and 200,287 in fiscal 2023. Cuba's own statistics office reports that the population fell to 9,748,007 at the end of 2024, 307,961 fewer than a year earlier, with net emigration of about 251,000 that year.

Social democrats share one premise with it: no one should lack basic necessities. The overlap ends there. It keeps only a bare-minimum floor funded by minimal necessary taxes, and it differs on scale and on compulsion. Help beyond that floor comes from markets, families, and voluntary giving, not from taking property.

Comparisons

The clearest tests are cases where similar people were divided between systems, or where one country changed systems. Critics note that none is a controlled experiment and that each has confounders, set out below; in every case the more market-oriented system or period outperformed the planned one. The Soviet Union and Mao's China are covered in Communism.

  • East and West Germany. The same nation, divided after 1945, ran a planned economy in the East and a market economy in the West. Before monetary union in 1990, IMF staff put East German labor productivity at 30% to 40% of the West German level. The German government's later account puts it at only about 20% to 25% in 1988. The two states had different occupying powers and foreign support, so not every gap is due to the economic system.
  • North and South Korea. In 2023 the Bank of Korea estimated North Korea's gross national income per person at 1.59 million won, 3.4% of South Korea's 47.25 million won. The estimate is uncertain because North Korea publishes almost no data, and both states relied heavily on foreign patrons. South Korea also grew under military rule before it democratized, so the comparison is about markets, not about democracy.
  • China after 1978. After China began market reforms, starting with returning farmland to individual households, GDP growth averaged over 9% a year. The World Bank credits China with lifting close to 800 million people out of extreme poverty, about three-quarters of the global reduction over that period. China remains a one-party state with a large state sector, so the gains came from introducing markets, not from changing the political system.
  • Vietnam after 1986. Before the Đổi Mới reforms, inflation reached 487% in 1986. After the country moved to market-oriented policies, growth averaged about 8% a year in the 1990s. The poverty rate fell from 58% in 1993 to 37% in 1998, and GDP per person rose from under $500 in 1986 to $5,066 in 2025, according to the World Bank. As in China, the ruling party kept its monopoly on power.
  • India after 1991. After a balance-of-payments crisis, India dismantled much of the "license raj" system of industrial licensing. Average tariffs fell from 80% in 1990 to 37% in 1996, and IMF research finds the reforms produced "decidedly more stable and sustainable growth from 1992 on." Rodrik and Subramanian note that India's growth had already accelerated around 1980, before the main liberalization, so 1991 deepened a change already under way rather than starting it.
  • Tanzania under Ujamaa. In 1973 the ruling party made it compulsory for rural Tanzanians to live in planned villages, and scholarly estimates put the number moved at about five million. Many farmers were moved far from their fields and trees. Purchases by the cashew authority fell from 140,000 tons in 1973 to 44,000 tons in 1978–79. By 1985, IMF staff report, agricultural production had been declining since the 1970s and cash-crop exports had fallen by half since 1970. Falling world crop prices and oil shocks contributed. After liberalization began in 1986, growth averaged about 7% a year from 2000.
  • Across countries. Single cases have confounders, so I also look at the cross-country record. Grier and Grier studied 141 countries from 1970 to 2015 and identified 49 sustained jumps in economic freedom. Compared with matched countries that did not reform, average annual growth ran about 2.1 to 2.9 percentage points higher over the next five years and about 1.0 to 1.9 points higher over ten. On their illustration, that leaves a reforming country roughly 16% richer after a decade. Critics note that reforms are not randomly assigned, and that the result is an average, not a guarantee for every country. The authors match on investment, institutions, human capital and other covariates, and the effect holds across their different thresholds and estimators.

I see the same pattern inside the United States. Prices have risen fastest where government pays, subsidizes or regulates most heavily, in hospital care, college, child care and rent, and have fallen or stayed far below inflation where firms compete for customers, in televisions, toys, software, phones and clothing (data below).

Labor and Unions

tldrIn short

Unions should be voluntary, and no one should have to join or pay one to work. Licensing ought to exist only to prevent real harm to consumers. I oppose a minimum wage: pay is set by agreement, fraud and forced labor stay crimes, and the safety floor covers hardship.

Position

I support the freedom of workers to form and join unions, and of employers and workers to bargain freely, under freedom of association. No one should be compelled to join or pay a union as a condition of work. Occupational licensing should meet the same burden of proof as any regulation: it is justified only where it prevents real harm to consumers, not to limit competition. I oppose any minimum wage. A minimum wage dictates the terms of a voluntary contract between a worker and an employer. How many jobs are lost is disputed, but it can price people whose labor is worth less than the floor out of work. Neither is consistent with negative rights. Wages ought to be set by agreement, including collective agreements reached by unions that workers join voluntarily. Low pay should be supplemented by the earnings-only top-up and hardship is covered by the bare-minimum safety floor, both set out in Welfare and Entitlements, not by price controls on labor. Fraud, coercion, wage theft, and forced labor should remain crimes. The abolition ought to cover state minimum wages too: rights do not change at state lines, and the top-up uses one national zero-bracket line.

question"Doesn't banning compelled dues weaken unions?"

Unions remain free to organize and bargain. They keep members by delivering value, as any voluntary association does.

question"Without a minimum wage, won't workers be exploited?"

Exploitation through force, fraud, or coercion should stay illegal: wage theft, deceptive contracts, and forced labor are rights violations and ought to be prosecuted. What ends is the state setting the price of a voluntary agreement. Workers keep the right to refuse a job, to quit, and to join a union and bargain collectively. Several of the highest-wage countries in Europe, including Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Austria, and Italy, have no national minimum wage. Wages there are set by collective agreements. Those agreements rest on very strong unions, which the law ought to allow only as voluntary associations, so the outcome in the United States would not simply copy theirs. People who still cannot earn enough are covered by the safety floor.

question"Why abolish the minimum wage instead of keeping a small one?"

A floor of any size is a rule that two consenting adults may not agree on certain terms, and it hits hardest those whose skills are worth the least. The federal minimum already binds very few people: in 2024, 1.0 percent of hourly paid workers earned $7.25 or less. The safety floor, funded by minimal necessary taxes, handles hardship directly rather than through a price control that can price people out of work. How large that job loss is remains disputed in the research, and the abolition does not depend on its size.

question"Isn't the minimum wage just a wage floor, not a ban on work?"

In price terms it is a floor: the law forbids a voluntary wage below a set price. When a worker's productivity is worth less than that floor, the job often does not exist at the legal wage. Employment effects are debated in size. The definition is not. I would abolish the floor because negative rights forbid the state from dictating the terms of a peaceful contract. I would cover hardship through the earnings-only top-up and the safety floor instead (Welfare and Entitlements).

question"Without a minimum wage, how do low earners get by?"

Through pay set by agreement plus the earnings-only top-up built into the flat tax's zero bracket. A low earner's pay should be topped up through the ordinary tax return, and the top-up should shrink gradually as earnings rise, reaching zero at the zero-bracket line. So taking more hours or a raise would always leave the worker better off. The support would come through the tax system rather than from a rule that the employer must pay more, so no one would be priced out of a job (Welfare and Entitlements).

question"Doesn't Acemoglu show that a minimum wage creates more good jobs?"

It is a serious case. In his 2001 model, firms that invest more in a job have to share more of the payoff in wage bargaining, so the market creates too many low-wage jobs. A wage floor can push hiring toward higher-wage jobs and raise average productivity. I grant that a floor can change which jobs get created. But the same model predicts more unemployment, and the paper says only that these rules "may improve welfare." It is a theory paper with no evidence that they do. It also does not answer my objection: a minimum wage overrides a voluntary contract, and wages ought to be set by agreement. Low pay is topped up through the tax return, and hardship is covered by the safety floor (Welfare and Entitlements).

Rationale

Union membership was 9.9% of U.S. wage and salary workers in 2024 (14.3 million), with 32.2% in the public sector and 5.9% in the private sector, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. In Janus v. AFSCME (2018) the Supreme Court held that compelling public employees to pay agency fees violates the First Amendment. Freedom of association is set out in Authority & Civil Liberties.

In 2024, 82,000 U.S. workers earned exactly the federal minimum of $7.25 an hour and 760,000 earned less, together 1.0 percent of hourly paid workers, down from 13.4 percent in 1979. Most were young, and about two-thirds worked in leisure and hospitality, where tips may supplement wages. As of July 2026, Denmark, Italy, Austria, Finland, and Sweden had no national minimum wage, and in those countries, as in Iceland, Norway, and Switzerland, minimums are set by collective agreements for specific sectors. In 2022 the share of low-wage earners was 4.1 percent in Sweden, 6.5 percent in Finland, 8.8 percent in Italy, and 9.7 percent in Denmark, against 14.7 percent across the EU. Portugal, which has a statutory minimum wage, had the lowest share, at 1.8 percent, so the absence of a minimum wage does not by itself explain low-pay rates.

Defenders cite Cengiz, Dube, Lindner, and Zipperer, who studied 138 state minimum-wage increases and found that the overall number of low-wage jobs was essentially unchanged five years later. Neumark and Shirley's review of U.S. studies found the opposite in most of the literature: a clear preponderance of negative employment estimates, strongest for teens, young adults, and the less educated. The abolition does not rest on the employment evidence: I rest it on the right of workers and employers to set their own terms.

Position

The tax system across all levels of government should be low, simple, and limited to funding core public functions.

At the federal level I would make the centerpiece a flat income tax with a large zero bracket and a short, transparent phase-in. Income below $42,000 for single filers and $84,000 for joint filers (adjusted for inflation) faces a zero rate. A linear phase-in then applies over the next $10,000 for single filers ($42,000 to $52,000) and $20,000 for joint filers ($84,000 to $104,000), so that the rate rises smoothly to the full flat rate of 20%. Income above the phase-in range faces that single flat rate. Capital gains and dividends receive a preferential but still flat 15% rate, which rises to the full 20% rate only if the automation trigger in AI and Automation is met. Through 2041 the 2-point transition surcharge applies to both rates, so the top rates are 22% on ordinary income and 17% on capital gains and dividends (22% if the trigger is met). I reject permanent progressive rate schedules, and the surcharge should go to the debt.

Most deductions, credits, and special preferences ought to be eliminated to keep the base broad and the rules simple.

Inherited assets should keep the original owner's cost basis (carryover basis) rather than a stepped-up basis. Today an heir's cost basis is "stepped up" to the value at death, so gains built up during the owner's life are never taxed. The Joint Committee on Taxation estimates this exclusion at $336 billion in forgone revenue for fiscal years 2024 to 2028. Under carryover basis the heir keeps the original cost basis and pays the flat tax only when the asset is sold. Nothing is taxed at death, so this is not an estate or inheritance tax, which I reject. JCT estimated that an earlier version of this change would raise about $105 billion over ten years. My view: Since restricting immigration will lower the population, we want as many things as possible to help parents and raise birth rates to replacement level (Immigration and Borders).

Secondary taxes (no VAT/sales; low corporate tax so business income is taxed once; transition levy; surcharge; Sanctuary Levy; permanent full expensing; local moderate property taxes; no estate tax; narrow excise and tariffs only), the two named time-limited exceptions to the single flat rate (the transition surcharge through 2041 and the Sanctuary Levy), and the essential-functions tax-take rule — including sanctuary care for the animals freed by the shutdown — are set out in In short and the example callouts above, not re-bulleted here.

question"Are payroll tax cuts and full expensing just corporate subsidies by another name?"

No. A subsidy transfers taxpayer money to a business. A payroll tax cut or full expensing takes less of the business's own money and transfers nothing. Both are consistent with the rule that taxpayer money subsidizes no business unless taxpayers opt in, and both apply across the board rather than to chosen firms or groups. Full expensing applies now. Payroll tax cuts are a long-run goal as the transition levy steps down.

question"Do these tax cuts stop outsourcing?"

Across-the-board payroll tax cuts and full expensing are the two broad tools with measured gains in U.S. jobs. Full expensing applies now. Payroll tax cuts are a long-run goal as the transition levy steps down.

question"Doesn't the flat tax mostly cut taxes for the rich?"

In dollar terms, the largest cuts go to the highest earners: the top federal rate is 37% today, against a flat 20% here (22% through 2041 with the transition surcharge), and capital income keeps a preferential rate of 15% (17% through 2041). Lower earners gain through the large zero bracket and the earnings-only top-up, and parents at every income get a refundable credit per child (Births and Family Formation), so most low-income workers owe no income tax at all. I do not claim the change is neutral by income. It rests on principle: one rate for everyone above the zero bracket, with no penalty for earning more. My static model's household table in Fiscal Policy shows every household tested paying less federal tax than today. Those are my estimates, not official scores.

question"Couldn't taxing billionaires pay for the government?"

Not for long. While $8.4 trillion is more than a full year of federal spending, and most wealth-tax proposals are yearly levies rather than one-time seizures, the yearly version raises little: Saez and Zucman estimated that Senator Warren's 2019 plan (2% a year above $50 million, 1% more above $1 billion) would raise about $2.75 trillion over ten years, about 1% of GDP a year, under a twentieth of federal spending at about 21% of GDP. But the fortunes themselves run out fast. Forbes counted 989 U.S. billionaires worth a combined $8.4 trillion on March 1, 2026. Federal outlays were $7.01 trillion in fiscal 2025, so taking every billionaire's entire fortune would pay for about 14 months of federal spending, once. Counting state and local governments, U.S. government spending ran at about $11.1 trillion a year in 2025 (BEA), which the same fortunes would cover for about nine months. Spending recurs every year. A fortune can be taken only once. That is one more reason I reject wealth taxes at every level, including California's Proposition 40 (State and Local Taxes).

Core claim: US has (or "might have") the most progressive tax system among developed countries

This is well-supported by a November 2025 Fraser Institute study (Measuring Tax Progressivity in High-Income Countries (OECD)). It ranks 45 high-income OECD jurisdictions on structural measures of progressivity (spread between top and bottom combined marginal personal income tax rates, income level at which the top rate applies relative to average wages, size of basic exemptions relative to average wages, share of revenue from progressive personal income taxes, and share from generally regressive consumption taxes).

  • California ranks 1st overall.
  • Texas ranks 4th (the study was updated in December 2025 to correct an earlier ranking of 5th).
  • Taking the US high- and low-tax jurisdictions together, the United States ranks highest among the countries/jurisdictions examined - ahead of nearly all Canadian provinces and European systems (California ranks above every Canadian province; Texas ranks below Newfoundland & Labrador, 2nd).

Scandinavian countries (often cited as models) rank much lower (e.g., Finland ~36th, Norway ~37th, Sweden ~41st) largely because they rely more heavily on broad consumption taxes (VAT rates of roughly 20-25.5%) that fall more evenly or regressively across income groups, and their top income-tax rates apply at lower multiples of average wages.

This is consistent with older OECD analyses of household taxes showing the US collecting a larger share of taxes from the top 10% relative to their income share than most peer countries. The high ranking is driven primarily by relatively low effective burdens on lower- and middle-income earners (large standard deductions, credits, absence of a national VAT, and top brackets starting at high multiples of average wages) rather than uniquely high statutory rates on the rich. High earners still pay the large majority of federal individual income taxes.

Important caveats for accuracy:

  • The Fraser Institute ranking is based on tax structure (rates, thresholds, and revenue composition), not a full distributional analysis of every tax paid after transfers.
  • Results can differ under other definitions (e.g., including all state/local taxes, property taxes, payroll taxes, or focusing on the extreme top with unrealized capital gains). Some analyses (including certain ITEP state rankings or studies of the very top of the wealth distribution) show less progressivity once these factors are emphasized.
  • It does not measure total tax levels or the net redistributive effect of government spending.

The claim is therefore accurate as a description of the US system's position among developed countries on standard progressivity metrics, though "most progressive in the world" is a slight overstatement relative to the study's OECD scope. Rationale

High and complex taxes distort work, saving, investment, and compliance. U.S. federal revenue as a share of the economy has remained relatively stable for decades even when top statutory rates changed dramatically; very high rates do not reliably produce proportionally higher revenue. Federal revenue ranged from 14.2% to 18.5% of GDP during the 1950s, according to OMB, when the top rate was 91% or higher, and CBO puts the 50-year average at 17.3% of GDP. That top rate reached few people. The 91% bracket of 1950 began at $200,000 of income, about $2 million today, and the top 0.1 percent paid an average effective income tax rate of about 21%. So the 1950s show high statutory rates, not high rates actually paid. Flat-tax reforms in other countries have shown that simplification is workable and need not collapse collections. An IMF review of flat-tax adoptions found that personal income tax revenue rose in some countries, such as Latvia, Lithuania, and Russia, and fell in others, with total revenue effects that varied by country.

A large zero bracket increases take-home pay for lower- and middle-income households and supports private spending. A short linear phase-in removes the sharp notch that would otherwise encourage people to hold income just below the threshold. Lower marginal rates above the phase-in reduce disincentives to work and invest, though the measured gains from flat-tax adoptions abroad come mainly from simplicity and compliance, not faster growth. Reviews find no evidence that the rate cuts paid for themselves, and in Russia's 2001 reform the rise in income tax revenue came from better compliance rather than more work. Neither a sales tax nor a VAT is used, so no new consumption tax can grow quietly. Corporate and payroll taxes should not serve as primary redistribution tools. Property tax is retained at the local level because it is a practical and accountable way to fund local order and infrastructure. Eliminating it would require less accountable replacements. Estate taxes confiscate property that has already been taxed.

No study measures whether any incentive reduces outsourcing itself. The evidence instead measures domestic employment and investment, and the two broad tools with measured gains in U.S. jobs are across-the-board payroll tax cuts and full expensing. The government ought to apply full expensing now and treat payroll tax cuts as a long-run goal as the transition levy steps down. In Norway, the abolition of lower regional payroll tax rates showed that each 1-percentage-point payroll tax increase cut local employment by 1.37% while wages fell only 0.32%. So lower payroll taxes support employment where wages do not fully adjust. A Swedish payroll tax cut for young workers raised their employment by 2–3 percentage points with no change in net wages. Bonus depreciation raised eligible investment by 10.4% (2001–04) and 16.9% (2008–10), and areas more exposed to it gained employment at roughly $20,000–$53,000 of forgone revenue per job, though earnings per worker did not rise. Targeted hiring credits, Opportunity Zones, domestic-content procurement rules, and firm-specific deals show weaker, short-lived, or far more costly results, often above $110,000 per job.

This structure is sustainable only alongside spending restraint. It can support strong defense, core public goods, and a minimal safety floor. It cannot support the present long-term trajectory of major entitlement programs without reforming those programs. The tax rules and the commitment to a smaller federal government are designed to fit together.

Corporate tax and other receipts cover what remains of core government. Interest is the item that shrinks only as the debt does, which is why the surcharge, the levy floor, and the debt brake all go to the debt first. CBO – The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036

question"Who pays for the benefits already promised?"

The transition levy, which replaces today's payroll taxes. It is a temporary tax that pays off existing promises. In 2026 Social Security costs about $1.67 trillion and Medicare about $1.06 trillion. The levy pays only for people who are protected and for credits earned before the change. It applies to all wages because 17.4% of earnings sit above today's Social Security cap, and a wider base lets the rate stay lower for everyone under it. Nobody earns new benefits from it, and nobody is enrolled in anything by paying it: it is a debt to the people already promised, and it shrinks as they pass. question"Why does the levy stay at 12% after the protected costs fall?"

Because the debt still has to be paid. On my model, letting the levy fall as soon as the protected costs fall would leave deficits in the 2050s. Holding it at 12% until debt is below 60% of GDP, then stepping it down half a point a year, would balance the budget in 2050 and keep it in surplus after. The levy would never be higher than in year one.

question"Where do the savings come from?"

Mostly from programs that end or shrink: non-emergency Medicaid, SNAP, farm payments, the Department of Education, and a 30% cut to other nondefense spending phased over three years. Spending lags appropriations: CBO estimates a one-third cut to transportation and education grants saves only $13 billion in its first year against $41 billion of funding cut. Medicare savings are counted only where CBO has scored them. Paying hospital outpatient departments site-neutral rates saves $156.9 billion over 2025–2034. question"What stops the plan from drifting off course?"

A debt brake. Each year debt is compared with the legislated path, leaving out sanctuary bonds while the Sanctuary Levy repays them on schedule. For each point of overshoot, the next year's income surcharge rises half a point, up to two points. The brake should be suspended in a recession. If it stays on for three years in a row, Congress ought to pass spending cuts to close the gap, and the surcharge should then end. Surpluses, owner income left after the top-up, and Federal Reserve remittances should go straight to the debt.

Monetary Policy

tldrIn short

Balanced budgets keep money stable. My goal is price stability, and I treat inflation as a hidden tax. The Fed should be reformed, not abolished: one price-stability mandate under a rule, no deficit finance, lending only to solvent banks, no bailouts, a full audit, legal competing currencies, and any profits to debt reduction.

Position

Stable money follows from fiscal restraint. With balanced budgets and debt reduction, the government does not rely on the central bank to finance deficits, and monetary policy aims at price stability. Inflation acts as an unlegislated tax on savings and wages, so preventing it is part of keeping the tax burden visible and matched to essential functions. I would reform the Federal Reserve, not abolish it. It should keep a single mandate, price stability under a published rule, and drop the employment mandate. It should not finance deficits or run asset purchases that work as deficit finance. As lender of last resort it ought to lend only to solvent banks, against good collateral, at a penalty rate, and never bail out insolvent firms, whose losses would otherwise fall on taxpayers who never consented. It should be fully audited in public. Competing private currencies should remain legal. Any profits it remits to the Treasury ought to go to debt reduction. Fed governors should be removable only for cause, such as misconduct or breaking the price-stability rule, proven with notice, the evidence and a hearing, and never for disagreeing with the President on interest rates. A Fed bound by one published rule needs no political steering, and that insulation keeps it from being pushed to finance deficits.

question"Isn't some inflation needed to manage the economy?"

Price stability is the aim. Balanced budgets remove the main pressure for inflationary finance, and stable prices let people plan, save, and invest.

question"Why not abolish the Fed?"

Before the Fed, money held its value over the long run but swung sharply, with frequent deflation and recurring bank panics in 1873, 1893, and 1907 that destroyed depositors' savings. The Fed's worst failure, the 1929 to 1933 collapse, came from its own discretion, as Ben Bernanke acknowledged in 2002. The lesson I draw is to limit discretion, not to remove the institution: a single price-stability rule, no deficit finance, and lending only to solvent banks. A return to gold is not favored, since countries that left the gold standard recovered from the Depression sooner.

Rationale

Consumer prices rose 9.1% over the year ended June 2022, the largest 12-month increase since November 1981, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Balanced budgets are set out in Fiscal Policy. By the Minneapolis Fed's consumer price index, the standard deviation of annual inflation was about 5.7 points in 1801 to 1912 and about 1.5 points in 1984 to 2025. But prices rose about 32-fold from 1913 to 2025, so a 1913 dollar buys about 3 percent of what it did. NBER business-cycle dates show average contractions of 21.6 months in 1854 to 1919 and 10.3 months in 1945 to 2020, though Christina Romer found that older data overstate pre-1914 volatility. The Federal Reserve Act sets three goals: maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates. The Fed remitted $107.4 billion to the Treasury for 2021; since then its interest costs have exceeded its income, and its deferred asset stood at $243.5 billion at the end of 2025.

Public-Sector Size and Civil Service

tldrIn short

Government should be sized to its essential functions, with defense first. Work private firms can do ought to be contracted out, and I would abolish agencies without an essential function. Career staff get severance or placement help. Political appointees' roles end with the office.

Position

I believe the size of government should follow from its functions. Spending and staffing ought to be matched to essential functions, with defense the top priority, and functions that private firms can perform should be contracted out, as government research already is. Agencies with no essential function, such as the federal Department of Education, should be abolished. When an agency is abolished, career staff who built their lives around these jobs deserve severance or placement help: that is the commitment side, honoring people who followed the rules. Political appointees are different; their roles end with the office, and I see no transition obligation to them.

question"Doesn't contracting out reduce accountability?"

Contracts set measurable deliverables and can be ended. A contractor that fails should lose the work, which is a stronger check than the near-permanence of a public office.

question"Should the government build new national monuments?"

Honoring veterans is worthy. A new monument should be paid for by private donors or opt-in funds rather than general taxes, because it is not an essential function that justifies compelled taxes. I believe the President should have discretion over monuments and improvements on federal property already under executive control. That discretion covers only property the executive already manages. It is not a power to take new land, spend money Congress has not appropriated, or go beyond the laws that authorize the executive (Government Structure and Checks).

question"Isn't American government already small next to other rich countries?"

Smaller than Europe's, yes; small, no. The IMF's October 2026 Fiscal Monitor puts U.S. general government spending, federal, state and local together, at 37.6% of GDP in 2025, against 57.2% in France, 50.1% in Germany and 43.5% in the United Kingdom. The same table puts China at 32.9%, Switzerland at 31.8% and Ireland at 22.3%. That measure counts spending, not state ownership or control, so it does not show that China's state is smaller than America's. It shows that American spending is high by any yardstick, including one on which a communist government spends less. I believe the size of government should follow from its essential functions, with defense first (Public-Sector Size and Civil Service), and that the budget should be balanced (Fiscal Policy). Measuring ourselves against France sets the bar too low.

Rationale

In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. The federal civilian workforce was about 2.3 million on September 30, 2024, according to the Office of Personnel Management. Government R&D through private contracts is set out in Bioethics, Progress, & Technology. The court fight over the current administration's attempt to fire the Voice of America director is graded in Current Administration.

Public Funding of NGOs

tldrIn short

I would defund NGOs that run on public money and end grants to nongovernmental organizations. A nonprofit should be paid only under a contract for an essential public service, with measurable deliverables, verified results and the right to end it for failure. No public money should pay for advocacy, lobbying, litigation or political campaigns, and no nonprofit is paid to pass public money on to others.

Position

The government ought to defund NGOs that run on public money. A nonprofit that lives on taxpayer grants is a government program with the accountability taken out: it spends public money without answering to voters and without the deliverables a contract would impose. The United States ought to end every federal grant to nongovernmental organizations, at home and abroad, and the states ought to do the same.

The rule has four parts. (1) No grants: no grant, cooperative agreement or other transfer without deliverables should go to a nongovernmental organization. (2) Contracts only: where a nonprofit performs an essential public function, it should be paid under a contract that names the service, sets measurable deliverables, checks them independently, pays only for what was delivered, and can be ended for failure, as with sanctuary operators (Sanctuary Plan). Where the work is put out to bid, nonprofits and firms should compete on the same terms. Money that follows a person to the provider they use, such as per-child school funding or the last-resort allowance paying for a placement, is payment for a service, not a grant. (3) No advocacy money: no public dollar, under a contract or otherwise, should pay for lobbying, advocacy campaigns, litigation against the government, or political organizing. (4) No pass-through: no nonprofit should be paid to hand public money on to other organizations. The agency should pay the provider that does the work. Causes with no essential function can still be funded through opt-in, taxpayer-designated funds, as charitable foreign aid is (Fiscal Policy). Tax exemption for charities is a separate question and is not changed here.

question"Don't NGOs deliver services cheaper and better than government?"

Supporters of NGO funding point to exactly that. If it is true, the contract rule costs them nothing: an NGO that really delivers more for less wins and keeps the contract on measured results, against public agencies and private firms alike. What ends is money handed over without competition or checked deliverables. Feeding Our Future, a Minnesota nonprofit that sponsored sites in the federal child nutrition program, went from receiving and disbursing about $3.4 million in federal funds in 2019 to nearly $200 million in 2021. It did so by opening more than 250 sites that filed fake meal counts, and it fraudulently obtained and disbursed more than $240 million, collecting more than $18 million in administrative fees for sponsoring them. Its founder was sentenced in May 2026 to 500 months in prison. A pass-through sponsor paid on self-reported claims is the model this rule ends.

question"Won't cutting grants close charities that people depend on?"

Critics cite the Urban Institute's finding that, in every state, 60 to 86 percent of the nonprofits that received government grants would have run at a loss without them from 2021 to 2023. That measures dependence, not value. Urban itself describes it as a measure of grant dependence, not a prediction. A charity that cannot cover its costs without taxpayers is already a public program, so where its service is essential it moves to a contract, and the rest compete for voluntary giving, which reached $592.50 billion in 2024, and for opt-in funds. Grants end over the phase-out window, not overnight.

question"Doesn't federal law already stop grants from paying for lobbying?"

Only narrowly. 31 U.S.C. § 1352 bars a recipient of a federal grant, contract, loan or cooperative agreement from using those appropriated funds to pay anyone to influence federal officials or Congress on a federal award. It restricts only the federal money itself and only lobbying on awards, so a grant still frees an organization's own money for advocacy, campaigns and lawsuits. Ending grants and barring advocacy spending under contracts closes that gap.

Rationale

In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition; that is why the phase-out window exists. The scale is large. The Urban Institute counts more than 80,000 501(c)(3) charities, about a third of those that electronically filed a full Form 990, reporting government grants each year from 2021 to 2023, worth $304 billion in 2021, $294 billion in 2022 and $240 billion in 2023, more than double foundation giving. That total leaves out Medicare and Medicaid payments, so it understates public funding.

France shows how far the model can go. In 2024 the French state made 114,778 payments to associations worth €9.5 billion. Subsidies were €7.3 billion of it, 76.7%, while payment for services was €923 million, 9.7%. The largest subsidy streams ran through school education (€1,162 million), territorial cohesion (€983 million), solidarity and equal opportunity (€973 million), special social and pension schemes (€875 million) and labor and employment (€797 million).

Accountability runs through elected officials (Civil Service Protections). A grant moves public money beyond that line. A contract keeps it there, because the agency that signs it answers for the results and can end it. Paying NGOs to lobby, campaign or sue also makes every taxpayer fund political speech they may oppose, the same coercion I reject in mandatory redistribution (Welfare and Entitlements). The current administration's planned triumphal arch, its East Potomac golf course closure, and its White House ballroom petition are graded in Current Administration: arch, golf course, ballroom.

Civil Service Protections

tldrIn short

Hiring should be by merit, and policy-setting jobs ought to answer to elected officials. Career staff cannot be fired for politics, but I want removal for poor performance or misconduct to be fast: notice, a 30-day decision, and one appeal decided within 90 days.

Position

Federal hiring should be by merit, through open competition and tested qualifications, never by party or loyalty. Positions that set or direct policy ought to serve at the pleasure of elected officials, so voters can hold the people they elect responsible for what the government does. Career staff in non-policy roles should keep one firm protection: no one is hired, fired, or punished for party, political speech, or refusing an unlawful order. Removal for poor performance or misconduct ought to be fast and fair: written notice of the specific reasons, a chance to answer, a decision within 30 days, and one appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board decided within 90 days. The probationary period should be two years, with an affirmative decision required before it ends.

question"Won't weakening protections bring back the spoils system?"

That is the strongest objection. Protections were built after the spoils era because jobs handed out for loyalty produced corruption and incompetence, and a workforce that can be fired at will can be pressured to serve a party rather than the law. The government ought to keep the part of the old settlement that answers this: merit hiring, and a ban on firing or punishing anyone for party, political speech, or refusing an unlawful order, enforceable in court. What it removes is delay. Reclassifying policy roles is legitimate only for positions that genuinely set policy. Using it to reach ordinary career staff would be the spoils system by another name.

question"Is it really that hard to fire a federal employee?"

It is slow. The Government Accountability Office found that removing a poor-performing permanent employee can take six months to a year or longer, and that an appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board averaged 243 days in 2013. Agencies dismissed about 3,500 employees for performance or conduct that year, most during the probationary period, when appeal rights are limited. The delay discourages managers from acting at all.

Rationale

Accountability runs through elected officials, so those who set policy answer to them (Public-Sector Size and Civil Service). Due process still applies to every removal (Due Process and Rule of Law), but process that takes a year protects poor performance more than it protects fairness. Keeping a hard line against political firing protects both the employee and the public from a workforce bent to party ends.

Welfare and Entitlements

tldrIn short

I would phase out existing benefits for people who followed the rules, with grandfathered Social Security and Medicare for life. Welfare should be cut to a bare-minimum floor for basic needs, with no compelled redistribution beyond that floor and other social programs opt-in, so no worker is made to fund programs they never chose. I support an earnings-only top-up paid through the tax return: 10% of earnings, peaking at $15,000 of earnings (at most $1,500 per worker), plus up to $1,000 more per child for up to three children, for all working parents, paid only on earnings. It is paid per worker and phases out on household income from $15,000 to $42,000 (married: $30,000 to $84,000). Legal residents and lawfully present non-citizens who pay taxes receive it on the same terms as citizens.

For people who cannot work, the order is private disability first (by default), then charity, then a last-resort means-tested allowance ($575/$900 plus local rent). Failing the net-taxpayer test suspends the vote (net fiscal voting bar).

Position

People who followed the rules and are on benefits should not be punished when the system changes to something better, so I would build in a phase-out period that honors existing commitments. The obligation is to the person, not to the old funding stream: a person's status, meaning their entitlement, is honored through the transition, while the funding sources behind those benefits may change.

I hold that failing the net-taxpayer test should suspend the franchise. A person cannot vote on the taxes and programs that move money to them if their personally directed transfers meet or exceed the taxes they pay, or if their pre-exemption income-tax liability is zero. That is a massive conflict of interest (Voting and Elections · net fiscal voting bar). Transfers include the last-resort allowance, the earnings-only top-up, and refundable credit cash-outs. Grandfathered Social Security and Medicare, shared public goods, and non-refundable relief such as the mothers' income-tax exemption are not counted as received. The benefit remains available. Failing the test suspends the vote until a completed tax year shows otherwise. The full counting rules, including joint filers and SS-only retirees, are at the voting-bar link above.

No. USDA "food insecurity" is a survey measure of constrained or anxious access to food, not chronic starvation. NHANES patterns show food-insecure adults in the United States, especially women, have higher obesity rates than food-secure adults, not lower body weight. The bare-minimum floor covers basic food need. It is not sized as if the typical insecure household were wasting away. Nature Scientific Reports – food insecurity and obesity, NHANES-based analyses

question"What happens if opt-in funding falls short?"

The program should scale to what its contributors fund. It should not fall back on general taxation. The bare-minimum safety floor for basic necessities should be funded separately by minimal necessary taxes, so a shortfall in an opt-in program should never remove that floor.

question"Isn't the top-up redistribution?"

Yes. It would be funded partly from owner income and otherwise from general revenue, so it is a narrow, named exception to the rule against compelled redistribution, like emergency shelter, emergency medical care, and the K-12 backstop. It should be limited in three ways: it should be paid only on earned income, it should end at the zero-bracket line, and it should replace the minimum wage and separate wage-subsidy credits rather than adding to them. Redistribution beyond the named exceptions remains rejected.

question"Why does the top-up include children?"

A worker supporting children has more to cover on the same pay, and the strongest evidence for earnings top-ups comes from working parents. The add-on would be modest and paid only on earnings, so it would reward work rather than having children. It would go to all working parents, married or single, with no marriage penalty, and it would stop at three children.

question"Why per worker but phased out by household?"

Paying each worker keeps the top-up tied to that person's own work. Phasing it out on household income keeps it for households that are actually low-income: a teenager or second earner in a well-off household should get nothing. Because the married range is double the single range, a couple should neither gain nor lose by marrying, splitting, or combining households.

question"What about people who can't work?"

They are covered in layers. Employers enroll workers by default in private disability insurance, and workers may opt out. The self-employed and people between jobs get the same choice through their tax return. The insurance is private. The state does not run it. Those within the grandfathered Social Security system keep that protection. People who can work some draw the earnings-only top-up on what they earn. Charity and mutual aid, including respite for family caregivers, come next. As a last resort, people with severe disabilities and the elderly poor receive a strictly means-tested allowance from general revenue, under the same backstop logic as grandfathered Social Security. It is checked by fraud enforcement and periodic medical reviews, and it is a narrow exception, not a general welfare entitlement.

question"How much does the last-resort allowance cost?"

In 2025, SSI paid $64.85 billion to about 7.4 million people. Restricted to severe disability and the elderly poor, the allowance's cash cost is estimated to stay close to that, because the higher standard is offset by the narrower eligibility. Health coverage for recipients is the larger cost, but it replaces Medicaid spending for the same people rather than adding to it. These are estimates, not official scores.

question"Won't a higher allowance invite fraud?"

It is checked by income and asset verification, fraud enforcement, and periodic medical reviews. Only severe disability should qualify, and fraud should be punished as theft. Income from any source reduces the allowance, so there is nothing to gain from hiding behind it while working.

question"Why not one flat national amount?"

Rent is the largest cost and varies more than anything else. A studio in Los Angeles has a fair market rent of $2,079 a month, far above what one flat amount could cover without overpaying everywhere else. Tying the housing part to local rent pays what basic housing actually costs where the person lives, no more.

question"Why does the allowance include health coverage?"

Severe disability and frailty mean ongoing care, not just emergencies. Emergency-only care would leave the people least able to provide for themselves without treatment for chronic conditions, which is often more expensive when it ends in the emergency room. Covering them inside the same narrow exception keeps everyone else's healthcare opt-in.

question"Why are severely disabled children covered when parents are responsible for their children?"

Parents remain responsible for the ordinary costs of raising a child. A severe disability can add costs far beyond what any ordinary family budget can carry. The child allowance, up to $575 a month, covers only those extra costs, in severe cases, so that parents can keep caring for the child at home.

question"What happens to an elderly person with no home and no family who needs care?"

A shelter, hospital, police officer, or charity refers them to the allowance office, or they apply at any shelter. Within a few days a licensed, neutral clinician assesses them, and once certified they are placed in the least costly setting that safely meets their needs, such as a studio with home care, assisted living, or a nursing home, run by private or charity providers. Until then they stay in shelter, or in hospital rather than being discharged to the street. If they cannot manage their own affairs, a court appoints a guardian or advocate, preferably a volunteer or a charity.

Rationale

In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition. That is why the phase-out window exists. Mandatory redistribution takes from some citizens to fund others, which I treat as coercion beyond the state's core role. Major entitlement programs also face long-term funding shortfalls under current law, so structural reform is needed regardless. The 2025 Trustees Reports project Social Security's combined trust funds to be depleted in 2034, with 81% of scheduled benefits then payable, and Medicare's Hospital Insurance trust fund in 2033. An opt-in model lets those who wish to fund social programs above the floor do so, while the minimal floor for basic necessities stays funded by minimal necessary taxes. Voluntary giving alone could not yet carry the floor: SNAP benefits in FY2024 ($92.9 billion) exceeded all U.S. charitable giving to human services in 2024 ($91.15 billion).

An earnings-only top-up is the tool with the strongest evidence for low earners. Hoynes and Patel found that a $1,000 increase in the Earned Income Tax Credit raised single mothers' employment by 7.3 percentage points and cut the share of their families below the poverty line by 9.4 points. Paying it only on earned income matters: in the Seattle-Denver income maintenance experiment, which guaranteed income whether or not people worked, husbands cut their hours by about 9 percent and wives by about 20 percent. Delivering the top-up through the flat tax replaces the EITC's separate rules, which produce large errors. The Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration reported that 27.3 percent of EITC payments in FY2024 ($15.9 billion of $58.4 billion) were improper. Improper payments will not vanish and should still be policed, but one return with one phase-out leaves fewer rules to get wrong. The top-up is far smaller than the EITC it replaces: the EITC paid about $69.6 billion to about 24 million claimants for tax year 2024, with a 2026 maximum of $7,316 for two children. I modelled the top-up on the Census Bureau's 2025 Current Population Survey microdata, applying the rules exactly as set out here to every household, with 2024 incomes carried forward to 2026 by wage and price growth. On my middle case it costs about $26 billion a year, about $17.5 billion for the base and $8 billion for the child add-on, paid to about 20 million households. Granted, survey data understate what the IRS pays out: the same data and method put the 2024 EITC at about $45 billion, against the $69.6 billion the IRS paid. My middle case scales the top-up up by the same ratio as the EITC's proper payments, the IRS total less the 27.3 percent paid improperly, bear to the survey figure, and it allows a small earnings response to the top-up's own rates. If the top-up instead runs over its survey estimate by the full EITC gap, improper claims included, and earnings respond more strongly, it costs about $36 billion. If the survey is right and only 85% of those eligible claim it, slightly more than the 81% who claim the EITC, it costs about $19 billion. The model counts every worker because the survey cannot separate workers without legal status, who would not receive it; about 9.7 million of them were in the workforce in 2023, so these figures overstate the cost by about $2 billion. Even at the high end it costs about half the EITC it replaces, which runs about $70 billion a year by 2026, so the replacement saves about $44 billion a year on my middle case ($34 billion to $51 billion). This is my own estimate, not an official score. Granted, by the same evidence a smaller credit gives weaker work incentives: the top-up pays a single parent of two at most $3,500, against $7,316 under the EITC. I accept that trade: the top-up keeps support tied to work and ends at the zero-bracket line, at a third to half the cost. The phase-out takes at most about 16.7 cents of each extra dollar, for a single parent of three, against 21.06 cents under the EITC for families with two or more children. The $1,000 add-on sits below the $1,700 refundable portion of the 2026 child tax credit (maximum $2,200 per child), which the flat tax also replaces. An amount above about $1,395 per child would push a single parent of three past the EITC's 21.06 percent.

In August 2026, 7.3 million people received Supplemental Security Income, averaging $738.73 a month, and 7.0 million disabled workers received earned Social Security Disability Insurance benefits averaging $1,635.90. The need is real: in 2023, 24.2 percent of working-age adults with disabilities living in the community were in poverty, against 9.9 percent of those without. Means-tested programs are also error-prone: SSI's improper-payment rate rose from 9.41 percent in FY2019 to 10.62 percent (about $6.5 billion) in FY2023, which is why the last-resort allowance carries fraud enforcement and periodic medical review. Work-focused design shrinks the group who cannot work at all. After the Netherlands required employers to attempt rehabilitation before a disability claim (2002) and replaced its disability law with one built around remaining work capacity (2006), new disability claims fell by about 25 and 21 percentage points respectively, and the caseload by more than 60 percent. Voluntary institutions once carried much of this load: by David Beito's conservative estimate, one in three adult American men belonged to a fraternal society in 1920.

The current federal SSI maximum is $994 a month for an individual and $1,491 for a couple in 2026. In December 2025, the average federal SSI payment was $688.47, and 7.26 million people received federal payments, 1.15 million aged and 6.11 million blind or disabled, of whom 1.02 million were children. That is well below what basic needs cost. The 2026 poverty guideline for one person is $15,960 a year, about $1,330 a month. The USDA's Thrifty Food Plan alone costs $236.30 to $281.20 a month for an adult aged 51 to 70. HUD's fair market rent for a Los Angeles studio is $2,079. Health and care needs are larger still. Medicaid spent $27,361 per disabled enrollee and $20,305 per aged enrollee in FY2023. A semi-private nursing home room costs a national median of $9,581 a month, against $6,673 for a home caregiver 44 hours a week, which is why I prefer home care where it costs less. The Medicare Part B premium is $202.90 a month in 2026. The current administration's 2026 SNAP rules are graded in Current Administration: work requirements and categorical eligibility.

infoWhat is an opt-in funding model?

An opt-in model funds a program through voluntary contributions from those who choose to support it, rather than through mandatory taxes on everyone. A voluntary checkoff, where a taxpayer chooses to direct a contribution to a program, counts as opt-in.

Social Security and Medicare Transition

tldrIn short

Social Security and Medicare should become voluntary for new benefits: nobody is forced into the new system. A temporary transition levy, which replaces today's payroll taxes, pays off the benefits already promised and ends when they end. I would protect current retirees, and people within ten years of full retirement age, before the new system comes in, as an honored commitment, and they keep their benefits for life. The old system fades out only as fewer people remain in it. The separate basic safety floor stays. Employers should sign workers up for private retirement savings by default, and anyone can opt out.

Position

Social Security and Medicare should become opt-in only. Participation and contributions for new benefits ought to be voluntary; no one should be compelled to join either program or to rely on it, and those who opt out arrange their own retirement savings and coverage. The transition levy is a temporary tax that pays off existing promises; it buys no new benefits and ends when they end (Taxation). Current retirees ought to be protected before the new system comes in: their benefits are an existing commitment that is not a rights violation, and existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition to the new system. People within ten years of full retirement age should get the same protection as current retirees, grandfathered on current benefit formulas, as part of the same honored commitment. Grandfathered people should keep Social Security and Medicare for life. The old system phases out because fewer people remain in it, not because benefits end on a set date. This protection is part of the position, not a practical concession. The separate bare-minimum safety floor for basic necessities, defined in Welfare and Entitlements, is unaffected.

Employers should enroll workers by default in a private retirement savings account, and workers should be free to opt out. The self-employed make the same choice on their tax return. The state should not run or hold the accounts. I don't set the default contribution rate. One rule ties this to the disability-insurance default in Welfare and Entitlements: defaults apply only where a person mainly protects themselves, as with disability insurance and retirement savings. Anything that funds other people stays opt-in.

noteRealistic transition

In a realistic world, many systems — like taxes, like education, like all the systems that would be changing — would have some gradual period of change for most of these changes to happen. That practical lag is a brief aside, not the main position.

question"Doesn't moving to opt-in break the promise made to people who paid in?"

Current retirees are protected before the new system comes in, because existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition. People who planned around the current system are not cut off, and the basic-necessities floor remains. Grandfathered people keep their benefits for life. The old system shrinks only as fewer people remain in it. The programs are also not financed for the long term under current law, so continuing them unchanged would itself break the promise of scheduled benefits.

question"Who pays for the protected benefits, and what about people who paid in but are more than ten years from retirement?"

Protected benefits are paid first by opt-in contributions and then by the transition levy, which replaces today's payroll taxes and ends when the protected benefits end. The government ought to pay for the rest of the transition with spending cuts, not new debt. The retirement age rises two years, phased in, for people not yet 67, and protected benefits for people now aged 57–66 are reduced 15% above a means-test threshold. That is the price of keeping everyone else's promise without new debt. People more than ten years from full retirement age keep a credit for the contributions they already paid, payable at retirement.

question"Won't moving to opt-in push the elderly into poverty?"

Critics warn that ending Social Security would push millions of older people into poverty, and the scale they point to is large. Social Security is the country's largest antipoverty program: in 2024 it moved 28.7 million people above the Census Bureau's Supplemental Poverty Measure line, 20.1 million of them aged 65 or older. Even with it, 15.0% of people 65 and older were poor under that measure in 2024. Without it, the Census Bureau estimates their rate would have been 32.7 points higher, about 47.7%. Benefit payments that year were about $1.47 trillion ($1,316.4 billion retirement and survivors, $155.0 billion disability), and the program costs about 5.3% of GDP in 2025. That is why the transition is built around the people at risk. Current retirees and everyone within ten years of full retirement age keep their benefits for life, so no one now old or nearly old loses income. Younger workers keep a credit for what they have already paid, and default enrollment in private accounts builds their own savings. The last-resort allowance remains for the elderly poor who still fall short. The price of that protection is that the benefits run for decades. The backstop should cover benefits on the order of today's payments at first, shrinking only as the grandfathered group shrinks, and the program is already short, with the combined trust funds projected to be depleted in 2034. For people who reach old age after the transition with too little saved, the allowance is thinner than a Social Security check by design: it is a bare-minimum floor, not a pension, and it still covers the elderly poor, because it is means-tested rather than earned. Any eligible elderly person below the line qualifies, whatever they paid in, and it pays for food, phone, transport, and personal needs plus local rent, rising each year with prices and rents (last-resort allowance).

question"Isn't default enrollment a form of coercion?"

No. Nobody would be forced to save: opting out should be free and simple, and the money should stay in the worker's own private account. The default only changes what happens when a person does nothing. The evidence shows how much that matters. In Madrian and Shea's study of one large U.S. employer, 401(k) participation among new hires rose from 37% under opt-in to 86% under automatic enrollment. In Great Britain, where employers have enrolled workers automatically since 2012, 89% of eligible employees were saving into a workplace pension in 2024. More people with their own savings means fewer who end up relying on the last-resort allowance in old age.

Rationale

Compelled contributions to retirement and health programs are redistribution beyond the floor, which I reject. The transition levy is different: it is a temporary tax that pays off promises already made, buys no new benefits, and ends when those promises end. Existing commitments that aren't rights violations are honored during the transition, which is why current retirees are protected before the new system comes in. The protection extends to people within ten years of full retirement age because the transition risk lands hardest on them. They have planned and paid in around the current system and have the least time left to build their own retirement savings and coverage. The programs also face shortfalls under current law. The 2025 Trustees Reports project Social Security's combined trust funds to be depleted in 2034, with 81% of scheduled benefits payable after that, and Medicare's Hospital Insurance trust fund in 2033. CBO projects Medicare spending to rise from 2.6% of GDP in 2025 to 5.2% in 2055. Structural reform is needed regardless; the government ought to set its direction toward voluntary participation. Default enrollment fills the gap that opt-in leaves, because inertia is strong: Madrian and Shea found that most workers hired under automatic enrollment also kept the default contribution rate and fund. That same finding is why I leave the default rate unset rather than picking a number the evidence does not single out; the rate will shape how much people save.

Births and Family Formation

tldrIn short

America should get back above replacement, 2.1 children per woman. I would do it with support that grows with family size, cheaper housing through freed-up building, and a higher-trust culture that honors marriage and children (phone-free schools, childhood-independence laws, car-seat mandate review), not with mandates or a large welfare state.

Position

The U.S. fertility rate hit a record low of about 1.6 in 2024 and has been below replacement since 2008. Americans say the ideal family has 2.7 children, so most of the gap comes from obstacles, not from what people want. I believe the government's job is to remove the obstacles it controls: taxes on parents, housing scarcity, and school costs. Culture and faith, which matter most, ought to be carried by families, churches, and communities.

The fertility collapse is mostly a marriage collapse. Age-controlled marital fertility has held up or risen in some cells while marriage rates cratered. Fewer and later marriages drive the drop (data below). Policy that only writes checks without making early pairing easier will underperform. Money is a lever, not the whole cause. I treat higher trust — built through assimilation, values-tested immigration, and national cohesion argued elsewhere — as a natalist prior, not a measured policy lever with a published effect size on this page.

The package I support, evidenced in the Data callouts: a refundable child credit of $1,500 for the first and second child and $3,000 for the third and each later child. A one-time refundable $5,000 birth credit for third and later births; and no federal income tax on a mother's earned income for life after her fourth child, half after her third, on up to $30,000 of earnings a year. Under the net fiscal voting bar, non-refundable relief is not received, and refundable cash-outs count only when liability would otherwise be ≤ $0. The mothers' exemption alone does not fail the liability gate when pre-exemption liability is > $0. SNAP, TANF, the last-resort allowance, and the top-up count in full.

Housing: by-right starter homes and duplexes, including family-size units (3+ bedroom apartments, single-stair buildings, no bedroom-count caps), and repeal of minimum lot sizes and parking minimums. Work and money: childcare deregulation within safety limits, with home daycare by right. Growth and rising earnings for young adults through the flat tax and job growth; no federal paid-leave mandate on employers; and no marriage penalty anywhere in the tax code. Fertility: IVF legal and unrestricted, with no insurance mandate and honest age-and-fertility information in existing health education. Childhood: phone-free schools set by states and districts, Reasonable Childhood Independence laws on the Utah model, and state review of car-seat age mandates. The leaving-home, meeting-place, and career-path levers are in the notes below. Culture, faith, and marriage should be carried by families and churches, not the state. The United States ought not to use immigration to fill the gap. The nation should renew itself through its own families (Immigration and Borders).

noteLeaving home earlier

I want young adults to be able to afford a place of their own in their early twenties, because people who move out tend to be the ones who marry and start families. The fix is supply, not subsidy: legalize small starter apartments, accessory dwelling units, shared and roommate housing, and by-right starter homes, so a first lease costs less.

Child Support

tldrIn short

I believe both genetic parents who consented to the sex owe support. Civil orders should come first. Willful refusal after an order brings escalating penalties. A rape victim owes no duty from the rape. The rapist owes full support.

Position

I hold that voluntary conception creates a support duty on both genetic parents who consented to the sex. Support should be enforced by civil order first; willful refusal after an order is a rights violation, a fraud on the child's claim, with escalating penalties; any fines are scaled to income and wealth. In cases of rape, the victim parent owes no duty created by the rape, and the rapist parent owes full support.

question"Does a parent who was raped owe support?"

No. Duties arise from voluntary responsibility, and rape is not voluntary for the victim. The rapist parent owes full support.

Rationale

Duties arise from voluntary responsibility, matching the voluntary-procreation responsibility in Ethical and Philosophical Framework. A parent who consented to the sex took on the child's claim; refusing an order to meet it defrauds the child. Rape is not voluntary for the victim, so no duty arises for the victim from it. Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness

tldrIn short

I'd leave housing mainly to property owners and the market. Basic zoning should be allowed, but rules that wipe out property value must be paid for, and housing subsidies need voluntary funding. The safety floor should offer emergency shelter only, not housing, and charities and private groups are free to run Housing First. Camping and drug use in public ought to be banned.

Position

Housing supply ought to be governed by property rights and the market. Ordinary zoning that coordinates land use is acceptable, but regulation that eliminates the economically beneficial use of property, or destroys most of its value, is a taking and requires compensation. Population growth should be met through densification, development, and voluntary exchange, not political allocation of land. Housing subsidies should require taxpayer opt-in. For homelessness, the safety floor ought to provide emergency shelters only; it is not a housing program. The one exception is the rent component of the last-resort allowance for severe disability and the elderly poor (Welfare and Entitlements). Charities and private groups are free to run Housing First programs, and opt-in funding can support them; the public rent component stays limited to the last-resort allowance. Camping on public property should not be permitted, and public drug use should be prohibited.

question"Doesn't Housing First work better than shelters?"

Supporters cite its housing results: a meta-analysis of randomized trials found Housing First participants about 2.46 times as likely to be stably housed, with fewer emergency-department visits and hospital stays, though no clear improvement in mental health or substance use. Results for its recipients do not make it a public duty. Housing First is a housing subsidy, so it is left to charities, private groups, and opt-in funding, which are free to run it. The public floor stays at emergency shelter, and the only public rent support is the last-resort allowance.

Rationale

Secure property and open land markets are how societies have historically absorbed population growth with rising living standards (Authority & Civil Liberties). Homelessness has grown. HUD's 2024 point-in-time count found more than 770,000 people experiencing homelessness on a single night in January 2024, an 18% increase from 2023. HUD attributed part of the rise to steep rent increases and years of under-building, along with migration and natural disasters. Supply restrictions that hold back building bear directly on the problem. Randomized trials show Housing First keeps more people stably housed than treatment-first approaches, which is why the government ought to leave charities and opt-in programs free to run it while the public floor stays at shelter. The basic-necessities floor is set out in Welfare and Entitlements, and the rule on public drug use in Drug Policy.

Blocking building is what pushes rents up, and a rent freeze adds a second shortage on top of the first: when rents are capped, owners take units off the rental market, so everyone still looking for a home faces fewer and costlier choices (data below). Government Home Lending

tldrIn short

The government should not use taxpayer money to make home loans cheaper. Federal lending and loan guarantees must cover their own costs, below-market rates need opt-in funding, and I would cut rules that only add closing costs. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac should leave conservatorship only once fully capitalized, as private companies with no federal backstop.

Position

Federal home lending should carry no hidden subsidy. Loans the government makes or guarantees ought to be priced to cover their own risk and costs, and any rate below that is a housing subsidy that requires taxpayer opt-in, like every other subsidy (Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness). Federal requirements that add cost without protecting the borrower or the taxpayer, such as duplicate reviews, unnecessary appraisals and mandatory products that automated checks can replace, ought to be removed. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac ought to leave conservatorship only once they meet the full capital rule, and then as private companies with no federal guarantee, explicit or implied; until then their guarantee fees ought to cover the full risk they place on taxpayers.

question"Doesn't the federal guarantee keep mortgages affordable?"

Supporters argue that the backing behind Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac keeps the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage widely available at lower rates, and it does lower rates. The lower rate is paid for by risk that taxpayers carry without consent, which makes it a subsidy. The two companies are still in federal conservatorship, and at the end of the second quarter of 2026 their estimated capital ratios were about 2.84% (Fannie Mae) and 2.36% (Freddie Mac), below the Federal Housing Finance Agency's 4% leverage requirement and its 8% risk-based requirement. Releasing them before they meet that rule would leave taxpayers holding losses an undercapitalized company cannot absorb. Private lenders and securitizers can offer long fixed-rate loans priced to their real risk.

Healthcare

tldrIn short

I want people to choose between a publicly supported plan and private insurance. No one should be required to buy coverage, but the public plan has one yearly sign-up window and a modest surcharge for joining late.

Position

Healthcare ought to be organized around freedom of choice. Individuals should be free to opt into publicly supported systems or purchase private coverage. I reject mandates. The voluntary public plan has one annual open-enrollment window, and people who join late pay a modest surcharge, modeled on Medicare Part B's 10 percent for each full year a person was eligible but not enrolled. No one should be required to join or penalized for staying out; the surcharge applies only to those who choose to join later. Emergency care should stay tax-funded, and people on the last-resort allowance should be enrolled automatically.

question"Without a mandate, will healthy people avoid coverage and drive up costs?"

Critics warn of adverse selection, and Massachusetts shows a mandate can work against it: when the state's mandate took effect, coverage in the individual market rose, and average costs and premiums fell. Most of the premium drop came from lower insurer markups; a smaller share came from a healthier mix of enrollees. The 2019 repeal of the federal penalty did not bring the collapse that warning predicts: a study comparing states that kept their own mandates found no significant change in coverage or marketplace enrollment. The government keeps choice and limits the problem without a mandate. The public plan has one annual enrollment window, and late joiners pay a modest surcharge, as in Medicare Part B, so people cannot wait until they are sick to sign up. No one is compelled to buy insurance.

question"What about people who can't afford private coverage?"

They can opt into a publicly supported system. Emergency medical care would remain part of the safety floor, so no one in a genuine emergency would be left without care (Economic Orientation). People on the last-resort allowance for severe disability or old-age poverty would be enrolled in the public plan automatically, paid under that narrow exception (Welfare and Entitlements).

question"Doesn't a majority support Medicare for All?"

KFF's September 2026 poll finds that 66% of adults favor a national health plan, sometimes called Medicare-for-all, in which all Americans would get their insurance from a single government plan, and 34% oppose it, the highest favorability KFF has recorded. When people hear claims about the plan, that share changes. After hearing it would "eliminate private health insurance companies," 52% favor it and 47% oppose it. Favor falls to 24% if the plan could lead to delays in getting some medical tests or treatment, and to 35% if most Americans would pay more in taxes. It rises to 75% if the plan would eliminate premiums and reduce out-of-pocket costs. I still oppose Medicare for All. People should choose between a voluntary public plan and private insurance, with taxpayers funding the public option only by choice, and Medicare voluntary for new entrants (Healthcare Funding; Social Security and Medicare Transition).

question"Wouldn't Medicare for All save tens of thousands of lives a year?"

The best-known estimate, Galvani and colleagues in The Lancet (2020), calculates that "ensuring health-care access for all Americans would save more than 68 000 lives and 1·73 million life-years every year compared with the status quo." The evidence that coverage saves lives is real but uneven. In Oregon's 2008 Medicaid lottery, coverage "generated no significant improvements in measured physical health outcomes in the first 2 years," though it raised use of care, lowered depression and "nearly eliminated catastrophic out-of-pocket medical expenditures." In an IRS experiment that sent letters to 3.9 million uninsured taxpayers, the added coverage reduced mortality among middle-aged adults. Either way, the lives in that estimate come from covering the uninsured, not from making one government plan compulsory for everyone. My plan reaches the same people without the mandate: anyone may join the voluntary public plan, people on the last-resort allowance are enrolled automatically, and emergency care stays tax-funded (Healthcare). Single payer then adds the congestion and delays CBO projects (see the prior-authorization question below) on top of compelled funding. Choosing a voluntary plan over a compulsory one does not mean choosing more deaths.

question"Wouldn't the taxes just replace premiums and copays?"

Yes. A single-payer plan would replace much of what people now pay in premiums, deductibles, and copays, so a taxes-only description is incomplete. In the same KFF poll, favorability rises to 75% when people hear that the plan would eliminate premiums and reduce out-of-pocket costs, and falls to 35% when they hear that most Americans would pay more in taxes. Those were asked as separate messages, not as one combined tradeoff. I still reject compelled funding. The public health option should be voluntary, with taxpayers funding it only by choice (Healthcare Funding).

question"Would ending prior authorization and claim denials speed up care under Medicare for All?"

Prior authorization and claim denials do delay some patients. In the Commonwealth Fund's 2023 survey of adults in 10 countries, 27% of U.S. adults skipped a doctor visit because of the cost in the past year, against 8% in Canada and 7% in the United Kingdom, and 32% said insurance denied payment or paid less than expected, against 18% and 3%. CBO's 2020 and 2022 single-payer analyses project that under illustrative designs the amount of care used would rise, and in that sense overall access would be greater, but demand would exceed supply, with "increased congestion in the health care system - including delays and forgone care," especially when cost sharing and payment rates are lower. Access would rise for some who are uninsured or face high cost sharing today, and decline for some now on private insurance in congested areas. Among those who saw or needed a specialist, 46% in Canada and 42% in the United Kingdom waited two months or more for an appointment, against 16% in the United States; 9.6% and 10.7% waited a year or more, against 1.6%. I still oppose Medicare for All. People should choose between a voluntary public plan and private insurance.

Rationale

Compelling citizens to buy a product conflicts with my limits on state coercion. Offering both public and private options keeps access while preserving choice. Federal health spending is a major driver of long-term budget pressure, which supports keeping public systems opt-in. CBO projects major federal health spending to rise from 5.8% of GDP in 2025 to 8.1% in 2055, with Medicare rising from 3.1% to 5.2% of GDP. Without a mandate, a voluntary plan risks adverse selection: Hackmann, Kolstad, and Kowalski found that when the Massachusetts mandate took effect, coverage in the individual market rose, and average costs and premiums fell. Most of the premium drop came from lower insurer markups; a smaller share came from a healthier mix of enrollees. After the federal penalty was repealed in 2019, one study comparing states that kept their own mandates found no significant change in coverage or marketplace enrollment. Medicare Part B shows a non-mandate tool. Its late-enrollment penalty adds 10% for each full 12-month period a person was eligible but not enrolled, usually for as long as they have Part B. The public plan borrows that design in modest form.

I still reject mandates and compelled single-payer funding. I want a voluntary public plan and opt-in financing because I limit state coercion and long-term federal budget pressure, not because I claim U.S. spending is unmatched once wealth is taken into account.

The public health option should be voluntary, with taxpayers funding it only by choice. I count emergency care among the basic services taxes pay for. Drug and insurance rules ought to exist only to protect safety and stop consumer abuse, and nobody should have to buy insurance.

Position

The publicly supported healthcare option should be opt-in, and its funding ought to follow the rule for all programs beyond core services: subsidies require taxpayer opt-in. Insurance regulation should be limited to preventing fraud and anti-consumer practices. No one should be required to buy coverage. question"What happens to people who have no coverage and need emergency care?"

K-12 Funding

tldrIn short

I'd have each state fund about $10,000 per child per year for basic K-12 education from its general revenue, roughly what Utah and Idaho spend, with an extra amount for students with disabilities that each state sets from actual need. The money follows the child to any public school, district or charter, anywhere in the state. Private and religious schools stay legal but get no public money; parents pay for them. Homeschooling families get a $1,000 tax credit per child per year. Pre-K is excluded.

Position

The $10,000 per-child average is the level the evidence points to now - close to what Utah and Idaho spend - not a floor and not a slogan. It moves up or down only with outcome data; any change alters state tax calculations. Students with disabilities get an added amount each state sets from actual need; no fixed national figure. Publicly funded means publicly accountable: the money follows the child to any public school, district or charter, statewide. Private and religious schools should stay legal with no public funds; parents pay. Homeschooling families get a fully refundable $1,000 federal income-tax credit per child per year (Homeschooling); pre-K is excluded.

My view: Since restricting immigration will lower the population, we want as many things as possible to help parents and raise birth rates to replacement level (Taxation; Immigration and Borders).

question"Is $10,000 per child enough?"

Utah spent $10,333 per pupil and Idaho $10,247 in FY2023, against a national average of $16,526, and both scored at or above the national results in 2024 NAEP eighth-grade math and reading (Utah 282 and 261, Idaho 278 and 261). New York spent $30,012 and scored 271 and 257. Critics answer that the states' student populations differ, so raw averages are not a clean test. The comparison holds within the same groups, where the low spenders still match or beat New York: in 2024 eighth-grade math, white students scored 289 in Utah and 284 in Idaho against 283 in New York, and Hispanic students 260 and 256 against 253. Across OECD countries, higher spending tracks math scores only up to about $75,000 per student over ages 6 to 15, and the United States spends more than $150,000.

question"Doesn't more money raise outcomes?"

Researchers find gains on average: a review of U.S. school-spending evaluations finds that $1,000 more per pupil, sustained for four years, raises test scores by about 0.03 standard deviations and college-going by about 2.8 percentage points, with smaller effects for advantaged students. That is $4,000 per pupil for three-hundredths of a standard deviation, while Utah and Idaho match or beat far higher spenders near $10,000. I hold an average of about $10,000 per child as the level the evidence supports today. It is not fixed: if the data shows better outcomes from spending more, the level moves up, and if it shows the same outcomes from spending less, the level moves down. Any change in either direction alters the state tax calculations.

question"If spending keeps rising and scores barely move, aren't schools just underfunded forever?"

"Underfunded" without a fixed definition is a slogan, not a budget. Real per-pupil spending has risen for decades while NAEP gains have been small. From FY1992 to FY2024 staff grew far faster than enrollment, and non-teaching staff grew fastest (data in "Where did the extra money go?"). Cross-state spending and NAEP results show a weak relationship once poverty and income are accounted for. Causal studies still find small average gains from sustained increases, on the order of a few hundredths of a standard deviation per $1,000 per pupil over several years. I do not claim the correlation is literally zero. I claim the United States already spends past the point where more money, without better use, reliably buys large score gains, which is why I hold about $10,000 per child as the working level and move it only with evidence (Education).

question"Where did the extra money go?"

From FY1992 to FY2024, public school students rose 17.2% while all staff rose 51.5%. Teachers rose 33.4% and non-teaching staff 69.0%. From FY1992 to FY2023, real spending per pupil rose 68% while real teacher pay fell 6%.

question"Are public schools just a jobs program for adults?"

NCES staffing data shows schools adding staff far faster than students. From fall 2000 to fall 2022, enrollment rose 5%, teachers 10%, principals and assistant principals 39%, and district administrative staff 95%, reaching about 189,000 full-time equivalent (about 386,000 with principals). The extra money has gone mostly to adults outside the classroom, and it hasn't reliably bought score gains (data in "Where did the extra money go?").

question"Why include charter schools?"

Charter schools received about 30%, or $7,147, less per pupil than district schools across 18 cities in 2019–20. CREDO's 2023 national study found charter students gained the equivalent of 16 more days of learning in reading and 6 in math per year than matched peers in district schools.

Rationale

Current spending is $16,526 per pupil (Census, FY2023), and a large part of the growth since 1992 went to non-teaching staff rather than teachers. States near $10,000 per pupil, such as Utah and Idaho, match or beat higher-spending states on NAEP.

In developed countries, differences among schools and teachers explain about 10% of achievement variance, and teachers alone about 1-8% (Detterman 2016). Most of the rest is tied to student characteristics, so more spending or moving students among existing schools has limited room to move outcomes, though individual teachers still matter: a one-standard-deviation better teacher in a single grade has been linked to about 1.3% higher earnings at age 28 (Chetty, Friedman, and Rockoff 2014).

Funding that follows the child to any public school lets families leave schools that fail them, and charter schools have delivered gains at lower cost. Keeping public money out of private schools keeps it publicly accountable.

In a realistic world, these programs phase out to bridge the gaps during the transition. That is why the phase-out window exists. Public K-12 schools collected about $981.8 billion in revenue in FY2023, or $19,871 per pupil: $443.3 billion state, $412.1 billion local, and $126.4 billion federal (12.9 percent). Spending was $18,905 per pupil in total expenditure and $16,526 per pupil in current expenditure. The largest federal K-12 programs that block grants would replace are IDEA Part B grants to states ($14.214 billion in FY2025) and Title I-A ($18.4 billion in FY2025).

The evidence that local control by itself improves results is weak. Funding matters from low baselines and in poorer districts — older finance-reform studies find a 10 percent rise in per-pupil spending across all twelve years of school linked to 0.27 more completed years of education and 7.25 percent higher adult wages, with larger effects for children from low-income families — but those wage effects do not license New York-level spend. The United States already spends past the point where more money, without better use, reliably buys large NAEP gains, which is why I hold about $10,000 per child as the working level and move it only with evidence. State funding of the per-child amount keeps the money guaranteed in poorer districts.

Governance and Curriculum

tldrIn short

I would abolish the federal Department of Education and put control local first. States should set minimum core-skill standards. Districts pick curriculum, limited to core skills and objective facts.

Position

I would abolish the federal Department of Education outright and end its programs or return them to the states. States that structured budgets around the transitional block grants are not cut off cold; their commitments are honored through the phase-out window. Control ought to be local first. Local districts should run schools, and the state guarantees the per-child funding, with state and federal law limited to child protections such as security of children's personal information and training and oversight for childcare, minimum core-skill standards, fraud and safety rules for public funds, and the protection of gifted programs and ability tracking (Gifted programs and tracking). Charter schools set their own curriculum and remain publicly accountable; each private school sets its own curriculum. Public-school curriculum standards exist and carve away biases of any sort; private schools set their own. For public schools, states set minimum standards for core skills, and local districts pick curriculum and materials. Teaching should focus on core skills (literacy, numeracy, science, and civic knowledge) and be entirely objective about facts and subject knowledge, not ideological, with no forced patriotism. No framework should be banned by viewpoint, but critical race theory, like any contested framework, may be taught only as one framework among others, never as settled fact, and no student or teacher may be required to affirm it (Cultural Marxism).

Gifted programs and ability tracking should stay. Every public school district ought to keep them, open to every student by merit, and entry may be decided by a test. No public school, district or charter, may cut, merge, or close a gifted program or an ability track to even out results between groups, and admission to a selective public school follows the test requirement in Tests in selective admissions. Fully private schools, which take no public money, set their own programs and admissions, under the same bar on immutable traits as a requirement or a quota (The merit rule). San Francisco moved eighth-grade algebra to high school to address racial gaps and brought it back ten years later, and Thomas Jefferson High School dropped its admissions test and cut offers to Asian-American students (Anti-meritocratic beliefs in practice).

My view: Gifted students should absolutely not be dragged down by less intelligent kids. Grouping students by what they can do lets each one move at the pace they can handle, and holding the fastest back to narrow a gap between groups punishes individuals for an average they did not set (From disparity to equal outcomes).

question"Doesn't tracking widen gaps between groups?"

Critics argue that it does, and San Francisco removed eighth-grade algebra on that ground. The gaps it aimed at persisted after the change (Anti-meritocratic beliefs in practice). A gap between groups is a fact to be explained, not a reason to hold back the student who is ahead. What helps the students behind is better teaching for them, not a lower ceiling for everyone else.

noteRealistic transition

In a realistic world, many systems — like taxes, like education, like all the systems that would be changing — would have some gradual period of change for most of these changes to happen. Here that means transitional block grants that phase out. That practical lag is a brief aside, not the main position.

Rationale

Education does not require a central ministry. Canada, Belgium, and Bosnia and Herzegovina have no national education ministry; Switzerland's cantons run its schools; Germany and Australia have national ministries but leave schools to their states. On PISA 2022, all five wealthy decentralized countries outscored the U.S. in math (Switzerland 508, Canada 497, Belgium 489, Australia 487, Germany 475, U.S. 465), while the U.S. scored 504 in reading, trailing only Canada (507) among them. Bosnia and Herzegovina did not take part in PISA 2022; in PISA 2018 it scored 406 in math and 403 in reading, far below the others. The comparison cannot show that decentralization causes these results, and Bosnia's scores show it does not guarantee them.

Local control would be chosen on grounds of accountability and closeness to the families affected, not on outcome evidence. Restricting curriculum to objective knowledge and skills would keep public schools from becoming instruments of ideology. Curriculum is already set locally in most of the country: local entities develop curriculum in 43 states, the state alone in one (Rhode Island), and both in five. Common Core has no official tracker. Roughly 30 states still use it in original, renamed, or revised form, and about 15 adopted and then repealed or replaced it.

Homeschooling

tldrIn short

Homeschooling should be fully allowed, with no school-performance testing. I want oversight to be tiered: clean households file an annual paper or virtual welfare check-in - no surprise entry. Complaint, prior-abuse history, or withdrawal-from-school after a CPS case triggers intensive unannounced visits. Position

Homeschooling should be fully allowed, with no testing requirement. Child-welfare oversight is tiered, not universal quarterly entry.

Clean households (no open complaint, no substantiated prior abuse in the household, no withdrawal from school that followed a CPS investigation or open case): one annual paper or virtual welfare check-in with a child-protection caseworker — confirmation the child is alive, reachable, and not reported missing or injured. no unannounced home entry on that track.

Triggered households (any of: a credible welfare complaint; substantiated prior abuse or neglect in the household; withdrawal from school after a CPS investigation or while a CPS case was open): intensive unannounced welfare visits by a child-protection caseworker, at random times, at a cadence the caseworker sets from risk (and at least as often as needed to clear or escalate the case). Visits check the child's welfare only, never schoolwork.

Homeschooling families would receive a fully refundable $1,000 federal income-tax credit per child per year (K-12 Funding).

Rationale

Homeschooling carries no testing requirement because no study shows that state testing or progress reviews improve homeschool outcomes. The most comprehensive review of the research (Kunzman and Gaither, 2020) finds the literature cannot isolate the effect of homeschooling, let alone the effect of regulation. Where data cannot show either way, the less regulatory option applies. Private schools publish yearly results because parents choose among schools and need comparable information; a homeschooling family is not choosing among providers, so the same reason does not apply.

The real risk is abuse hidden behind a withdrawal from school. Knox et al. (2014) is a clinical series of 28 child-torture cases; 8 of 17 school-age victims (47%) had been removed from school and labeled homeschooled. That is a child-safety signal worth acting on when a trigger is present — complaint, prior abuse, or CPS-linked withdrawal — not a warrant for surprise state entry into every clean homeschool four times a year. An n=28 clinical series does not justify universal quarterly unannounced visits. The answer is tiered welfare oversight: annual paper or virtual check-in for clean households. Intensive unannounced visits when a trigger fires — not school-performance testing, and not surveillance of every family that educates at home.

Infrastructure and Regulation

tldrIn short

Taxes should pay to keep core infrastructure safe and working. I would regulate businesses only for safety, serious environmental harm, worker exploitation, and consumer protection, and leave everything else to the market.

Position

Core infrastructure should be maintained to safe and functional standards with tax revenue. I believe business regulation is legitimate only for genuine safety, prevention of serious environmental harm (as defined in Energy & Environment), prevention of clear worker exploitation, and protection against anti-consumer practices. Outside these domains, the market ought to operate freely.

question"Who decides what counts as genuine safety or clear exploitation?"

The burden of proof rests on the regulation. A rule should show a real safety, environmental, worker-exploitation, or consumer-protection harm it addresses. Rules that cannot meet that test should be removed, and the market should govern everything else.

Rationale

Roads, bridges, and water systems are public goods that markets do not reliably provide, and their condition directly affects safety. Regulation beyond clear harms raises costs and is prone to capture by established firms. Dawson and Seater find that federal regulation accumulated since 1949 has significantly reduced U.S. output growth. Djankov and colleagues find that heavier entry regulation across 85 countries goes with more corruption and larger unofficial economies rather than better goods. Limiting regulation to defined harms keeps government effective where it is needed and out of the way elsewhere.
