Civilizational Nationalism

tldrIn short

I believe the country should put its own citizens first, keep strong borders, and admit immigrants who share Western values and assimilate. Those values, not ethnicity, define who fits.

Position

I give priority to cultural cohesion, high-trust social conditions, national sovereignty, and strong borders. I believe the state's primary duties are to its own citizens and to the long-term continuity of the political community. Immigration should be selective and oriented toward compatibility with the shared Western value structure, judged at the country level and through values vetting of every immigrant, and cultural assimilation is paramount. The border ought to be secured, with lethal force used when necessary. "Western" names a value structure, not an ethnicity, so compatibility is open to anyone of any ancestry who holds those values; being Muslim creates a presumption of failing the values test, rebuttable only by verified individual evidence of conduct and record.

question"Isn't nationalism just ethnic exclusion?"

No. The community is defined by shared values: individual negative rights, liberty, religious freedom with separation of church and state, the rule of law, civic responsibility, and economic opportunity. People of any ethnicity who hold those values are compatible, and being Muslim creates a rebuttable presumption of failing the values test. Compatibility judgments govern admission only; they impose no legal penalty for belief on anyone already within the community; the two civic disabilities for excluded ideologies are not penalties.

question"Does civilizational nationalism mean barring immigrants by religion?"

It can. Being Muslim creates a presumption against admission. So does coming from a country where Islamist movements govern or where survey data (Pew's 2013 survey, fieldwork 2008 to 2012) show majority support for state-enforced religious law. Either presumption can be rebutted only through strict individual values vetting on verified evidence of conduct and record, never on self-declaration alone (Islam and Extremism; Immigration and Borders).

question"Isn't the real problem that the country is changing demographically?"

The change is real. Pew Research Center's analysis of Census Bureau data found a record 53.3 million immigrants in January 2025, 15.8% of residents, before the number fell to 51.9 million, or 15.4%, by June 2025, the first decline since the 1960s. But a population share is not what I judge. "Western" names a value structure, not an ethnicity, so the question is whether newcomers hold those values and assimilate, whatever their ancestry (Western Values and Compatibility). On that measure there is work to do: Pew finds that 52% of immigrants ages 5 and older are proficient in English, 57% of those who arrived before 2000 against 47% of those who arrived in 2010 or later. That is why immigration should stay selective, with values vetting, and why every lawful non-citizen resident should have to pass the naturalization English and civics test within two years of admission (Immigration and Borders). A country of any ancestry that holds these values is still recognizably America; one that lost them would not be, whatever it looked like.

Rationale

The detailed immigration position is set out in Foreign Policy & International Relations, border enforcement in Border Security and Immigration Enforcement, and the definition of Western compatibility in Western Values and Compatibility. Assimilation and national identity are developed in Cultural & Social Issues, and English as the common civic language reflects Executive Order 14224, which designated English the official language of the United States in 2025.

Western Values and Compatibility

tldrIn short

To me, "Western" means a set of values, not an ethnicity or a place: rights, liberty, religious freedom with church-state separation, rule of law, civic responsibility, and opportunity. Anyone who holds them is compatible, whatever their ancestry. Being Muslim creates a presumption of failing it, which only verified individual evidence can overcome. Compatibility should be judged by country-level presumptions and individual vetting, govern admission only, and penalize no one's belief inside the community. The two civic disabilities for excluded ideologies are not penalties.

Position

"Western" names a value structure rather than an ethnicity or a geography: individual negative rights, liberty, religious freedom with separation of church and state, the rule of law, civic responsibility, and economic opportunity. Compatibility means holding those values. It is judged at the country level on the Questionable Compatibility list: grounds A and B carry a complete entry ban by nationality or origin; grounds C–I and security adds carry a presumption against entry, with no free immigration and only case-by-case admission — including places where Islamist movements govern or where survey data show majority support for state-enforced religious law — and at the individual level through values vetting of every immigrant (see Religion & Secularism and Foreign Policy & International Relations). Compatibility judgments govern admission to the political community; they impose no legal penalty for belief on anyone already within it; the two civic disabilities for excluded ideologies are not penalties (Role of Religion in Government).

My view: Being Muslim creates a presumption of failing the values test. A Muslim applicant is presumed incompatible with the shared Western value structure, and answers in an interview do not overcome that. Only verified evidence of conduct and record does.

question"Isn't judging cultures as incompatible just prejudice by another name?"

The test is about values, and it cuts in both directions. A person who holds these values is compatible whatever their ancestry. A person who rejects them is not, whatever their ancestry. Being Muslim creates a presumption of failing it, which verified individual evidence can overcome. I judge many cultures outside Europe and North America compatible. The judgment is aimed at the value structure and applied to individuals through vetting rather than assumed from origin alone, and it imposes no legal penalty for belief on anyone already in the political community.

question"How is compatibility with Western values tested in practice?"

By objective standards, not by opinion. A lawful non-citizen resident should have to pass the English and civics test used for naturalization within two years of admission, and removal should require a court finding by clear and convincing evidence. Opinions, beliefs, and speech should never be grounds for removal (Removal of Non-Citizen Residents); that rule governs U.S. residents, while foreign security removals follow Hostile conduct, removal, and entry. I would ban entry completely, by nationality or place of origin, from any place whose regime or dominant factions wage or celebrate attacks that target civilians for genocide or ethnic cleansing, or where reliable polling shows 5% or more openly supporting such attacks. The list is reviewed every year, and a place comes off only when open support stays below 1% in consecutive annual reviews and no authority there endorses such attacks. Further grounds for a ban are set out in the list (Hostile conduct, removal, and entry; Questionable Compatibility).

Rationale

The values in that list are the ones the rest of my framework already depends on. Individual negative rights and liberty are the core of Ethical and Philosophical Framework. Religious freedom with separation of church and state is set out in Religion & Secularism. The rule of law and civic responsibility appear in Authority & Civil Liberties, and economic opportunity in Domestic Policy & Governance. A person who holds these values is compatible whatever their ancestry. A person who rejects them is not, whatever their ancestry.

Because the test is about values, it cuts in both directions, and being Muslim creates a presumption of failing it. I judge many cultures outside Europe and North America compatible. Many legally immigrated Mexican and other Latin American communities are named as examples in Cultural & Social Issues. I judge cultures whose governing norms clash with these values, such as Islamist movements that seek state-enforced religious law, incompatible. That judgment is aimed at the value structure, and it is applied to individuals through vetting rather than assumed from origin alone.

Christianity's historical role is acknowledged as part of how these values developed in the West, while scripture is never mandated (Religion & Secularism). The standard is the values themselves, which a citizen can hold as a Christian, a member of another faith, or an atheist.

Shared values matter because trust is the medium of a rights-respecting order. Robert Putnam found that in the short run, residents of more ethnically diverse U.S. neighborhoods showed lower trust, even of their own group, and less community cooperation, and that successful immigrant societies overcome this by building shared identities. A 2020 meta-analysis of 1,001 estimates from 87 studies found a statistically significant but small negative relationship between ethnic diversity and social trust, stronger for trust in neighbors. A common value structure is the shared identity that lets a diverse society keep its trust.

Economic Orientation

tldrIn short

I support free markets, a flat tax with a large zero bracket, opt-in welfare, no business subsidies without opt-in, regulation that must justify itself, and balanced budgets with defense first. A flat, time-limited Sanctuary Levy of no more than 3%, on income above $75,000 single or $150,000 joint, should pay for the care of animals freed by the shutdown of animal agriculture and testing.

Position

I want a free-market-leaning economy, with low and simple taxation built on a flat income tax with a large zero bracket. I reject mandatory welfare and large-scale redistribution in favor of an opt-in model. Subsidies to any business or industry should require taxpayer opt-in, industrial policy ought to be minimal and tightly justified, and the burden of proof should rest on every regulation rather than on the people regulated. Budgets should be balanced, with defense the top spending priority. There are two time-limited exceptions to the single flat rate. The first is a 2-point transition surcharge through 2041, all of it to the debt (Taxation). The second is a flat Sanctuary Levy of no more than 3%, on income above $75,000 single or $150,000 joint, for the care of animals freed by the shutdown of animal agriculture and testing. It ends when the sanctuary population falls below 1% of its starting animal units and its bonds are repaid (Sanctuary Plan).

question"Without mandatory welfare, what happens to people in need?"

The welfare model is opt-in, and emergency protections remain, such as emergency shelters and emergency medical care set out in the detailed domestic policy section. What I reject is compulsory redistribution as the default, not help for people in genuine emergencies. The rule protects two people at once: the worker whose wages would otherwise fund programs they never chose, and the person in need, whose floor is funded separately so no opt-in shortfall can remove it (Welfare and Entitlements). If the compulsory part is too small, the gap falls to voluntary giving, which anyone may add to; if it is too large, the excess is taken by force every year from people who did nothing wrong. Some people will get less from the state than today's programs give, and everyone already on Social Security and Medicare is grandfathered for life.

question"Isn't a flat income tax regressive?"

The flat rate would sit on top of a large zero bracket, so the lowest earners would pay no income tax at all, and everyone above it would pay the same rate on income past that bracket. Taxes would stay low and simple and fund only essential functions. In dollar terms the largest cuts go to the highest earners, whose top federal rate falls from today's 37% to the flat 20% (22% through 2041 with the transition surcharge). I accept that on the principle of one rate for everyone (Taxation).

question"Don't billionaires own almost everything while wages stagnate?"

No on both counts as usually stated. The Federal Reserve's Distributional Financial Accounts show high concentration at the top, but not "billionaires own all wealth": the top 0.1% holds on the order of about 14% of household wealth in recent DFA releases, and the rest of the distribution still holds the majority. Real average hourly earnings have risen over the long run, with a sharp real dip in the 2021–22 inflation spike and a recovery since (BLS). Housing and wealth are not owned only by billionaires. I reject wealth taxes and large-scale redistribution anyway on rights grounds (Taxation). The myth correction matters so the debate is about principles, not invented shares. Across countries, more billionaires per person goes with less poverty, not more (Economic System). How the US tax system ranks on progressivity, and what the "billionaires pay 8.2%" figure measures, is in Taxation. Federal Reserve – DFA: Distribution of Household Wealth · BLS – Real average hourly earnings

question"Why is defense the top spending priority?"

The state exists first to protect negative rights and the security of the community that sustains them, including defense against external threats (Political Philosophy). Defense is that core function, so it comes first in a balanced budget.

question"Does economic nationalism mean heavy tariffs?"

No. I treat tariffs as good in theory, as leverage and as protection for strategic industries, and questionable in practice, because studies of the 2018 tariffs found they were passed almost entirely into prices paid by American buyers. American industry should be supported mainly through full expensing, long-run payroll tax cuts as the transition levy steps down, and regulatory relief, and through trade deals in which the United States also exports to each partner (Trade and Economics).

Rationale

The tax, welfare, and regulatory positions are set out in Domestic Policy & Governance, including Welfare and Entitlements, Infrastructure and Regulation, and Fiscal Policy. The no-subsidy rule is applied to energy in Energy & Environment. The scale of business subsidy the rule removes is visible in agriculture. Direct government payments to U.S. farm producers totaled about $54 billion in 2020, measured in 2024 dollars, much of it pandemic assistance, according to USDA's Economic Research Service.

Cultural Stance

tldrIn short

I see traditional family life as the preferred default but not a legal mandate. Merit should replace identity politics, speech should be near-absolute, and the government ought to condemn Nazism, communism, socialism, and Islam. Private belief is left alone, but holding those doctrines disqualifies a person from sworn authority roles under the values test.

Position

I prefer the nuclear family and traditional social patterns as the default, without legal mandates for private life; same-sex marriage should be legal, and divorce allowed. I oppose identity politics and DEI frameworks, and people should be treated on merit. Free speech ought to be near-absolute, with narrow exceptions for true threats and incitement to imminent violence. Nazism, communism, socialism, and Islam should be condemned in the government's official voice, with no official platforms, public funding, or public honors. All four are Civilizational Risks at the same level (Terrorism and Domestic Extremism). The criminal law should restrict them only as it restricts any other ideology; as excluded ideologies, Nazism, communism, socialism, and Islam also carry two civic disabilities, which are not penalties.

question"How can the government condemn an ideology and still protect free speech?"

Condemnation is the government speaking for itself. It is not a restriction on anyone else. Citizens keep full freedom to hold and express these views. What they do not get is official endorsement, public funding, or public honors.

question"Does cultural assimilation mean banning other languages?"

No. English is the common language of civic life, and immigrants are expected to learn it as part of joining the shared Western value structure, but private use of any language is free (Language and Assimilation).

Rationale

Order and Security

I would meet crime hard: a mandatory death penalty for murder (including attempted murder), violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, human trafficking, large-scale distribution of hard drugs, and giving or selling any hard drug to a minor. forfeiture for repeat offenders; and decisive action against cartels. Position

My orientation on crime is hardline. Murder (including attempted murder), violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, human trafficking, large-scale distribution of hard drugs, and giving or selling any hard drug to a minor carry a mandatory death penalty under a strict evidentiary standard. Repeat offenders who reach three marks forfeit the right to life, with the victim choosing execution, experimentation, or life with labor (a judge decides when victims disagree or the victim is an animal; where the victim has died, experimentation is the default unless the victim's close family or friends step forward and choose, and it stays the default if they disagree). Sentences for first and second offenses should be greatly increased in length, every custodial sentence served as compelled labor, idle permanent incarceration abolished, and cartels and chronic rights violators should face decisive action. I accept substantial state coercive power as necessary to suppress recurrent predation and disorder, with independent oversight of police. Islam and communism should be treated as civilizational and security threats, monitored only through public data and lawful process, with no legal penalty for belief; the two civic disabilities for excluded ideologies are not penalties.

The Future and Technology

Relation to Other Political Traditions

tldrIn short

I share the classical liberal and libertarian commitment to rights, property, and markets, but I accept far more force against criminals and at the border. I share national conservatism's emphasis on fiscal restraint, a strong military, assimilation, and patriotism, but I base culture on values rather than heritage and keep religion out of government. Grounding rights in sentience sets me apart from all three.

Position

I don't file my views under an existing label. I agree with classical liberalism and libertarianism on negative rights, private property, free markets, and a state limited to protecting people from force and fraud (Ethical and Philosophical Framework; Domestic Policy & Governance). I part from libertarianism in accepting far more coercive power against those who violate rights: the mandatory death penalty (Criminal Justice & Security), strict borders with lethal force when necessary (Foreign Policy & International Relations), and decisive force against organized threats. National conservatism, as most people use the term, means being fiscally conservative, socially traditional, and pro-military, with an emphasis on cultural cohesion and national identity. I share that commitment to fiscal restraint, a strong military, assimilation, patriotism, and national interest in trade (Cultural & Social Issues; Foreign Policy & International Relations). I part from it in three places: I ground the shared culture in a structure of values open to people of any ethnicity rather than in religious or ancestral heritage; church and state ought to stay separate and churches ought to be taxed (Religion & Secularism); and the state ought to recognize same-sex marriage (Cultural & Social Issues). What sets me apart from all three is sentientism: rights rest on sentience rather than species, which extends protection to animals and to any truly sentient artificial being (Ethical and Philosophical Framework).

question"Isn't this just national conservatism with a libertarian economic policy?"

No. The movement's own statement of principles calls for public life rooted in Christianity in nations with a Christian majority and for public support of the traditional family. The government ought to keep church and state separate, tax churches, and recognize same-sex marriage. Grounding rights in sentience, meanwhile, has no counterpart in any of the three traditions.

Rationale

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes liberalism as a tradition in which liberals accord liberty primacy as a political value, with a presumption in favor of liberty that puts the burden of justification on anyone who would restrict it. That is the same burden I place on regulation (Domestic Policy & Governance). The Encyclopedia describes libertarianism as a family of views built on strong rights of self-ownership and property and a correspondingly minimal state. In everyday usage, national conservatism names a fiscally conservative, socially traditional, pro-military outlook centered on cultural cohesion and national identity, and that everyday sense is the one I mostly share. The national conservative movement's formal Statement of Principles, published in 2022, goes further. It calls for public life rooted in Christianity in nations with a Christian majority and for public support of the traditional family, which is where I part ways. Measured against those definitions, my economic and rights positions sit closest to classical liberalism, my security and immigration positions closest to national conservatism, and my religious and sentientist positions apart from all three.

Communism

tldrIn short

Communism is the program of abolishing private property, with one party holding a monopoly of power. I condemn it because every state that built it banned rival parties, ran forced-labor camps, and caused famines and mass killings that cost millions of lives. Today it is carried mainly through cultural Marxism.

Position

I judge communism by its program and its record. Marx and Engels described the goal as an association in which "the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all." They also wrote that the theory "may be summed up in the single sentence: Abolition of private property," to be achieved at first "by means of despotic inroads on the rights of property." Every state that acted on this program in the twentieth century gave one party a monopoly of power. It confiscated land and businesses, punished political opponents, and directed labor by force. The worst cases include the Soviet Gulag, the Soviet famine of 1931–34 including the Holodomor in Ukraine, China's Great Leap Forward famine, and the Khmer Rouge regime in Cambodia. These are violations of the right to life, liberty, and property on a scale few other systems have matched. Communism is condemned at the same severity as Nazism, socialism, and Islam, without making belief in it a crime, and its organized violent groups treated as threats (Terrorism and Domestic Extremism).

The United States ought to treat communism the way it treats Nazism in everything the government does itself. State history standards ought to teach the crimes of communist regimes alongside the Holocaust, the nation ought to remember their victims, and no communist figure or regime ought to receive a public honor, name, statue, holiday, or public funding, just as no Nazi one does. Existing public honors, names, statues, and holidays for communist figures or regimes ought to be removed, as a Nazi one would not be left standing (Cultural Stance). That is the government speaking for itself. Private citizens keep their full right to praise Lenin or Mao, as they keep the right to praise Hitler (Free Speech and Censorship).

question"Wasn't that not real communism, only distortions by Stalin, Mao, and Pol Pot?"

I judge the program and its results. The program itself called for abolishing private property and for "despotic inroads" to do it. The abuses followed from giving a state unchecked power over all property and labor, in countries with very different cultures and leaders. I judge a plan that has failed the same way in every state that tried it by that record.

question"Didn't communist states also reduce illiteracy, expand health care, and industrialize?"

Whatever gains in literacy, health care, and heavy industry occurred, I don't accept trading some people's lives and liberty for others' gains, and market economies achieved these gains without camps or famine (Economic System). China's own reversal after the Great Leap, returning private plots and tools to peasants, is a concession from inside the system.

question"Death-toll figures are disputed. Aren't critics inflating them?"

While critics claim the totals are inflated, the dispute is over how many tens of millions died, not whether they did. The widest total, the 85 to 100 million in The Black Book of Communism, was contested even by two of its own authors, so I set it beside the lower counts (Rationale). For the Great Leap famine, early demographic estimates were 16.5 to 23 million excess deaths, and later studies found 23 to 30 million. Western scholars put Gulag deaths from 1918 to 1956 at 1.2 to 1.7 million. Even the lowest figures describe deaths caused by state policy on a scale that settles the moral question.

question"If communism is condemned, why not ban the party?"

Because the state ought to punish harm, not belief. The two civic disabilities for excluded ideologies are not penalties. Advocacy should be protected up to true threats and incitement to imminent violence, the same rule that applies to every ideology (Free Speech and Censorship). Punishing belief would copy the method being condemned.

question"If communism is treated like Nazism, why not make glorifying it a crime, as some European countries do?"

Because in the United States glorifying Nazism is not a crime either, and I keep it that way. The law restricts only true threats and incitement to imminent violence, for every ideology (Free Speech and Censorship). Equal treatment applies to what the government itself does: what public schools teach, which victims the nation remembers, and whom it names, honors, or funds. The Supreme Court has held that a government choosing which permanent monuments to place on public land is speaking for itself, which the Free Speech Clause does not restrict, so none of this needs a new speech law.

question"Isn't equating them wrong? Nazism aimed at extermination, while communism's deaths came from a failed utopian plan."

Defenders of the distinction point to intent: Nazism made killing a goal, while communism promised liberation. The record answers them. Soviet authorities blacklisted Ukrainian villages from food and barred starving peasants from leaving to find it, and a UN-backed tribunal convicted the Khmer Rouge's surviving leaders of genocide. A victim's rights are violated the same whether the regime that starved or shot them promised liberation or not, and the state's treatment of the two follows the victims, not the slogans.

Rationale

I rest on individual negative rights to life, liberty, and property (Political Philosophy). Communist states removed all three as a matter of system design. The 1977 Soviet Constitution named the Communist Party "the leading and guiding force of Soviet society and the nucleus of its political system," which left no lawful route for opposition. Forced collectivization began in 1929. Peasants were made to give up land and property, and resisters and "kulaks" were deported. Britannica reports that between 1931 and 1934 at least 5 million people died of hunger across the USSR, including at least 3.9 million Ukrainians, and that Ukrainian villages were blacklisted from food and peasants were barred from leaving to seek it. Britannica puts the Gulag at about 5 million prisoners by 1936; the NKVD's own records, opened in the 1990s, are lower, about 1.2 million in its camps and labor colonies at the end of 1936, not counting prisons and deported "special settlers." Western scholars estimate 1.2 to 1.7 million Gulag deaths from 1918 to 1956.

In China, the Great Leap Forward caused what Vaclav Smil called the largest famine in human history. Demographic reconstructions put the toll at 23 to 30 million excess deaths, and Smil wrote that it was "largely a manmade catastrophe." Britannica gives a lower estimate of about 20 million. In Cambodia, Yale's Cambodian Genocide Program estimates that about 1.7 million people, 21% of the population, died under the Khmer Rouge from 1975 to 1979. A UN-backed tribunal later convicted the regime's surviving leaders of genocide and crimes against humanity.

The widest estimate comes from the authors of The Black Book of Communism, who put the cumulative toll of communist rule at between 85 and 100 million. The breakdown reported at publication in 1997 gave China 45 to 72 million and the Soviet Union about 20 million. Critics, including two of the book's own contributors, Nicolas Werth and Jean-Louis Margolin, objected to its editor's "obsession" with reaching a hundred million dead, and Werth counted 15 million Soviet victims. The dispute is over the size of the total, not its order: Werth's 15 million Soviet victims and the Great Leap's 23 to 30 million already come to 38 to 45 million before Cambodia, North Korea or anywhere else is counted.

Cultural Marxism

tldrIn short

Cultural Marxism is the critical theory of the Frankfurt School and its offshoots, which recasts class struggle as oppressed groups against oppressors and carries it into culture and education. I condemn it because it assigns a person's standing by group instead of by merit or conduct.

Position

By cultural Marxism I mean a specific strand of Marxist thought: the critical theory of the Frankfurt School and the broader strands of critical theory that grew from it, such as critical theory applied to race, sex, and culture, which recast Marx's class struggle as a struggle between oppressor and oppressed groups and carry it into culture, institutions, and education. The lineage is documented in the school's own texts. Max Horkheimer's 1937 essay "Traditional and Critical Theory" defined the program against ordinary scholarship: critical theory "never aims simply at an increase of knowledge as such," but at "emancipation from slavery." The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy places the Frankfurt School in "the Western European Marxist tradition" and lists feminist theory, critical race theory, queer theory, and postcolonial theory as broader strands of critical theory. Herbert Marcuse's 1965 essay "Repressive Tolerance" carried the program into speech and schooling: "Liberating tolerance, then, would mean intolerance against movements from the Right and toleration of movements from the Left," including "the withdrawal of toleration of speech and assembly" from groups he opposed and "new and rigid restrictions on teachings and practices in the educational institutions." I hold this to be communism carried into culture. It sorts people into oppressor and oppressed groups and treats the freedom of those labeled oppressors as something to withdraw, the same assignment of standing by group that I reject in every excluded ideology. My claim is about these published ideas and their spread, never about anyone's ancestry or a secret plot.

I answer it with the merit rule, tests in selective admissions, and gifted programs, never with a penalty for belief.

question"Isn't 'cultural Marxism' a conspiracy-theory label?"

Critics argue that it is. The philosopher Joan Braune calls it "an antisemitic conspiracy theory" in which "the Jewish Marxists of the Frankfurt School implemented a slow takeover of 'culture.'" I reject that theory as she describes it: I claim no plot and no secret coordination, and the ancestry of any Frankfurt School member has nothing to do with my judgment. My claim is about published ideas and their spread, and both are documented. The lineage runs from Horkheimer's 1937 program of critical theory, through Marcuse's 1965 call to withdraw toleration from the Right and to restrict teaching in schools, to the broader strands of critical theory the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy lists, and its spread is measured in the survey and faculty data. Marxist scholars use the term for their own tradition: the UCLA critical theorist Douglas Kellner wrote that cultural studies "had predecessors in a number of currents of cultural Marxism," naming Georg Lukacs, Antonio Gramsci, Ernst Bloch, Walter Benjamin, and T.W. Adorno among others. A name that Marxist scholars give their own tradition is not a slur when I apply it to the same tradition (Cultural Marxism).

question"Isn't intersectionality only a descriptive legal tool for overlapping discrimination?"

Its defenders say it is. Crenshaw called it "a provisional concept" and built it from employment cases in which courts would not hear Black women's claims, and in that use it asks a court to hear a claim it was dismissing. I hold every individual claim of discrimination open to a court, whatever its source. The concept did not stay there, and Crenshaw did not mean it to. The 1989 article ends on "restructuring and remaking the world where necessary" and on "collectively challenging the hierarchy." "Mapping the Margins" defends identity politics against its critics and asks for finer group lines, not fewer: "The problem with identity politics is not that it fails to transcend difference, as some critics charge, but rather the opposite—that it frequently conflates or ignores intragroup differences." A framework that names, stacks, and ranks groups so that they can be empowered as groups is a program, not a description. The record shows where the program leads: racial preferences at Harvard and UNC struck down by the Supreme Court, test-free admissions at Thomas Jefferson that cut offers to Asian-American students, and eighth-grade algebra removed in San Francisco and restored ten years later (Anti-meritocratic beliefs in practice). Each came from the outcome-by-group logic of equal outcomes, not from a court's need to hear one plaintiff (Intersectionality).

Rationale

exampleIntersectionality, equal outcomes, merit, and acceptance among the young

Intersectionality. The term comes from the legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw. In "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" (1989) she argued that antidiscrimination law worked from a "single-axis framework" that erased claims by Black women, who can be harmed by race discrimination, sex discrimination, or both at once: "Discrimination, like traffic through an intersection, may flow in one direction, and it may flow in another." The same article went past that legal point. It pictured a basement holding everyone "disadvantaged on the basis of race, sex, class, sexual preference, age and/or physical ability," "stacked—feet standing on shoulders—with those on the bottom being disadvantaged by the full array of factors," and it called for "collectively challenging the hierarchy" and for "restructuring and remaking the world where necessary," beginning with those at the bottom. In "Mapping the Margins" (1991) Crenshaw wrote that the work "arises out of and is inspired by two emerging scholarly discourses," the first being "critical race theory," and Encyclopaedia Britannica describes that movement's "immediate precursor" as critical legal studies, "an offshoot of Marxist-oriented critical theory." I hold that intersectionality is the oppressor-versus-oppressed sorting of cultural Marxism with the groups stacked: it ranks a person's standing and claims by how many disadvantaged groups the person belongs to, and the person's own conduct drops out of the ranking.

From disparity to equal outcomes. Once every gap between groups is read as proof of oppression, the remedy becomes equal outcomes by group. Ibram X. Kendi states the logic plainly: "A racist policy is any measure that produces or sustains racial inequity between racial groups," "There is no such thing as a nonracist or race-neutral policy," and "The only remedy to racist discrimination is antiracist discrimination. The only remedy to past discrimination is present discrimination." Britannica records the central criticism of critical race theory as "interpreting racial inequity as proof of institutional racism." Equalizing outcomes by group requires handing out admissions, hiring, pay, and standing by group membership instead of by individual merit or conduct. That is the same assignment of standing by group that the excluded-ideologies test rejects, and it is why I hold equality of outcome to be discriminatory by definition (Race and Diversity). A gap between groups is a fact to be explained, not proof of who wronged whom, and measured achievement gaps that persist at equal family income are set out in Intelligence, Race, and Crime.

Anti-meritocratic beliefs in practice. The same logic is turned against merit itself. MIT's dean of admissions noted in 2022 that "the entire concept of 'merit'" "has been critiqued as merely laundering intergenerational privilege," and Kendi counts "standardized tests that produce racial inequities" among the things racist power calls "race neutral." Where that belief governed, it raised the bar for some applicants to lower it for others. In Students for Fair Admissions (2023) the Supreme Court's opinion noted that at Harvard an African American applicant in the fourth-lowest academic decile had a higher chance of admission (12.8%) than an Asian American applicant in the top decile (12.7%), and that at UNC, in the second academic decile from the top, 83% of Black applicants were admitted against 58% of white and 47% of Asian applicants. At Thomas Jefferson High School for Science and Technology in Virginia, after a board member demanded that the district "be explicit in how we are going to address the underrepresentation of [b]lack and Hispanic students," the board in December 2020 adopted an admissions policy that no longer relies on standardized tests. Asian Americans' share of offers fell from between 65% and 75% to 54.36%, and although the entering class grew by 64 seats, they were offered 56 fewer. The Fourth Circuit upheld the policy, and the Supreme Court declined review in 2024 over a dissent by Justice Alito, joined by Justice Thomas: "What the Fourth Circuit majority held, in essence, is that intentional racial discrimination is constitutional so long as it is not too severe." San Francisco's school board moved Algebra 1 out of eighth grade in 2014 to address racial gaps in advanced math. Stanford researchers later found the large racial and ethnic gaps in advanced math enrollment persisted, and in February 2024 the board voted 6 to 1 to bring the course back. The evidence on merit runs the other way. Dartmouth's faculty found that SAT scores alone explained about 22% of the variation in first-year grades and high school GPA alone 9%, and Dartmouth restored its testing requirement in 2024. MIT restored its own in 2022, finding that tests help it identify disadvantaged students who could not otherwise show readiness. Across Ivy-Plus colleges, Opportunity Insights found that students with a 1600 SAT earned first-year grades 0.43 points higher than students with a 1200, and that the scores show no calibration bias: students from less advantaged backgrounds do not outperform peers from more advantaged backgrounds with similar scores. A test that predicts equally well for every group measures merit, not privilege. The policy that follows is the merit rule, with tests in selective admissions and gifted programs and tracking.

Acceptance among the young and in universities. In the Cato Institute's March 2025 survey, fielded by YouGov, 62% of Americans aged 18 to 29 held a favorable view of socialism and 34% a favorable view of communism, against 43% and 14% of all adults. In the Victims of Communism Memorial Foundation's 2020 survey, also fielded by YouGov and the latest edition of its annual report I could verify, 30% of Gen Z, then aged 16 to 23, viewed Marxism favorably and 28% communism, against 6% and 3% of the Silent Generation. Gallup's figures on young adults and socialism are in Public Opinion Trends. The faculty who teach them lean overwhelmingly one way. The Higher Education Research Institute's 2016–17 survey of 20,771 full-time undergraduate teaching faculty at 143 four-year institutions found 48.3% liberal and 11.6% far left, against 11.7% conservative and 0.4% far right. The liberal share was 48.8% in 2013–14 and 36.8% in 1989–90. Mitchell Langbert's study of 8,688 tenure-track, Ph.D.-holding professors at 51 of the top 66 liberal arts colleges found a Democratic-to-Republican registration ratio of 10.4 to 1, or 12.7 to 1 without West Point and Annapolis, and 39% of the colleges had no Republican faculty at all. Critics note that these surveys measure favorable views of labels that respondents define for themselves. Cato says its poll did not define socialism, and party registration is not critical theory. The label is the point: a third of young adults no longer treat communism as disqualifying, and the faculty who form them register Democratic at more than six times the 1.6-to-1 ratio Langbert found among Americans with some graduate education.

Nazism

tldrIn short

Nazism ranked human beings by supposed race and used the state to strip, expel, and kill those it ranked lowest. I condemn it because it assigned rights by group identity instead of to every sentient individual, and murdered six million Jews and millions of others.

Position

Nazism is incompatible with my framework at its root. Its core claim was a racial hierarchy in which "Aryan" Germans were superior and Jews and others were inferior. It turned that claim into law, took total control of the state, and then pursued conquest and extermination. I ground rights in sentience, which every person has equally, so any doctrine that assigns rights by race, ancestry, or disability contradicts my framework directly (Animal Rights and Sentientism). Nazism is condemned at the same severity as communism, socialism, and Islam, without making belief in it a crime, and its organized violent groups treated as threats (Terrorism and Domestic Extremism).

question"Doesn't your own nationalism and civilizational language resemble it?"

No, and the difference is the test itself. Nazism assigned worth by blood. I judge compatibility by values that anyone of any ancestry can hold, and I protect every sentient individual's rights, including non-citizens' within U.S. borders (Western Values and Compatibility). It does not penalize belief and leaves removal to courts with due process, which is the opposite of the Nazi method.

question"Why give Nazism its own topic when no serious movement defends it?"

Because it is the clearest case for testing a principle. Its racial laws, its "euthanasia" killings, and its genocide show what happens when a state decides some sentient beings have no rights. Naming the specific rules that made this possible shows why I put rights before the state, the majority, and the group.

Rationale

Each stage of Nazi rule removed rights I protect. The Reichstag Fire Decree of February 1933 suspended constitutional protections for personal liberty, free expression, the press, assembly, and the privacy of mail and telephone, and let the regime jail opponents without charge. The Nuremberg Race Laws of 1935 limited citizenship to those "of German or related blood." They banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and other Germans and were extended to Roma and Black people. These laws punished identity, not conduct.

The "euthanasia" program began in 1939 and targeted institutionalized disabled people as "life unworthy of life." By T4's own count, 70,273 people were killed at six gassing centers from January 1940 to August 1941, and historians estimate about 250,000 deaths across all phases. The U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum notes that T4's gas chambers and staff were later used for the killing centers of the Holocaust. The museum counts six million Jews murdered, about 2.7 million of them at killing centers and about 2 million in mass shootings. It also counts around 3.3 million Soviet prisoners of war, around 1.8 million non-Jewish Poles, 250,000 to 500,000 Roma, and 250,000 to 300,000 disabled people. Lebensraum ("living space") policy planned the removal of Slavs and Jews from eastern Europe. Pre-invasion guidelines stated that "many tens of millions of people in this territory will become superfluous and will have to die or migrate to Siberia."

The lesson I draw is that rights ought to belong to every sentient individual and should not depend on the state, a majority, or a group's ranking. Nazism reversed every part of that rule.
