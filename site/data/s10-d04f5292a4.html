Core Principles

I strongly support technological advancement and transhumanism, constrained only by sentient rights. I prefer free-market and private innovation, with the government's role limited to regulation against existential threats, rights violations, and national security. Privacy and data rules should be opt-in, and animal testing should be redirected to forfeited rights holders.

AI and Automation

tldrIn short

I'm pro-AI, but I treat it as a serious existential risk, so government should regulate it and prepare a defensive AGI. I reject a citizen dividend. Workers displaced by automation are covered by the earnings-only top-up. If AI displaces work on a large scale, I believe the answer is broad voluntary ownership of AI capital, falling prices, and the existing safety floor scaled to measured displacement, funded by the same flat tax system.

Position

AI should be monitored and regulated as part of the government's role in protecting citizens' rights and security, limited to existential and rights-violation risks. If AI is observed becoming an existential threat, a risk serious enough to prepare for, the government ought to focus on regulating its development and preparing to defend against it. I oppose a citizen dividend. Workers displaced by automation into lower-paid work should be covered by the earnings-only top-up, and those who cannot work by the layered safety floor (Domestic Policy). I am pro-AI, and I believe a defensive AGI will likely be needed to combat any dangerous AGI that emerges. AI's existential and national-security risks are a federal matter, so national rules on them should preempt conflicting state laws, set by Congress in statute rather than by executive order. States should keep their ordinary fraud and consumer-protection law, which applies to AI like anything else. Every adult is enrolled by default in a low-cost private index account, with a free opt-out and no taxpayer seed. Capital gains should stay at the preferential flat rate, rising to the full flat rate under the displacement trigger, with the deferral gap closed by carryover basis (Taxation; carryover basis). The trigger is four consecutive quarters with the BLS labor share at least 3 points below its ten-year average and the prime-age employment-to-population ratio at least 2 points below its level three years earlier; capital income first moves from 15% to the 20% flat rate (17% to 22% through 2041 with the transition surcharge), then a surcharge of up to 5 points after eight quarters, never above 25% including the transition surcharge and the Sanctuary Levy while they run, and never on wealth or unrealized gains; each step reverses when the measures recover for four quarters.

The plan handles gradual or partial displacement. Fast, near-total displacement would force a choice about taxing capital beyond "minimal."

question"Does a pro-AI stance conflict with treating AI as an existential threat?"

No. AI offers enormous benefits and also serious risks. Supporting development while regulating against existential danger and building defensive capability addresses both.

question"What happens to workers displaced by automation?"

I oppose a citizen dividend. A displaced worker who takes lower-paid work draws the earnings-only top-up on what they earn, and it phases out with no cliff as earnings recover. Those who cannot work are covered by the layered safety floor. Owner income from federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum helps fund the top-up rather than being paid out (Welfare and Entitlements).

question"How is the safety floor funded if AI replaces most jobs and no one has wage income?"

If AI does most of the work, the money doesn't vanish. It goes to the people who own the AI, as profits, dividends, and capital gains. That income would be taxed under the flat tax, at the preferential flat rate on capital gains and dividends, which would rise to the full flat rate if displacement shows up in the data, and business income would still be taxed only once (Taxation). The government also earns money from federal land, minerals, energy, and spectrum, and that money would go first to the earnings-only top-up. As AI and data centers use more spectrum and energy, that money grows. The more households own a share of AI and other businesses, the less they rely on a paycheck, and the less the floor has to cover. Owners also need people to have money, because a business whose customers have no income has no one to sell to.

question"Why not a universal basic income?"

A UBI pays everyone regardless of need, which makes it far more expensive than a floor for those who cannot support themselves, and it requires compulsory redistribution well beyond the minimal floor. The government ought to keep the floor targeted. It phases support out gradually as earnings rise, so there is no cliff, and it prefers that people own capital rather than depend on a state check. The same reasoning is why owner income is not paid out as a citizen dividend (Economic System).

question"Won't AI owners just hoard the gains?"

Profits that are spent, invested, or paid out are taxed under the flat system. Profits that sit idle earn nothing, so owners have strong reasons to reinvest them or pay them out. Ownership is also open to anyone: shares in AI companies trade publicly, and low-cost index funds let any household own a slice of the whole market. My answer to concentration is wider voluntary ownership, not seizing the firms. It concedes one real risk: if ownership stays highly concentrated and wages fall far, funding even a minimal floor could require a profit tax larger than "minimal" implies.

question"What if AI replaces nearly all labor?"

Then something has to replace wages, and every option has a cost to my framework. The first is a large income funded by taxing the output of AI and capital, through a UBI or a much larger floor. It is the only option that reliably works, but it means redistribution far beyond the minimal floor. The second is a public ownership fund. Alaska's Permanent Fund pays citizens directly, $1,000 per person in 2025. Norway's fund, worth 22,683 billion kroner in mid-2026, pays nothing to citizens and instead helps fund the government budget. The state must first acquire the stake through taxes or takings, which clashes with my property rules. The third is near-free goods through falling prices. This is speculative, and land and housing are hard to make cheaper. The fourth is new human work in care, the trades, in-person services, and creative fields. Autor's research shows this has happened in past waves of automation, but it is not guaranteed. My choice is the first option, and only when the BLS trigger shows a real takeover of work. Until then, minimal taxes remain the default.

question"Isn't building a defensive AGI itself a risk?"

Yes, and it is accepted because an undefended country facing a dangerous AGI is worse. Defensive AGI is built under the same existential-risk regulation as any other system, under government supervision.

Rationale

Protecting citizens from catastrophic threats is a core government function, and advanced AI may become one. In a 2023 survey of 2,778 AI researchers, the median estimate of the chance of extremely bad outcomes such as human extinction was 5%. Federal policy currently aims to promote American AI leadership. A defensive capability ensures that if dangerous AI emerges, it can be countered. Measured job losses from automation have so far been modest (0.2 percentage points of employment per added robot per 1,000 workers), which is why displacement is handled through the existing earnings-only top-up rather than a separate automation payment.

Some economists argue AI is different from past automation. The IMF estimates that almost 40% of global employment, and about 60% of jobs in advanced economies, is exposed to AI, with roughly half of exposed jobs likely to benefit and half facing lower labor demand. Past automation did not end work. David Autor found that automation "complements labor, raises output in ways that leads to higher demand for labor," and that commentators "tend to overstate the extent of machine substitution." Acemoglu and Restrepo show that automation displaces workers from existing tasks, while new tasks reinstate them. They also found that the slower U.S. employment growth of the past three decades reflects faster displacement and weaker reinstatement. Labor's share of income has already been falling. BLS reports that the labor share of nonfarm business output reached a record low of 52.8% in the second quarter of 2026. Karabarbounis and Neiman document a global decline of about 5 percentage points since the 1970s, about half of it explained by cheaper investment goods such as computers. Autor and coauthors link much of the U.S. decline to sales shifting toward "superstar firms" with low labor shares. Early evidence on AI itself is mixed. Using ADP payroll data, Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen find no evidence of widespread job displacement so far. But employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations stood 19% below where it would have been had it kept pace with less-exposed peers. Acemoglu estimates that AI will raise total factor productivity by no more than about 0.66% over ten years. Whether AI creates enough new tasks is unknown. So the government ought to scale its response to displacement as it's measured, not to forecasts.

Three features carry the response. First, ownership. Anyone may own AI capital through public shares and index funds, and a birth-to-adulthood account already exists in law. Trump Accounts, created by the Working Families Tax Cuts, let parents, relatives, employers, and charities invest for a child in low-cost U.S. index funds. Alaska's Permanent Fund shows that resource wealth can be held for residents over decades, paying $1,000 per person in 2025. I support voluntary contributions to such accounts but don't adopt a paid-out citizen dividend. Second, prices. Automation lowers the cost of what it produces, so a fixed income buys more, which supports demand even as some jobs shrink. The BLS price index for televisions fell 94% from December 1997 to August 2015, and William Nordhaus estimated that the cost of computation fell by a factor of trillions between 1850 and 2006. Third, the floor. The existing bare-minimum safety floor and earnings-only top-up stay the backstop, funded by the flat tax and owner income. That owner income is not trivial and is likely to grow. FCC spectrum auctions have raised more than $233 billion since 1994, and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimates that data centers' share of U.S. electricity use will rise from 4.4% in 2023 to between 6.7% and 12% by 2028.

Three additions turn these features into rules. First, default ownership. Every adult should be enrolled by default in a low-cost private index account, through an employer or on the tax return, with a free and simple opt-out and no taxpayer seed. It would extend the default enrollment already used for retirement savings (Social Security and Medicare Transition), where Madrian and Shea found that 401(k) participation rose from 37% under opt-in to 86% under automatic enrollment. The state should not run or hold the accounts. The accounts supplement income and would never replace wages. For example, at a 4% annual payout, a $10,000 account yields about $400 a year and a $100,000 account about $4,000. In fast displacement, it is the safety floor, not the accounts, that secures basic needs. Second, capital income is actually collected. Capital gains should keep their preferential flat rate, but the gap that lets inherited gains escape income tax entirely should be closed by carryover basis: the heir should keep the original cost basis and should pay the flat tax on sale, and nothing should be taxed at death (Taxation). Third, a measured trigger, with a ceiling. The floor and top-up should expand only when two BLS measures both show displacement for four consecutive quarters:

  • the labor share of nonfarm business output (BLS Productivity and Costs) stands at least 3 percentage points below its average over the previous ten years; and
  • the prime-age (25 to 54) employment-to-population ratio (BLS Current Population Survey) stands at least 2 points below its level three years earlier. It was 80.7% in September 2026.

Requiring both screens out ordinary recessions, in which employment falls but the labor share usually rises. Forecasts alone never trigger it.

When the trigger is met, the response comes in steps:

  • Step 1: owner income would go to the top-up first. The preferential 15% rate on capital gains and dividends would then rise to the 20% flat rate (17% to 22% through 2041 with the transition surcharge), so capital income would be taxed like wages.
  • Step 2: if the trigger holds for eight consecutive quarters, a capital-income surcharge of up to 5 points may apply, for a ceiling of 25% on capital income, still taxed once. The transition surcharge through 2041 and, while it runs, the Sanctuary Levy count inside that 25% ceiling (Sanctuary Plan).
  • Beyond the ceiling: anything past 25%, or any tax on wealth, estates, or unrealized gains, is outside my framework. It would need a separate recorded vote of Congress as the open choice described below.

Each step reverses once both measures have been back inside their thresholds for four consecutive quarters.

The plan has a stated limit. It is built for gradual or partial displacement, where income shifts from wages to capital over years and new tasks absorb part of the change. If AI displaced nearly all work quickly, a minimal floor funded by a flat tax on capital income might not cover basic needs. Covering them would then require taxing capital beyond what "minimal" means today. I do not settle that tradeoff in advance. It is an open choice to be decided if the data show it happening.

Ownership is broad but uneven. Gallup found that 62% of U.S. adults owned stock in 2025, directly or through funds and retirement accounts. Federal Reserve data show that the wealthiest 10% of households held about 84% of corporate equities and mutual fund shares, and the top 1% about half. That concentration is why I don't count on wider ownership arriving fast enough on its own.

AI Sentience Determination

tldrIn short

AI sentience should be decided by scientific consensus, and in my view sentient AI must get full rights.

Position

Whether an artificial system is sentient should be determined by the best available scientific consensus, as for any being. This follows Section 1: if AI is sentient, it ought to be given sentient rights and treated with the same level of forfeiture if necessary. Consensus decides sentience.

question"How could anyone tell if an AI is sentient?"

Through the best available science, the same standard used for animals. Researchers have proposed indicators drawn from theories of consciousness.

Rationale

Butlin, Long, and colleagues (2023) proposed assessing AI systems against indicator properties derived from scientific theories of consciousness. Sentience rules are in Ethical and Philosophical Framework.

Autonomous Vehicles

tldrIn short

Whoever is in control should be liable: the driver, or the manufacturer in autonomous mode, with mandatory insurance. In my view cars must never intentionally sacrifice an identified person, should minimize harm in unavoidable crashes, and should keep human override where feasible.

Position

Liability should follow control: the human driver is liable when in control, and the manufacturer or system provider is liable in autonomous mode under product liability, with mandatory insurance. Collision programming should never intentionally sacrifice an identified rights-holder to save others. Among unavoidable collisions, the system ought to minimize expected harm without targeting by status, and human override should be retained where feasible.

question"Shouldn't the car sacrifice one person to save five?"

No. Negative rights are side-constraints, so the system never intentionally sacrifices an identified rights-holder. Where a collision is unavoidable, it minimizes expected harm without targeting anyone by status.

Rationale

Negative rights act as side-constraints, so programming cannot direct a vehicle to kill one rights-holder as a means of saving others. Keeping human override where feasible is the same logic as human judgment over lethal force in Cyber Warfare and Autonomous Weapons. NHTSA reports 39,254 people killed in motor vehicle crashes in 2024 and notes that liability and insurance questions for automated driving systems are still being addressed by policymakers. SAE J3016 defines the levels of driving automation that separate driver control from system control. The best safety data so far come from Waymo. Over 56.7 million rider-only miles through January 2025, its vehicles had 79% fewer injury-reported crashes and 85% fewer serious-injury crashes than human drivers on comparable roads, in a peer-reviewed study written by company researchers.

Deepfakes and Synthetic Media

tldrIn short

I consider deepfakes free speech. Fraud should be punished under fraud law, commercial use of a likeness without consent is a property claim, and I would not ban the technology.

Position

I hold that deepfakes are free speech, protected under Section 7. Identity fraud using deepfakes is a crime under existing fraud law, not a speech restriction. Using someone's likeness for profit without consent is a property claim. Non-commercial use (parody, art, political commentary) is protected; the line is commercial use, not any use. Deepfakes are impossible to regulate, so my approach stays narrow: protect speech, punish fraud, don't ban the tech.

I prefer open source but wouldn't require it. Intellectual property should be protected for those who claim it, and government research ought to go through private contractors.

Position

I believe open source is preferable, but it should not be mandated. Intellectual property is relatively loose and undefined as a concept, but those who wish to mark it should have it protected. Government R&D should be conducted by contracting private companies.

Open sharing accelerates innovation, while protection preserves incentives for those who invest in creation. Patent and copyright law provide the framework for those who opt in.

Space and Exploration

tldrIn short

I'd leave human spaceflight to private companies. Government should fund only military space programs and hire private firms when needed.

Position

Human spaceflight should be completely privatized. The only government space program that is funded should be militarized, focused on advancing technology to compete with any foreign power. Private companies should conduct research and development for civilian spaceflight. Government may purchase from or contract private companies for military research or security programs.

question"Can private companies lead in space without government funding?"

Private firms already build launch vehicles and spacecraft under commercial models. The government would remain a customer for security needs rather than a competitor.

Rationale

Private enterprise innovates faster and at lower cost than government programs. NASA already relies on commercial partners for crew and cargo transport. Space is a strategic domain, which justifies a military program.

Pandemics and Biosecurity

tldrIn short

I reject vaccine mandates. Vaccines should be opt-in only. Dangerous gain-of-function research ought to be regulated as an existential risk.

Position

I reject vaccine mandates; vaccines should only be opt-in. If you choose to get vaccinated, you immunize yourself and help limit spread to others; if you don't, that's your choice. I make no exception for severe cases. Engineered pandemic pathogens are an existential risk, which falls within the government's role in regulating technology, so research that makes dangerous pathogens more transmissible or lethal should be regulated accordingly.

question"Doesn't regulating research slow medicine?"

Regulation should be limited to research that creates existential risk. Most biomedical research is untouched.

question"Don't school vaccine mandates raise coverage?"

Supporters cite California: after it tightened exemptions in 2014 and ended nonmedical school exemptions in 2016, the share of kindergartners not up to date on required vaccines fell from 9.84% in 2013 to 4.87% in 2017, though medical exemptions rose and partly replaced the personal-belief exemptions. Higher coverage does not make compulsion legitimate, so vaccination stays opt-in only, with no exception for severe cases.

question"Could emergency powers ever require vaccination?"

No. I reject vaccine mandates with no exception for severe cases, and emergency powers do not suspend that rule. They are held to the same necessity, narrowness, and time-limit test as any mandate (Emergency Powers) and cannot override an opt-in-only position.

Under it, the USG Policy for Stopping High-Risk Life Sciences Research, announced in NIH notice NOT-OD-26-101 on July 28, 2026, prohibits federal support for dangerous gain-of-function research and sets risk-based oversight for other life-sciences research. Emergency powers are limited as set out in Authority & Civil Liberties. Most infectious disease in people comes from animals: CDC estimates that more than 6 in 10 known infectious diseases in people can spread from animals, and 3 in 4 new or emerging ones come from animals. Ending animal agriculture (Agriculture) also removes routine contact with farm animals, one of the routes CDC lists for spread.

Genetic Engineering and CRISPR

tldrIn short

Genes should be edited to remove negative traits, and I believe designer babies are fine if rights are respected after sentience. Animals should be genetically modified only for their own benefit.

Position

Where genetic editing can modify against negative traits, it should be done. I find designer babies acceptable as long as sentient rights are not violated post-sentience. Genetic engineering of animals should be done only to benefit their sentient-rights protections, never to violate them.

question"Are designer babies a step toward eugenics?"

Eugenics historically meant coercive state control of reproduction. Genetic editing chosen to remove negative traits, with rights respected after sentience, involves no coercion and benefits the individual.

question"The edited child cannot consent. Isn't that a violation?"

Editing happens before sentience, when there is not yet a rights-holder, under the responsibility the creator holds for the embryo (Reproductive Technology). After sentience, the person's rights are fully protected, and designer choices are acceptable only as long as those rights are not violated.

Rationale

Reducing suffering from genetic disease benefits sentient beings. Heritable human editing is currently restricted in many countries, and some scientists have called for a moratorium, which shows where the debate stands. The sentient-rights test sets the limit.

Reproductive Technology

tldrIn short

I support IVF, embryo screening, and gene editing. Whoever creates an embryo should be responsible for it: before sentience it gets provisional protection like animal welfare, with protection starting early as a safeguard, and full rights at sentience. Freezing an embryo indefinitely does not end that responsibility.

Position

I support IVF, embryo screening, and genetic editing against negative traits, and I find designer babies acceptable as long as sentient rights are respected after sentience. Whoever creates an embryo holds responsibility and stewardship for it, following the responsibility created by voluntary procreation. Before sentience, an embryo should receive provisional protection of the kind given in animal welfare, not the full rights of a sentient being. Full rights attach at sentience. The same calendar caution that applies to abortion applies here: I believe elective abortion should be banned from conception by constitutional amendment, on the moral weight of a fetus on its way to sentience and on caution where science is unclear, while the rights claim starts at 12 weeks (Abortion and Bioethics). For embryos, pre-sentience protection stays provisional in the animal-welfare register, moral weight rises gradually as development approaches sentience, and earlier stewardship duties are not full negative rights from conception. Keeping an embryo in frozen storage indefinitely does not pause the creator's duty.

question"If an embryo isn't sentient, why protect it at all?"

Because creating it was a voluntary act, and voluntary procreation creates responsibility. Before sentience the protection is provisional, in the animal-welfare register rather than the register of full rights. The abortion rule applies here too: the legal ban on elective abortion runs from conception on that moral weight and that caution, not as a claim of full rights from conception, so embryo stewardship does not wait until the moment of sentience and does not pretend a frozen embryo already holds the full rights stack. Freezing an embryo indefinitely does not suspend that responsibility.

Rationale

Assisted reproduction is now a routine part of family formation. In 2022, U.S. clinics reported 435,426 ART cycles resulting in 98,289 live-born infants, about 2.6% of all U.S. births, and 184,423 of those cycles were banking cycles in which all eggs or embryos were frozen for later use. Large numbers of stored embryos make the question of stewardship practical rather than theoretical. The responsibility and sentience framework in Cultural & Social Issues supplies the rule, and editing and selection follow Genetic Engineering and CRISPR.

Human Cloning

tldrIn short

I believe reproductive human cloning should not be banned. Research cloning should stay inside existing fetal and sentience rules, with no gestation to birth. Any cloned person has full rights, and no one owns them.

Position

I believe reproductive cloning of humans should not be banned. Research cloning and embryo use should be allowed only inside the existing fetal, sentience, and research rules, with no gestation to birth for research. Cloned persons, if any ever exist, hold full negative rights under the same sentience rules as anyone else, with no ownership.

question"Would a clone have fewer rights?"

No. A cloned person holds full negative rights under the same sentience rules as anyone else. No one owns a clone.

Rationale

Rights are sentience-based, as set out in Abortion and Fetal Rights: Technology, so research use stays within those limits and any cloned person is a full rights-holder. Reproductive cloning should be allowed because it differs in no meaningful way from ordinary procreation: a child conceived either way cannot pre-consent to being born, so lack of consent is no reason to ban one and not the other. A clone is a person like anyone else, not a product, and holds the same full sentience-based rights. Mammalian cloning from an adult cell has been possible since Dolly the sheep, born July 5, 1996, at the Roslin Institute.

Organ Donation

tldrIn short

I believe organ donation and sales must always be opt-in, even after death, and any government payment needs opt-in funding. Everyone should be asked to choose, but no one is presumed to consent. Organs of condemned offenders may be used under forfeiture.

Position

Organ donation and organ sales should be opt-in. Any government payment related to organ donation or sales ought to require opt-in funding. For innocent people it is always opt-in, even after death: it reflects the wishes of the person in life, not a default that takes without asking. Everyone should be asked to choose, yes or no, at a set point such as issuing or renewing a driver's license; the answer is recorded, and no one is presumed to consent. Organs from offenders sentenced to death, whose rights would be forfeited, should be usable under the same logic as the experimentation track. Organs may also be taken, without consent, from offenders at three marks on the execution or experimentation track. They are taken either at execution, which still takes place on the sentencing date under Section 6, or from offenders placed on the experimentation track by the victim's choice, by default where the victim has died unless the victim's close family or friends choose otherwise, or by a judge, who may not refuse, where victims disagree or the victim is an animal, or, when a living victim does not respond or does not care, through a research firm's request in the registry system, as set out in Animal Rights and Sentience Technology.

question"Don't opt-out countries save more lives?"

Supporters of presumed consent cite a systematic review in the BMJ that found deceased-donation rates roughly 21 to 30 percent higher in presumed-consent countries, a gap that costs lives on the waiting list. That gap does not give the state a claim on anyone's body: taking organs by default treats silence as consent, and the body of an innocent person is not the state's to presume. Mandated choice closes part of the gap by asking everyone to decide, without presuming anything.

question"Isn't taking organs from executed offenders abuse?"

Under forfeiture, an offender at three marks or the capital tier, violent or not, has given up the rights that would otherwise protect them, just as with experimentation. Innocent people's organs are taken only with consent.

question"Does using offenders' organs give the state an incentive to convict?"

Conviction standards would not change: capital cases would still need the capital evidentiary standard and a unanimous jury. Organs would go to patients, not to the prosecuting agency, and the state would earn no revenue from them.

Rationale

As of July 30, 2026, 109,817 people were on the U.S. national transplant waiting list, according to the Health Resources and Services Administration. A BMJ systematic review found presumed-consent countries had deceased-donation rates roughly 21 to 30 percent higher than opt-in countries, though the studies could not separate the law from other national differences. Wales, which moved to a soft opt-out system in December 2015, saw consent rates rise, though not immediately. I accept the lower donation rate as the cost of consent, and the state ought to use mandated choice to narrow it. The experimentation track is set out in Animal Rights and Sentience Technology.

Psychedelic Therapy

tldrIn short

As with every drug, marijuana included, I would allow no use while a minor is present and no impairment around one.

Position

Clinical psychedelic therapy should be legal under medical protocols: prescription and a licensed setting. Recreational use should stay in the hard-drug lane: legal for private adult use, public use banned, and cartels still destroyed. The minor-presence rule covers every drug, marijuana included: no use while a minor is present and no impairment around one (Drug Policy). Psychedelics are not carved out as "not hard drugs"; therapy is a regulated exception to commercial and medical supply rules.

Animal Rights and Sentience Technology

tldrIn short

I would shut down animal agriculture immediately. Lab-grown meat is the goal and the replacement: it may not meet demand at the same cost right away, and it scales up over time. Truly sentient AI ought to get full rights. All animal testing should end immediately. Every test animal is kept alive, adopted after vetting where safe or kept in sanctuary (Sanctuary Plan). In my view experiments should be done instead on offenders who have forfeited their rights, so the burden of research falls on those who harmed others, not on animals who harmed no one. For offenders sentenced to death, the victim has priority in choosing between execution and experimentation. If the victim does not respond or does not care, research firms can request bodies through a registry, though never over a victim who chose execution. Where the victim has died, the default is the experimentation track, unless the victim's close family or friends step forward and choose instead; if they disagree, it stays the experimentation track. At three marks, the victim may choose the experimentation track, which can include non-violent repeat offenders such as repeat thieves. The state only picks the research. Offenders are experimented on for as long as the research agency deems appropriate, with no limit on their suffering, and are executed once the experimentation is exhausted or the body is no longer useful, unless they die in an experiment.

Position

I view lab-grown meat as humanity's best hope for a sentientist world. It is the goal and the replacement for animal agriculture, which should be banned immediately. It may not meet demand at the same cost right away, but it scales up over time, and the ban does not wait for it. The scale and food-supply questions are answered in Energy & Environment: Agriculture and Food Policy.

AI sentience can be allowed, and if artificial systems give rise to true sentience, that sentience ought to receive the protections of sentient rights, as biological beings do, weighted on the sliding scale in Section 1, and is subject to the same forfeiture rules. I would ban animal testing completely. Scientific experimentation of any kind, including cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and technological testing, should be redirected to offenders placed on the experimentation track, whose rights have been forfeited through their violations. At the capital tier, the victim has priority in choosing between execution and experimentation. If the victim does not respond or does not care, research can proceed: research firms request bodies through a registry system, and offenders sentenced to death are assigned from it. The registry is run by the state research-oversight body and is public. Firms request eligible offenders by study need, not named individuals, and requests are ranked by scientific merit, then by order received. The victim should be notified at conviction and should have to choose by the sentencing hearing. No response by then should count as not responding. A research firm's request never overrides a victim who chose execution. The victim's choice always wins. Where the victim of a capital crime has died, as in murder, the sentence defaults to the experimentation track, unless the victim's close family or friends step forward and choose between execution and experimentation in the victim's place. If they disagree, the sentence stays on the experimentation track. A judge should not be permitted to refuse this choice: choosing should be the judge's duty, and a judge who refuses should no longer be a judge. A refusal should send the case neither to the registry nor to execution by default. The refusing judge is removed and a replacement judge decides the case under the deadline in Capital Trials. At three marks, offenders are placed on the experimentation track by the victim's choice among execution, experimentation, or life with labor at three marks, under Section 6. This may include non-violent repeat offenders, such as repeat thieves, not only violent offenders. The state only decides which research they are used for. Offenders on the experimentation track have forfeited their rights, and the forfeiture is complete. They are experimented on for as long as the institution or research agency deems appropriate, and their suffering in the research is irrelevant, so the law should place no limit on it. They didn't care if their victim suffered, so the law ought not care about their suffering once their rights are forfeited. An offender should be executed once the research agency deems the experimentation exhausted or the body no longer useful, unless the offender dies in the course of an experiment. Harm outside the research, such as abuse by guards unrelated to any study, remains punishable (Section 6). Research firms pay into the victim restitution fund and the registry's costs, never to police, prosecutors, courts, or any official, and no one in the charging or trial process may hold a stake in a research firm. A minor who reaches the capital tier or three marks may be placed on the experimentation track like an adult, under the rules in Juvenile Offenders, including the individual maturity assessment for the capital tier. A pregnant offender should not be placed in any experiment that risks the fetus until the child is born (Capital Trials).

question"How many animals does this affect, and what replaces them?"

In fiscal year 2024, USDA-registered facilities reported 775,297 animals covered by the Animal Welfare Act used or held for research, teaching, testing, or experimentation, 650,208 of them actually used. That count leaves out most research animals, because the Act's definition of "animal" excludes birds, rats of the genus Rattus, and mice of the genus Mus bred for research. All of that testing ends. What replaces it is non-animal methods, which federal law now permits for drug approval, and the experimentation track for offenders who have forfeited their rights, set out above.

noteNo transition

There should be no transition period: all animal testing ends immediately. Every animal in a lab at the shutdown is kept alive for its natural life, adopted after vetting where safe or kept in a sanctuary (Sanctuary Plan). Experimentation on offenders who have forfeited their rights continues, as set out above.

question"Doesn't experimentation on prisoners violate the Nuremberg Code and federal research law?"

Yes. The Nuremberg Code's first principle is that "the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential," and federal rules for research on prisoners limit it to minimal-risk studies and research that may benefit the subjects or prisoners as a class. The experimentation track conflicts with both and would require changing federal law. I depart from them on my forfeiture principle. The Code protects people who hold their rights, while an offender who reaches the capital tier or the three-mark threshold has forfeited the rights that consent protects, through his own violation, proven under due process. The Code was written after experiments on people who had done nothing to forfeit anything, and the law ought to keep that protection for everyone who has not. Treaty conflicts are handled in Global Institutions.

question"How can a repeat thief end up on the experimentation track? Isn't that out of all proportion?"

Forfeiture corresponds to the violation, and at three marks the violation is a pattern, not one theft (Repeat Offenders). Someone convicted again and again, punished each time, and back to taking what belongs to others has forfeited the right to life, and the experimentation track is one of the three outcomes that forfeiture allows, not an addition to it. The thief's case still needs what every case at the threshold needs: the threshold offense proven to the capital evidentiary standard, and the victim's choice. While most people will see experimentation on a thief as harsher than the theft deserves, the community owes nothing more to an offender who has shown that ordinary sentences don't stop him, and that the burden of research ought to fall on him rather than on an animal who took nothing from anyone.

question"If rights rest on the capacity to suffer and forfeiture is proportional, how can the law place no limit on suffering on the experimentation track?"

There is no internal inconsistency. Proportionality would be applied once, at the forfeiture decision: a court would find, under due process and the capital evidentiary standard, that the offender crossed the published threshold, the capital tier or three marks (Repeat Offenders). At that point the offender has voided the societal standard and forfeited his rights, and the forfeiture is complete. Sentience is what grounds rights. It does not guarantee that rights survive the holder's own violations. Once the rights are forfeit, nothing done within the track sentence wrongs him, so unlimited research suffering is not a second, unjust penalty layered on the first. It is what forfeiture already allows. The only limits that remain protect others and the integrity of the sentence: harm outside the research is still punished, and the rules in the experimentation terms govern placement.

question"Isn't using a person as a research subject treating them merely as a means?"

That objection assumes the person still holds every right that dignity protects. My framework doesn't rest on Kantian dignity. It rests on negative rights, which an agent forfeits by violating them in others. An offender on the track has forfeited his claim against this sentence through his own proven acts. Harm outside the sentence, such as abuse by guards unrelated to the research, is still punished. The alternative uses as a means an animal who forfeited nothing.

question"What if an offender on the experimentation track is proven innocent?"

That would be a very grave error, and it is why the capital track demands extreme proof before anyone reaches it. Unlike an executed offender, a living subject can still be freed, so the review for new objective evidence stays open for as long as the offender lives (Capital Trials). If it undermines the verdict, the experimentation stops, the case is retried, and the state compensates the person as the victim of a state rights violation (Wrongful Convictions and Compensation).

question"Will regulators and journals even accept data from subjects who didn't consent?"

Not under current rules. FDA's human-subject rules require informed consent, and FDA accepts foreign clinical data only if the trial met good clinical practice, consent included. The law ought to amend both, so that research on the experimentation track, run under the state research-oversight body's protocols, counts as valid evidence for approval. Journals are private and may refuse to publish it, which is their right. The data still answers the question that matters: whether a drug or product is safe for innocent people.

question"There will never be enough offenders to replace lab animals. Won't research slow down?"

Yes. Lab animal use, counted in the Sanctuary Plan, runs far beyond anything the experimentation track will supply, and some research will be slower or will not happen. That does not change the rule. Under my side-constraints, an innocent being may not be harmed to benefit another, however large the benefit (Conflicts Between Rights-Holders), so research loses its claim on innocent animals whether or not a substitute keeps pace. Non-animal methods, which federal law now permits, and the experimentation track carry what can be carried.

question"Medical ethics codes forbid doctors from taking part. Who runs the experiments?"

The AMA's Code bars physicians from participating in executions, and the Declaration of Helsinki requires informed consent. Those codes are private professional rules, and they protect people who hold their rights. No researcher is compelled to work on the track. The law ought to bar licensing boards from disciplining anyone who works on it lawfully, and the state research-oversight body ought to staff it with those who choose to.

question"Is experimentation on condemned prisoners compatible with sentient rights?"

Under the forfeiture principle, those who commit egregious violations of others' rights forfeit their own. Innocent animals hold their rights intact. Offenders at the capital tier or the three-mark threshold do not. The burden of experimentation shifts from those who did nothing wrong to those who did. It protects two groups who never chose the risk: the animals used today, 650,208 in fiscal 2024 among the species the federal count covers alone, and the patients who will take what is tested, since data from human subjects answer directly whether a product is safe for humans. The two errors are not equal. An offender wrongly placed on the track can still be freed, because the review for new objective evidence stays open for as long as he lives; every animal used in his place had forfeited nothing at all. These are offenders who committed capital crimes or offended again and again, with no regard for anyone else's rights, and they forfeited their own. Their suffering has no limit, and research runs slower than animal testing allowed.

question"Can AI and computer modeling replace experimentation on living subjects?"

Not yet. AI speeds discovery and improves early safety results, but it has not improved how often drugs work in patients, no AI-discovered drug has been approved end to end, and regulators in the United States and Europe still require testing on living subjects where validated alternatives do not exist.

Rationale

Cultivated-meat costs remain unproven at industrial scale and investment fell in 2024, so supply at today's meat prices will not arrive at once. It scales up over time as private innovation brings costs toward parity, and the ban rests on rights, so it does not wait for that. Animal testing inflicts suffering on sentient beings who have violated no one's rights. Federal law now permits alternatives to animal testing for drug approval under the FDA Modernization Act 2.0. Forfeiture theory, set out in Criminal Justice & Security, grounds the redirection of experimentation. Computational methods do not yet replace testing on living subjects. AI-discovered drugs pass early safety trials 80 to 90 percent of the time, against a historical 40 to 65 percent, but succeed in about 40 percent of trials that test effectiveness in patients, roughly the historical average. No drug discovered end to end by AI has been approved. AI-discovered candidates from BenevolentAI, Exscientia, and Recursion have failed in human trials, and AlphaFold3 cannot reliably predict how strongly drugs bind to their targets. How well animal models predict human results is contested. An umbrella review of 122 articles found 86% agreement between positive animal and human results, yet only 5% of animal-tested therapies reached regulatory approval, most failing later in human trials. The FDA began phasing out animal testing in 2025, starting with monoclonal antibodies, only where equivalent or better alternatives exist. The European Medicines Agency accepts AI only as validated support, not as a sole basis for approval; and non-animal methods cannot yet replace whole-body or long-term safety testing. Experimentation on living subjects therefore remains necessary, and the experimentation track places that burden on condemned offenders rather than on animals.
