Core Principles

I stand for America First realism with selective Western imperialism. The United States should prioritize its sovereignty, security, cultural cohesion, and manufacturing strength. It ought to engage forcefully against direct threats (especially Iran and extremist Islam) while avoiding open-ended nation-building, and participate in alliances and institutions only when they clearly serve American interests.

Interventionism vs Non-Intervention

tldrIn short

The U.S. should intervene where its safety is at stake, especially against Iran. I view nation-building skeptically because it usually fails. Openly hostile cultures may be confronted and defeated. Non-hostile ones should be left alone.

Position

The United States should involve itself directly in areas of interest that strengthen the country's safety, such as intervention against Iran, a direct enemy and belligerent extremist force. I view nation-building and regime change with skepticism because of their track record of efficacy. I favor Western imperialism and colonialism that destroy openly hostile cultures whose governing norms violate sentient rights, which is what I mean by ethically inferior. Imperialism here means defeating and containing the threat, without governing the territory. Non-hostile cultures should be left alone in the majority of cases. When U.S. action takes control of a foreign country's assets or revenue, they ought to be held in trust for its people, with public audits, spending only for their benefit, a fixed end date unless Congress renews it, and creditors' claims settled in court.

question"Doesn't favoring imperialism against hostile cultures contradict skepticism of nation-building?"

No. Nation-building tries to reconstruct a society into a friendly democracy, which has repeatedly failed. Action against openly hostile cultures aims at defeating the threat, not at remaking the society in America's image. The line is drawn by hostility: hostile forces are confronted, non-hostile ones are left alone.

question"Who decides that a culture is hostile, and by what test?"

Conduct decides, not ethnicity or religion. A regime or movement is openly hostile when its governing institutions attack or sponsor attacks on the United States or commit systematic rights violations. Ethically inferior means governing norms that violate sentient rights. The President and Congress make the determination under their war powers. In real time, the President may act alone only to repel an attack under way or imminent, or to protect U.S. forces and citizens. That is the immediate response already allowed in Emergency Powers. A sustained campaign to defeat and contain a regime or movement needs Congress's authorization within 30 days, or it ends on a fixed date. That is shorter than the 60 days plus 30 for withdrawal in today's War Powers Resolution. When the evidence of attack or sponsorship is incomplete, the default is defensive action only: interception, protection of forces, sanctions, and intelligence gathering. A regime or movement is designated openly hostile, and offensive action begins, once the attack or sponsorship is attributed to its governing institutions on evidence the President certifies to Congress.

question"Isn't 'ethically inferior and openly hostile' a stipulated test?"

Where it comes from. Both halves come from my axioms. "Ethically inferior" ranks governing norms on the one scale I use everywhere: whether they violate sentient rights. "Openly hostile" is conduct: governing institutions that attack or sponsor attacks on the United States or commit systematic rights violations. Organized groups that systematically violate rights may be met with decisive force, the same forfeiture rule I apply to individuals, scaled up (Rights, Order, and Forfeiture at Scale). Both halves are required. A culture I rank low that is not hostile is left alone.

The rivals.

  • Nation-building measures success by remaking a society, and its record in Iraq, Afghanistan, and Libya is failure.
  • Pure non-intervention leaves attackers unanswered and the people they attack unprotected.
  • Ethnicity or religion as the test would judge by identity, not conduct.

What would change it. The test turns on conduct, so a designation should change with conduct. When the evidence of attack or sponsorship is incomplete, action stays defensive, and a sustained campaign ends unless Congress authorizes it within 30 days. A designation should lift once the governing institutions stop attacking or sponsoring attacks and stop systematic rights violations, confirmed on the same kind of evidence the President certified to designate them.

Rationale

Foreign policy exists to protect the security, prosperity, and continuity of the United States. Realist thinking holds that great powers should act when core interests are at stake and stay out when they are not. Recent efforts in Iraq, Afghanistan, and Libya showed that military victory is often achievable while building a stable, friendly state almost never is. Iran funds and directs proxy forces that have attacked U.S. troops and allies, pursues nuclear capability, and has plotted assassinations on U.S. soil, which makes it a concrete security problem rather than a distant one.

Alliances and NATO

tldrIn short

I believe the U.S. should join alliances only when they make the country safer and must never give up its sovereignty. It should stay diplomatic but not bow to allies or foreign courts.

Position

The United States should not be part of or a contributor to any alliance that erodes the nation's sovereignty or the best interests of its people. It ought to join alliances that increase the security and interests of the nation. I support diplomacy and foreign relations, but national principles come first, and the United States should not capitulate to allies' demands or submit to any foreign tribunal. It should take territory only with the consent of its people and their government, by purchase or treaty. With allies, security needs ought to be met first through basing and defense agreements. Threats and tariffs against a friendly country should be a last resort, justified only when the security concern is overwhelming and truly justified.

question"Does conditional membership weaken the credibility of alliances like NATO?"

Alliances are valuable because they serve the security of their members. Membership on the condition that it serves American interests is the same calculation every ally makes. Credibility comes from strength and clear interests, not from unconditional commitment.

Rationale

The federal government's first duty is to its own citizens, and alliances are tools toward that end. NATO's collective-defense commitment under Article 5 serves U.S. security where it deters common threats. Sovereignty means U.S. actions answer to U.S. law and voters, not to foreign bodies.

Trade and Economics

tldrIn short

American manufacturing comes first for me, imports should come with matching exports, and I treat tariffs cautiously. Outsourcing should be restricted only where it involves a country judged to be a national security threat, such as China. China is a dangerous rival, and trade with it should continue only on reciprocal terms.

Position

American manufacturing should be bolstered wherever possible. Every import brought into the country ought to have a deal structure under which the United States also exports to that partner, keeping trade mutually beneficial. I believe tariffs are good in theory and questionable in practice. Domestic trade should be prioritized and incentivized through tax and regulatory relief only, such as full expensing and the long-run payroll tax cuts in Domestic Policy & Governance. Outsourced labor should face regulations and restrictions only where it involves a country determined to be a national security threat to the United States; China is one example. I treat China as a dangerous rival, and trade with it should continue under these same conditions. Tariffs are taxes and belong to Congress. The President should impose them only under clear, narrow statutory authority, for a stated national-security or leverage purpose, and for a fixed period.

question"Do restrictions on outsourcing conflict with your free-market position?"

No. I support free markets at home, and outsourcing restrictions should be limited to countries determined to be national security threats to the United States, such as China. Reciprocity requirements and these narrow restrictions protect the industrial base the country needs to defend itself. The broad work of making U.S. labor cheaper is done through full expensing now and across-the-board payroll tax cuts in the long run, as the transition levy steps down, set out in Domestic Policy & Governance. Tariffs are approached with caution because their practical effects are mixed.

question"Don't Americans pay for tariffs?"

Mostly, yes. Studies of the 2018 tariffs found they passed almost completely into the prices Americans paid for imported goods, so the cost fell on U.S. buyers rather than on foreign exporters. That is why I treat tariffs as good in theory, as leverage and as protection for strategic industries, and questionable in practice. The preferred tools for supporting American industry are full expensing and, in the long run, payroll tax cuts.

Rationale

A manufacturing base is a strategic asset in war and crisis. The United States runs a large goods trade deficit with China, and the FBI identifies China's intelligence and economic espionage as a major threat. Reciprocity keeps trade beneficial to both sides rather than one-directional. The practical case against broad tariffs is measured. The 2018 tariffs passed through almost completely to U.S. import prices, with the full cost falling on domestic buyers and a loss in U.S. real income of about $1.4 billion a month by the end of 2018.

Sanctions and Economic Statecraft

tldrIn short

I support sanctions and trade limits that target adversaries like China, Russia, and Iran, working alongside military deterrence.

Position

Economic tools ought to serve national security. Trade should be reciprocal, and restrictions should apply to adversaries and national-security threats such as China, Russia, and Iran, including limits on outsourcing and technology transfer. Sanctions should be used where they weaken an enemy's capacity to threaten the United States, alongside military deterrence rather than in place of it.

question"Do sanctions actually work?"

Not alone. They are one tool that raises an adversary's costs and limits its access to technology and finance, used together with deterrence. Across 204 sanctions episodes studied by the Peterson Institute, sanctions were at least partly successful in 34%, succeeding about half the time for modest policy goals but in about 31% of attempts at regime change or democratization.

Rationale

The International Emergency Economic Powers Act authorizes the President to regulate economic transactions to deal with an unusual and extraordinary foreign threat once a national emergency is declared (50 U.S.C. § 1701). Under the emergency-powers rule in Authority & Civil Liberties, such declarations lapse unless the legislature renews them.

Foreign Aid

tldrIn short

Foreign aid must serve America's own security and interests. In my view aid that is purely charitable should be paid for only by people who choose to give, not by required taxes.

Position

Foreign spending ought to advance the security and long-term interests of the United States. Assistance that serves that purpose, such as support for allies against shared threats or efforts that counter adversaries, should be judged under the same national-interest test as alliances and institutions. Charitable aid that serves no American interest is redistribution abroad and ought to be funded only through taxpayer opt-in or private giving.

question"Won't cutting aid cede influence to China and other rivals?"

Aid that counters adversaries or secures American interests passes the national-interest test and can continue. What does not pass is charity carried out with compelled tax money. Americans who want to fund humanitarian work abroad remain free to do so, through opt-in programs or private giving.

question"Doesn't cutting aid cost lives?"

It can. The State Department credits PEPFAR, the U.S. HIV/AIDS program, with saving 26 million lives since 2003. I do not deny that. It rests on principle: compelled taxes do not fund charity abroad any more than at home. Programs like PEPFAR continue where they pass the national-interest test, and otherwise through opt-in funding and private giving, which remain open to everyone who wants to keep them going.

Rationale

The state's primary duty is to its own citizens, as set out in Ethical and Philosophical Framework, and I reject mandatory redistribution at home. Applying the same rule abroad keeps policy consistent. Foreign aid accounts for about 1% or less of the federal budget, and U.S. global health funding was $12.4 billion in FY2024. The amounts are small relative to the budget, so the question is one of principle: whether compelled taxes should fund charity abroad. The opt-in model is defined in Domestic Policy & Governance.

Global Institutions

tldrIn short

I believe the U.S. should join global institutions only when they help it and never submit to international courts or law over its own.

Position

Participation in global institutions follows my position on alliances: the United States should contribute only to those that benefit it and serve its best interests. It may contribute, but it should not be held to any standard, tribunal, or global court for its sovereign actions or capitulate to any international law. The United States ought to follow its own law and what is best for its people and shared values, and no globalist bureaucrats or courts should dictate its conduct. Treaties the United States has ratified remain binding as U.S. law until it exits them through its own constitutional procedure. The United States should stay in the UN and other bodies where participation benefits it. The rejection of foreign tribunals applies to every nation's sovereignty, not only America's. The United States should not recognize or cooperate with the International Criminal Court. Sanctions on its officials ought to be kept for concrete acts against Americans or allied personnel, such as warrants or arrests, not for the court's existence or general investigations.

question"Does rejecting international law isolate the United States?"

No. The United States can cooperate with any institution that serves its interests. What it rejects is surrendering authority over its own actions to foreign bodies that answer to no American voter.

question"Doesn't this let the U.S. ignore treaties it signed?"

No. A treaty the United States has ratified is U.S. law and is followed until the country exits it through its own procedure. What I reject is a foreign court or body claiming authority over U.S. actions.

Some ratified treaties conflict with my positions, and the United States ought to exit them before those positions take effect. The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, ratified by the United States in 1992, says in article 7 that "no one shall be subjected without his free consent to medical or scientific experimentation," and article 4(2) allows no derogation from it. The U.S. reservation to article 7 limits only "cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment" to what the Fifth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments forbid. It does not reach the experimentation clause. The U.S. reservation to article 6 keeps capital punishment for anyone "other than a pregnant woman" under "existing or future laws," but the Human Rights Committee reads article 6 to allow death only for crimes "involving intentional killing" and to bar new capital offenses after ratification. So the United States ought to withdraw from the Covenant through its own constitutional procedure before the experimentation track and the new capital offenses take effect. While the Covenant has no withdrawal clause and the Committee holds that no state may leave it, the United States still ought to leave, because no nation is bound forever to a foreign committee's reading of a treaty it chose to join. The Convention Against Torture can be denounced under its article 31, effective one year after notice, and the United States ought to denounce it too if its definition of torture is read to reach the experimentation track. Leaving the Convention does not lift the bar on removing any resident to torture, which the law ought to hold in statute.

Rationale

Sovereignty means accountability to one's own citizens. The United States is not a party to the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court. Institutions such as the UN operate under a charter that lets members participate without surrendering domestic authority. The American Service-Members' Protection Act of 2002 (22 U.S.C. 7421 et seq.) bars U.S. cooperation with the International Criminal Court and authorizes the President to use "all means necessary and appropriate" to free U.S. and allied personnel it detains.

World Health Organization

tldrIn short

The United States should not be bound by the WHO. I'd cooperate on specific health work, such as surveillance data sharing, where Americans clearly benefit, with voluntary funding tied to results.

Position

The United States should not be a member bound by the World Health Organization's rules, regulations, or pandemic agreements, consistent with Global Institutions. It may cooperate with the WHO and other health bodies case by case through specific agreements, such as disease-surveillance data sharing, where the benefit to Americans is clear. Any funding should be voluntary, targeted to named programs, and tied to published results. Domestic defense against pandemics, natural or engineered, is the government's own duty (Pandemics and Biosecurity).

question"Doesn't leaving the WHO weaken the world's early warning against pandemics, including for Americans?"

Supporters of membership point to disease surveillance: disease does not stop at borders, and the WHO coordinates reporting and response among nearly every country. Cooperation does not require membership. The United States ought to keep that benefit by cooperating on surveillance and data sharing through specific agreements, without being bound to the organization's rules. Membership has not guaranteed good performance. The Independent Panel for Pandemic Preparedness and Response found that the COVID-19 outbreak likely met the criteria for a global emergency at the WHO committee's first meeting on January 22, 2020, but the declaration came only on January 30.

question"How much does the United States pay?"

More than any other country. The United States contributed $1.284 billion in the 2022–2023 biennium, 15.6% of the WHO's revenue. Most of it was voluntary and earmarked. The assessed contribution has been set at the 22% maximum share of member assessments.

Rationale

The United States ought to take part in global institutions only where they serve the national interest, and never submit to their authority. Health cooperation serves that interest when it is specific and measured; open-ended membership and funding do not guarantee results, as the 2020 delay showed.

Immigration and Borders

tldrIn short

I see a culturally similar society as strongest, so immigration should be limited to people who fit Western values and assimilate, with American labor needs as a secondary criterion. Entry should be closed by default, open only through named paths in statute, each under the same values vetting: family, employment, seasonal work, students and visitors, refugee resettlement, and asylum, which is one category of application, not a pass to enter. The diversity visa lottery should end. I want less immigration from Muslim-majority countries. Being Muslim carries a presumption against admission, which an individual applicant can overcome only with verified evidence of conduct and record. The border must be heavily secured, with lethal force when necessary, and illegal immigrants removed by force. Refugee resettlement is a national-interest privilege. Residents who are removed keep the bars against return to persecution or torture; people denied at the border or applying for asylum do not. Birthright citizenship should end by amendment for future births.

Position

I believe cultural cohesion in values should be the aim. Measured diversity–trust links are real but small on average and strongest for trust in neighbors; that is a reason for vetting and assimilation priority, not a diversity compliment. Culture-mashing raises tribalism and clashes of ideals - so I hold that immigration is not a virtue and not a protected entitlement. Entry should be closed by default, open only through named entry paths set in statute, each under the same values vetting. Starting from today's categories, these are the paths I would name:

  • Family: spouses, minor children, parents, siblings, and adult children of citizens, and spouses and minor children of green-card holders.
  • Employment, permanent and temporary, including H-1B: only for American labor needs the domestic workforce cannot meet first (Skilled-Worker Visas). Investment may count as one factor after vetting, never as a purchase of status (Investor Visas).
  • Seasonal work: temporary farm and non-farm seasonal visas, under the same labor-needs-first rule.
  • Students and visitors: study, business, and tourist visas. Student visas ought to carry a fixed term set by Congress, with an extension granted for documented academic progress and a fresh values check, rather than an open stay that lasts as long as a program does (see Fixed terms for foreign students (see also Student internship limits blocked)). Work on a student visa ought to be limited to training the degree program requires, set by statute or published rule; any other work goes through the employment path, open only for labor needs the domestic workforce cannot meet first.
  • Refugee resettlement: a national-interest privilege, not a right, under the same vetting. It is sad, but protecting non-citizens from persecution abroad is not the purpose of the U.S. government; a refugee applicant who fails vetting is simply not admitted (Removal of Non-Citizen Residents).
  • Asylum: claims made here or at the border should continue to be heard under the same vetting. Asylum is one category of application, not a pass to enter and not a duty to protect. Entry requires meeting the same assimilation and values standard as every other path; a claimant who fails vetting is simply not admitted.

The diversity visa lottery should end.

Every path carries the same conditions: individual values vetting, with the Muslim and listed-country presumptions where they apply; no entry from places under the hard entry ban (asylum claims from those places are still heard under the same vetting, and a claimant who passes it may enter), and no lottery or open channel from listed places (Questionable Compatibility); and residence conditional on self-support, passing the English and civics test within two years, and obeying the law.

My selection order: Western-values fit and assimilation first; American labor needs second. I want less immigration from Muslim-majority countries. Muslim identity carries a rebuttable presumption of incompatibility, overcome only by clear and convincing verified conduct and record, never by self-declaration: acceptance of constitutional supremacy over religious law, equal rights for women and non-Muslims, freedom to leave the faith, and rejection of violence (Islam and Extremism). Border force, removal, birthright abolition going forward, and refugee resettlement are covered in In short. Resettlement is a national-interest privilege under the same values vetting. Persecution abroad gives an applicant no claim on the United States: its government exists to protect its citizens, not non-citizens.

question"Doesn't the Fourteenth Amendment guarantee birthright citizenship?"

Yes, as currently interpreted, which is why abolition is proposed through constitutional amendment. The Supreme Court read the Citizenship Clause to cover children born on U.S. soil in United States v. Wong Kim Ark. The change applies only after the amendment, leaving existing citizens' status untouched.

question"Isn't lethal force at the border excessive?"

No, because it is held to clear limits. I want the border protected, with lethal force when necessary, and necessary should mean one of two things. The first is the rule for any use of deadly force: an agent reasonably believes the person poses an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury to the agent or to someone else. That is already Department of Homeland Security policy, and it tracks Tennessee v. Garner (1985). The second goes beyond current policy and should be rare: a mass, coordinated push to force through the border, an invasion-style breach. Ceuta shows what that looks like. On July 30 and 31, 2026, about 50,000 people by the Spanish government's count, and about 60,000 by the count of Ceuta's president, crossed from Morocco into Spain's enclave of Ceuta in about 24 hours, by land and sea, some swimming or wading and some scaling the border fence, into a city of 85,000. At least 57 people died, a toll Spain later raised to 72 on its side of the border. Spain's prime minister called it "a violation of Spain's territorial integrity." Ceuta saw a smaller rush in May 2021, when about 8,000 people entered in two days. Other pushes have been openly violent. In June 2022, between 1,500 and 2,000 migrants, some carrying sticks, stormed the fences at Melilla, Spain's other enclave. In March 2020, after Turkey opened its side of the border, migrants throwing stones and wielding metal bars tried to force their way into Greece at Kastanies. A border that can be overrun that way is not defended. Border agents should wear body cameras, as I require for ICE officers, and should answer under the objectively unreasonable standard I set for every officer in place of qualified immunity (Police Power and Accountability). Illegal entrants who are caught should be removed by force.

question"Doesn't the economy need immigrant labor?"

Supporters of high immigration point to the budget: the Congressional Budget Office estimated that the 2022–2026 immigration surge would reduce federal deficits by about $0.9 trillion over 2024 to 2034. That gain does not decide admission. My position rests on social cohesion, not on economics: I treat a shared culture of values as worth more than the fiscal gain. Labor needs are met first through the domestic workforce, with full expensing and, in the long run, lower payroll taxes making American labor cheaper, and immigration that remains is selected first through values vetting and assimilation, then by American labor needs.

question"Don't undocumented immigrants pay more in taxes than they cost?"

No. They pay real taxes, about $96.7 billion in 2022 by ITEP's estimate, much of it through sales and property taxes and payroll taxes for programs they cannot draw on. The claim leaves out the spending side. States and localities pay for schooling their children, plus health care, shelter, and justice costs, and those costs exceed what they collect. The National Academies found the first generation costs state and local budgets more than natives do, mainly through education. CBO found the 2021–2023 surge cost states and localities a net $9.2 billion in 2023. The size of the gap depends on the population estimate, whether U.S.-born children are counted, and whether broader economic effects are included. The direction does not change.

question"How does an applicant overcome the presumption?"

By evidence of conduct and record, not by declaration. Every immigrant goes through individual values vetting. Two groups start with the burden on them: Muslim applicants, and applicants from countries where Islamist movements govern or where survey data show majority support for state-enforced religious law. To overcome it, the applicant should have to show by clear and convincing evidence all five of the following:

  1. Constitution over religious law: acceptance that the Constitution and civil law override any religious law, including in family, inheritance, and criminal matters.
  2. Equal rights: acceptance of equal legal standing for women and for non-Muslims and nonbelievers.
  3. Freedom to leave the faith: acceptance that anyone may leave or change religion, or criticize it, without penalty.
  4. No violence: rejection of violence for religious or political ends, with no membership in, support for, or funding of a designated terrorist or Islamist organization.
  5. A verifiable record: a record of conduct that matches 1–4 and can be checked.

Evidence that counts: criminal and security record checks. Public-data review of the applicant's own posts, publications, and affiliations, within the public-data limits in Surveillance and Digital Technology; memberships, donations, and employers; prior lawful residence and compliance with the law in a country that meets these standards; and sworn references who can be checked. A structured interview and a sworn statement should be required, but they are never enough on their own. The statement would be made under the perjury rules in Oath of Office and Perjury, so a lie found later would be fraud, which is conduct and a ground for removal.

Who decides: a consular or USCIS officer trained for values vetting would decide first. A denial would get one review by a second officer, on the written record. The applicant would carry the burden throughout. Being Muslim would set where the burden starts. It would never be the test itself, and an applicant of any faith who fails 1–5 would be refused.

Passing vetting would admit a person. It would not lift the excluded-ideologies amendment, which bars adherents of Islam, as it bars adherents of Nazism, communism, and socialism, from authority roles (Oath of Office and Perjury). Nor would passing vetting lift the mosque bar: no new mosques may be built, and existing mosques would stay capped with no expansion, relocation, or rebuilding (Religious Freedom).

question"Why this compatibility test, and what would change it?"

Where it comes from. Entry is a privilege, not a right, so refusing it takes nothing anyone holds. The test checks the same values my rights framework rests on: the Constitution over religious law, equal rights for women, non-Muslims, and nonbelievers, freedom to leave a faith, and rejection of violence. High-trust societies protect negative rights better in practice, and that trust rests on shared norms (Sovereignty and Borders).

The rivals.

  • Open borders treat entry as a right, which I reject.
  • Selection by labor needs alone ignores whether a newcomer accepts the rights order. I keep labor needs, but second, after values.
  • Selection by ethnicity or ancestry judges people by what they cannot change and says nothing about their values.

What would change it. A place comes off the list when it meets none of the grounds in consecutive annual reviews, and for the polling ground that means open support for genocidal attacks below 1% (Questionable Compatibility). Any individual can overcome the presumption with verified conduct and record. The presumption for being Muslim rests on what the faith teaches, so I would revisit it only if those mainstream teachings changed (Group Statistics and Individual Judgment).

question"What stops officers from abusing the presumption?"

The rules are written so that abuse leaves a record. The five points are named in advance, and an applicant of any faith who fails them is refused, so being Muslim sets where the burden starts but is never the test itself. Every decision is made on a written record, and a denial gets a second officer's review on that record. Sworn statements fall under the perjury rules, for officers' records as well as applicants' statements.

On top of that, I believe every denial should state in writing which of the five points failed and on what evidence. Denial rates should be published every year by post, by officer, and by nationality. An inspector general independent of the deciding agency should audit a random sample of approvals and denials each year. An officer who denies on grounds outside the five points, or falsifies a record, should be removed from vetting and prosecuted for the falsification. Admission decisions themselves stay outside court review, as they are under current law, because admission is a privilege; removing a resident still takes a court finding (Removal of Non-Citizen Residents).

question"Won't this damage relations with Muslim-majority allies?"

Some, and I accept that cost. Governments may answer with visa limits of their own, and some cooperation may cool. Three things limit the damage. The presumption is individual and rebuttable, so citizens of any country who meet the five points are admitted. A place comes off the list when it meets none of the grounds in consecutive annual reviews, so every government has a clear path off it. And alliances should serve U.S. interests: a partner that works with the United States against terror does so because it serves its own interests too, and that does not end over visa policy. question"Immigrants commit less crime than natives. Why prioritize removal?"

The data do show lower crime among undocumented immigrants: in Texas arrest records, which check the immigration status of every arrestee, undocumented immigrants had considerably lower felony arrest rates than native-born citizens. Incarceration is not the same as offending, but the national record points the same way: immigrants have had lower incarceration rates than the U.S.-born for 150 years, and by 2020 they were 60% less likely to be incarcerated, and 30% less likely than U.S.-born whites, a gap that has widened since 1960 and that the authors find is not explained by immigration policy or by immigrants' observable characteristics. Removal does not rest on crime rates. It rests on the rule of law and on sovereignty: a nation decides who enters and stays, and entering or remaining illegally is itself the violation of that law.

question"Aren't undocumented immigrants law-abiding?"

No. Lower arrest rates are one thing. Being law-abiding is another. Entering without inspection is a federal crime under 8 U.S.C. § 1325, and staying past a visa's terms makes a person removable under 8 U.S.C. § 1227. Either way, the person is breaking immigration law from the first day. Unlawful presence also tends to bring more violations with it. Working without authorization breaks the employment rules of 8 U.S.C. § 1324a, which usually means showing an employer a forged or borrowed green card or Social Security number. Using a false Social Security number is a felony under 42 U.S.C. § 408(a)(7). Using fraudulent immigration documents is one under 18 U.S.C. § 1546. And using another person's identity is aggravated identity theft under 18 U.S.C. § 1028A when it is tied to such a felony. Many people stay below the threshold for arrest, but that is not the same as obeying the law. The law ought to be enforced as written, and removal should follow.

Rationale

Shared values make trust and cooperation possible, which is why I favor cohesion over diversity. Critics cite the fiscal gain from immigration, which CBO put at about $0.9 trillion in lower deficits over 2024 to 2034; cohesion outweighs it. At the state and local level, unauthorized immigration is a net cost, driven mainly by schooling. Federal law already sets grounds for inadmissibility, so vetting for values extends an existing power. Critics note that no study shows values screening predicts later conduct. The vetting does not need one: it rests on the principle that a nation may choose members who share its values. The measured link between diversity and lower trust is real but small on average, strongest for trust in neighbors. About 9% of U.S. births in 2023 (roughly 320,000) were to unauthorized or temporary-status mothers. Enforcement positions are set out in Border Security and Immigration Enforcement. Denmark's crime-by-origin pattern and the higher CIS household-level means-tested use among legal immigrant–headed households in the U.S. (data above) are noisy priors for screening, not proof that every applicant fails and not a substitute for named-recipient tests at removal or at the ballot. They are why nationality and welfare rules sit upstream of individual cases under my compatibility bar (Questionable Compatibility; Western Values and Compatibility).

Questionable Compatibility

tldrIn short

My list has two tiers. Places on grounds A or B (the regime or dominant faction backs genocidal attacks, or 5% or more of people openly support them) should face a complete entry ban by nationality or place of origin, with no case-by-case visa path. The ban lifts only when open support stays below 1% in consecutive annual reviews and no authority there endorses such attacks. Places on grounds C-I, or added for security, are of questionable compatibility: free or open immigration is off, and anyone from them is admitted only case by case, starting from no. The list is a prior, not a verdict on any person. A place is listed on any one of nine grounds:

  • genocidal attacks backed by its regime or a dominant faction
  • 5% or more open support for such attacks
  • state sponsorship of terrorism
  • unresolved ethnic cleansing
  • state war crimes, or a pattern of state killing and torture with impunity
  • Islamist governance or majority support for religious law
  • death for apostasy, blasphemy, or homosexuality
  • terrorist cartel or gang control
  • state denial of women's civil equality Findings must be verified by the United States, and the list should be reviewed every year.

Position

The United States ought to keep this list. It has two tiers, and they are not the same.

Hard ban (grounds A and B). Entry should be banned completely, by nationality or place of origin, from any place whose regime or dominant factions wage or celebrate attacks that target civilians for genocide or ethnic cleansing, or where reliable polling shows 5% or more of the population openly supporting such attacks. When the people who hold power or set the norm openly back genocidal attacks, screening cannot reliably sort the hostile from the rest, because hostility is common, rewarded, and easy to conceal, and one wrong admission can cost lives. A/B places are therefore not priors for case-by-case screening - there is no case-by-case visa path from them. A place comes off the hard ban only when open support stays below 1% in consecutive annual reviews and no governing authority or dominant faction there still endorses such attacks (Hostile conduct, removal, and entry). West Bank and Gaza, Iran, Yemen, Lebanon, Sudan, Burma, China, Afghanistan, and every other A/B listing sit on this tier.

Questionable Compatibility (grounds C–I, plus security adds). Free or open immigration from these places should be off. No visa class, lottery, or open channel ought to admit anyone from them as a matter of course. Each applicant should be decided case by case, starting from no and carrying the burden of showing fit. I hold that entry is a privilege, not a right (Hostile conduct, removal, and entry), and a nation owes admission to no one whose society is hostile to it or incompatible with the values it rests on: individual negative rights, liberty, religious freedom with separation of church and state, and the rule of law (Western Values and Compatibility). Each of these grounds marks a place whose laws, rulers, or prevailing culture reject those values or wage violence against civilians. Where that is true, ordinary screening is less trustworthy, so the default flips: the United States ought not to carry the risk unless an individual case clears it.

A place should be listed if it meets any one ground, and come off only when it meets none of them in consecutive annual reviews. For the polling ground that means open support below 1%, and for every ground it means no governing authority or dominant faction there still does what put it on the list. I count a poll answer that calls such an attack "correct" or "legitimate" as open support. Every finding must be verified by the United States government. A foreign or international finding counts only once the United States has confirmed it on the evidence, and I reject findings I judge unfounded, such as the claim that Israel is committing genocide in Gaza (Israel and the Middle East). Beyond the grounds, the government and its immigration enforcement may add a place to the list or set a listing aside as security requires.

Compatibility bar

I treat nationality as a prior on cultural fit. The question at the border is not whether a place has been proven hostile, but whether it has shown it is compatible, so a presumption against entry is the default and clearance is the exception. A place stays clear only if it fails none of these hard bars:

  • No open majority, or 5% or more celebrating, for genocidal attacks on civilians (A, B).
  • No state death penalty for apostasy, blasphemy, homosexuality, or leaving Islam (F, G).
  • Women's civil equality not denied by the state as a formal baseline (I).
  • No terrorist cartel or gang the United States lists holding territory or working with the state's collusion (H).
  • Not a pattern, documented by the United States, of state unlawful killings and torture with impunity (E).

I don't use gay rights as a baseline metric. Women's civil equality is one. Clearing a place clears no person: every individual from a clear place should still be screened. A place goes under review when it fails no hard bar but its polling or religious-freedom record is weak or out of date.

The grounds

  • A. Genocidal attacks backed by those in power: the regime or a dominant faction wages or celebrates attacks that target civilians for genocide or ethnic cleansing.
  • B. Open support: reliable polling shows 5% or more of the population openly supporting such attacks.
  • C. State sponsor of terrorism: the United States designates the state as a sponsor of terrorism, or it knowingly hosts the leadership of a designated terrorist group.
  • D. Unresolved ethnic cleansing: forces of the state or its allies drove out a population for its ethnicity within the last ten years, and the victims have not been allowed to return in safety.
  • E. State war crimes or killing with impunity: the state's own forces commit murder, torture, rape, or deliberate attacks on civilians as a pattern, on a finding verified by the United States. Or the State Department's annual human rights report finds credible reports of both unlawful killings and torture by the state and finds the government took no credible steps to identify and punish the officials responsible. A government credited with credible steps does not meet this ground.
  • F. Islamist governance or religious law: an Islamist movement governs the state or a large share of its territory, or polling shows a majority of the population wants religious law made the law of the land.
  • G. Death for faith or homosexuality: the law punishes apostasy, blasphemy, or homosexuality with death.
  • H. Terrorist cartel or gang control: a cartel or gang the United States designates a Foreign Terrorist Organization or Specially Designated Global Terrorist holds substantial territory in the country, or operates with the effective collusion of the state or with impunity from it, as found by the United States government.
  • I. Women's civil equality denied: the state denies women civil equality in law as a formal baseline, such as male guardianship over adult women or unequal legal standing in court.

question"Can the federal government abolish sanctuary cities without violating states' rights?"

I treat abolishing sanctuary jurisdictions as enforcing a federal responsibility that local governments have no authority to obstruct. People may know the law. What I reject is a city government using its official channel to train the public on how to frustrate federal removal while it runs as a sanctuary jurisdiction. question"What about people who have worked here for years and broken no other law?"

Many of them are good neighbors and hard workers, and theirs are the hardest cases. But sympathy for a person cannot decide whether the law applies to that person. Immigration law decides who joins the political community. If that is settled by who managed to arrive and whose story moves us most, the citizens' consent is replaced by the fact of arrival. Hard cases are exactly where a rule has to hold, or it is not a rule. The 1986 amnesty was sold as a one-time fix: it legalized about 2.7 million people, the unauthorized population fell to about 1.9 million in 1988, and it then rose to about 12.4 million by 2007. Analysts also blame weak workplace enforcement and few legal channels for low-skilled work, but the amnesty did not end illegal immigration, and I believe a second one would invite the next wave. Removal applies to long-settled workers as to anyone else, with full due process (Due Process and Rule of Law). A nation that cannot control who enters cannot enforce its own laws or decide who joins the political community, and the high-trust society I aim for depends on enforced law. Expanding ICE gives enforcement the capacity it lacks, and the body-camera requirement applies the same accountability standard set for police. Official city coaching on how to meet ICE without cooperating is the sanctuary model in practice, not a neutral public-service announcement. ICE and CBP publish enforcement and encounter statistics that document the scale of the task.

Investor Visas

Position

Immigration status should never be sold. An investor ought to go through the same individual values vetting and civics requirements as every other applicant (Immigration and Borders). Investment may count as one factor for an economic visa only after vetting is passed, and only with proof that the money was lawfully obtained and audited proof that the promised jobs were created. A payment or gift to the government should buy no place in line.

question"Investors bring capital and jobs. Why not welcome them?"

They can be welcome, on the same terms as everyone else. Investment is a real benefit, so it may count once vetting is passed. It is never a substitute for vetting.

Rationale

Admission is a privilege granted to those who fit the shared value structure (Western Values and Compatibility). Selling status replaces that test with wealth, and the EB-5 record shows the fraud risks that come with it.

Removal of Non-Citizen Residents and Loss of Citizenship

tldrIn short

Green card and visa holders should be removed, after a court finding by clear and convincing evidence, if they take cash welfare, SNAP, housing aid, non-emergency Medicaid, or the last-resort allowance, fail the naturalization English and civics test within two years, or are convicted of any crime above a basic infraction. Refugees go to a safe third country, never to persecution or torture. These bars are for residents: anyone lawfully living here who is removed or deported, including a resettled refugee, keeps them. People denied at the border or applying for asylum do not; the government exists to protect its citizens, not non-citizens, so the American taxpayer has no duty to protect or shelter them or to fund or arrange their transfer. Someone who entered unlawfully and claims asylum from inside the country is an asylum seeker, not a resident. No one should be removed for opinions or speech. I believe terrorism, material support, and treason should be punished, not deported: death where the capital standard is met, secure confinement under compelled labor otherwise, for citizens and non-citizens alike. I support a constitutional amendment adding forfeiture of citizenship for dual nationals so convicted, never leaving anyone stateless and never applied retroactively.

Position

I hold that lawful residence for a non-citizen is a conditional privilege: self-support, integration, and obeying the law. The rule covers green card and visa holders already here, and reaches only conduct after it takes effect. The three removal grounds, the refugee safe-third-country path, the speech bar, and the clear-and-convincing court finding are in the In short above - not repeated here. Each case should still require written notice, a hearing before a judge, the chance to present evidence, and federal-court review (Due Process and Rule of Law). A third country should count as safe only after review of that person's case, not on diplomatic assurances alone.

Terrorism, material support to a designated terrorist organization, and treason should never be handled by deportation instead of punishment, citizen or not. They ought to be prosecuted as crimes and treated as attempted murder is: death where guilt meets the capital evidentiary standard (Mandatory Death Penalty; Evidentiary Standard); secure confinement under compelled labor where proven beyond a reasonable doubt but short of that standard (Compelled Labor). Any knowing support counts, not only an attack. For a capital sentence the support should have to be a documented money trail or communications meeting the capital standard. Deportation would only move the threat elsewhere and let it return; punishment ends it.

A dual national convicted of one of these offenses should also forfeit U.S. citizenship as an added penalty alongside the sentence, never in place of it - equally for dual nationals by birth and by naturalization, only for conduct after enactment, and never leaving anyone stateless. The law names offenses, not religions. Because the Supreme Court holds that citizenship cannot be taken without the citizen's consent, the forfeiture ought to be enacted by constitutional amendment, and the removal rules for non-citizens by statute.

question"Isn't 'failing to integrate' too vague to enforce?"

It would be if it meant attitudes or lifestyle, so it does not. The only test is the one the country already uses for new citizens: federal law requires naturalization applicants to show "an understanding of the English language" and knowledge of U.S. history and government. A resident who cannot pass that test within two years of admission has not met the condition. Broader standards, such as hostility to the country's way of life, are left out because they cannot be measured and would turn removal into a penalty for speech (Free Speech and Censorship).

question"Isn't removal for any theft too harsh?"

Residence is a privilege extended on condition of obeying the law, and a resident who steals has broken that condition. Basic infractions such as traffic tickets are excluded so that the rule reaches crimes against people and property, not paperwork. Current law already makes many non-citizens deportable for crimes. This rule removes the gaps and the discretion, with a conviction and a court finding required in every case. The rule applies the same way to a long-term green-card holder with citizen family: residence was granted on the same condition, and the rule reaches only conduct after enactment, so every resident knows the terms in advance. Family members who are citizens keep their citizenship and may choose to stay or to leave. The rule protects the citizens who extend the privilege, who should not be the ones to pay when its terms are broken, and the lawful residents who keep those terms, whose standing should not be judged by those who don't. The two errors are not equal. A resident removed in error loses residence here, after notice, a hearing, and federal-court review; a resident who keeps offending and stays leaves the risk with people who never agreed to carry it. The condition was known when residence was granted, and a family that wants to stay together can leave together.

question"Doesn't the Constitution forbid stripping citizenship?"

For involuntary loss, yes, which is why this part requires an amendment. In Afroyim v. Rusk (1967) the Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment defines "a citizenship which a citizen keeps unless he voluntarily relinquishes it," and in Vance v. Terrazas (1980) it required proof of intent to give up citizenship. In Trop v. Dulles (1958) a plurality held that stripping citizenship as punishment was cruel and unusual, noting that "statelessness is not to be imposed as punishment for crime." The amendment answers the first point directly and the second by limiting the rule to dual nationals, so no one is left without a country. The forfeiture is added to the criminal sentence, death or secure confinement, and never replaces it. Current law already revokes citizenship obtained by fraud, where naturalization was "illegally procured or ... procured by concealment of a material fact."

question"Can lawful residents be deported for using benefits or committing crimes without an amendment?"

Yes, with full due process. The Supreme Court upheld deporting long-time legal residents in Harisiades v. Shaughnessy (1952), treating deportation as a civil matter rather than punishment. In Zadvydas v. Davis (2001) it held that "the Due Process Clause applies to all persons within the United States, including aliens, whether their presence is lawful, unlawful, temporary, or permanent," which is why every case here goes through a hearing, the clear-and-convincing standard, and court review. Existing law already points this way. A non-citizen who becomes "a public charge" within five years of entry from causes not shown to have arisen after entry is deportable. Most qualified non-citizens are barred from federal means-tested benefits for their first five years.

question"Don't immigrants use less welfare than natives?"

Measured per person and counting Social Security and Medicare, yes: the Cato Institute finds immigrants consumed 24% less in welfare and entitlement benefits per capita than the native-born in 2023, $8,234 against $10,772. That total is driven by the old-age programs people pay into, where natives are older. On means-tested programs, the Center for Immigration Studies finds that 51% of households headed by legal immigrants used at least one major program in 2024, against 37% of U.S.-born households. That household figure is a noisy prior — CIS's unit includes child-linked benefits and, in some tables, refundable credits and school meals — not a measure of adult dependency and not the removal test. Part of the gap is benefits received for U.S.-born children, which my removal rule does not count. The rule asks whether the named resident is supporting themselves on personally directed transfers, case by case. The franchise uses the same named-recipient / tax-unit logic under net fiscal voting bar.

question"Isn't it unfair to people who paid taxes and lost a job or fell ill?"

The rule should apply only to benefits received after it takes effect, so no one should be removed for past use that was lawful when it happened. Emergency care should never be counted, and a U.S.-citizen child should keep any benefit the child qualifies for without putting the parent's residence at risk. Residents would know the condition in advance and could protect themselves through work, savings, private insurance, or sponsors. The last-resort allowance should not be available to non-citizens (Welfare and Entitlements). A resident who needs long-term support can return to the country of citizenship.

question"Doesn't removing refugees violate the ban on returning people to persecution?"

Not when they go to a safe third country. Federal law bars removal to a country where a person's "life or freedom would be threatened" because of race, religion, nationality, social group, or political opinion, though that bar has exceptions for serious criminals and security threats. A separate statute, section 2242 of the Foreign Affairs Reform and Restructuring Act of 1998, makes it "the policy of the United States" not to return anyone to a country "in which there are substantial grounds for believing the person would be in danger of being subjected to torture," and its regulations bar removal where torture is "more likely than not," with no such exceptions. That statute takes its terms and its regulations from the Convention Against Torture, so the law ought to write the torture bar for residents into the Immigration and Nationality Act on its own terms, using the federal criminal code's definition of torture, so that it holds whether or not the United States stays in the Convention (treaty exit). Removal to a third country that will receive the person meets both bars. Federal law already lists additional countries to which a person may be removed when the first choice will not accept them. The review protects people the United States already took in to live here, resettled refugees included: removing them must never deliver them to persecution or torture, and a third country counts as safe only after review of that person's case. The two errors are not equal: a careful review costs time, while a removal to the wrong country can cost a life no later ruling returns. The duty runs to residents only. People denied at the border or applying for asylum are not owed U.S. protection, shelter, or a funded transfer (Removal of Non-Citizen Residents).

question"Isn't death for material support far beyond current law?"

Yes. Under 18 U.S.C. § 2339B, knowingly providing material support to a designated foreign terrorist organization carries up to 20 years in prison, or any term of years or life "if the death of any person results." I go further on principle. Money and communications are what let a terrorist organization kill, so I treat knowing support as taking part in its attempts at murder. Because the penalty would be death, the proof should be a documented money trail or communications that meet the capital evidentiary standard, with no conviction on inference alone. Cases proven beyond a reasonable doubt but short of that standard would go to secure confinement under compelled labor.

question"The immigration courts are already overwhelmed. How can they handle removal for every theft?"

They cannot as they stand. At the end of August 2026 the immigration courts had 3,092,988 pending cases, even after closing 899,741 cases so far in fiscal year 2026, and new removal grounds would add to that load. The United States therefore ought to pair the rules with capacity: dedicated removal courts, separate from the asylum backlog, hear these cases. They should be funded with enough judges, interpreters, and public defenders to decide cases within 180 days from notice. For crime cases, the removal hearing would be joined to the end of the criminal sentence, since the conviction already establishes the ground. A deadline without the judges to meet it would trade due process for speed, which the United States ought not to allow.

question"Doesn't this create two classes of citizens?"

No. Every offender, citizen or not, would face the same criminal punishment for terrorism or treason: death where the capital standard is met, secure confinement under compelled labor otherwise. The only difference would be the added forfeiture of citizenship for dual nationals, which would exist for dual nationals alone so that no one becomes stateless.

question"Why the focus on Islamist terrorism if the law is religion-neutral?"

Because the data put it first, though not alone. The Global Terrorism Index 2026 found that four jihadist organizations, the Islamic State, JNIM, the Pakistani Taliban, and al-Shabaab, caused 3,869 deaths in 2025, 70 per cent of all terrorism deaths worldwide. In the West, 527 of 990 terrorism deaths over the past two decades, 53 per cent, occurred in religiously motivated attacks, and almost all of those were caused by jihadist groups or individuals. The same report records the counter-trend. Religious attacks were just over ten per cent of the 1,449 Western attacks since 2007, 30 per cent had a political motivation and 32 per cent no single clear ideology, politically motivated attacks rose by almost 20 per cent in 2025, and the high-profile attacks that year included the assassination of Charlie Kirk. A law that names offenses rather than faiths covers every one of these threats.

Rationale

I treat immigration as a privilege the nation grants to those who fit its shared values, with assimilation paramount (Immigration and Borders; Language and Assimilation). Federal law already reflects the self-support condition. A non-citizen who becomes a public charge within five years of entry is deportable, and qualified non-citizens who entered on or after August 22, 1996 are generally barred from federal means-tested benefits for five years. Federal housing assistance is limited to listed categories of non-citizens. The public-charge rules for admission have shifted with administrations: a 2019 rule stopped being in effect on March 9, 2021, and a 2022 rule similar to the 1999 guidance took effect on December 23, 2022. Writing the condition into statute, with a court finding by clear and convincing evidence in every case, makes it stable. The design adapts two pledges from Britain's Restore Britain party: to deport a legally resident foreign national who "lives in social housing, claims benefits," "fails to integrate," or "commits crime" (Restore Britain – Reverse Mass Immigration), and to strip citizenship from "dual-national Islamic extremists" (Restore Britain – Make Britain Safe Again). The United States ought to narrow both: the benefits are listed, integration is a two-year test, every removal needs a court finding, and the citizenship penalty follows a conviction under religion-neutral offenses and never leaves anyone stateless (United Kingdom). The safe-third-country rule keeps the United States within its obligations against returning people to persecution or torture without granting refugees more than safety. Terrorism is different in kind. An attack meant to kill is an attempt at murder, and I treat attempted murder as capital (Terrorism and Domestic Extremism); deporting a terrorist would only move the threat abroad, where it can plan again, while execution or secure confinement ends it. I keep citizenship forfeiture as an added penalty for dual nationals so that an offender's tie to the country ends with the crime. An IDF colonel who heads the minorities department told The Jerusalem Post in 2025 that Druze enlistment had reached 85% since October 7, that Bedouin service was just over 60%, and that Christian Arab enlistment had tripled in a year, but that Muslim soldiers still number only a few dozen and Christian soldiers a few hundred. Calcalist, citing IDF and national civil service data, reported that in the 2022–23 service year 481 Arab citizens enlisted in the IDF (433 Muslims and Bedouin, 48 Christians) and 3,319 joined civilian national service. In the Israel Police, ministry data reported in 2026 show 3,294 new officers in 2025, of whom 96 were Muslim, 2 Bedouin, and 12 Arab Christian, down from 165, 7, and 59 in 2023, even as overall recruitment rose. These counts exclude the Druze.

question"Does Israel protect civilians in Gaza?"

Palestinian civilians should not be targeted. Hamas endangers them by operating among them. A 2019 case study by the NATO Strategic Communications Centre of Excellence found that Hamas has used human shields since 2007, launching rockets and placing military infrastructure in or near residential and commercial areas. An analysis in the West Point Combating Terrorism Center's CTC Sentinel reports that Hamas dug its tunnel network near and under schools, mosques, and UN facilities. Israel has issued evacuation orders and warnings before strikes, including "roof knocking," though a 2009 UN fact-finding mission questioned how effective such warnings are when there is little time or no safe place to go. Israel allows aid into Gaza, and patients are evacuated for treatment abroad through Israeli crossings: the World Health Organization counted more than 10,700 medical evacuations by the end of 2025. Israeli authorities and some U.S. investigations say Hamas often steals or diverts aid. The U.S. evidence is mixed: the USAID Inspector General is investigating credible allegations of Hamas diversion and theft, while an internal USAID review of 156 reported incidents of loss or theft of U.S.-funded aid found no evidence that Hamas benefited. The UN's tracking system reported that 88% of UN aid trucks entering Gaza from May 19 to August 22, 2025 were intercepted, taken "either peacefully by hungry people or forcefully by armed actors," without attributing the losses to Hamas. Israel's military has claimed, based on its intelligence, that Hamas diverted up to 25% of aid. Hamas denies it, and neither claim has been independently verified.

U.S. intelligence, in an assessment declassified in January 2024, said it was confident that Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad used Gaza's al-Shifa hospital complex to command forces and hold at least a few hostages, and that they largely evacuated it days before Israeli troops entered. The evidence was not released. In 2014, UNRWA reported finding weapons hidden in one of its vacant Gaza schools and condemned it as a flagrant violation of its premises. A UN board of inquiry later identified them as a mortar tube and mortar rounds.

On UNRWA, the UN's oversight office reviewed Israel's allegations against 19 of its roughly 13,000 Gaza staff and found that evidence, if authenticated, could indicate nine may have taken part in October 7; they were fired. An independent review led by former French foreign minister Catherine Colonna found UNRWA's neutrality rules the most elaborate in the UN system but in need of improvement, and that Israel had not provided evidence for its broader claim that many UNRWA employees belong to terrorist groups. After the Gaza Humanitarian Foundation began distributing food in May 2025, the UN human rights office counted at least 1,373 Palestinians killed while seeking food by July 31, 859 of them near its sites, most by the Israeli military.

Muslim Brotherhood

Position

The United States ought to designate as a terrorist organization every Muslim Brotherhood chapter shown to support Islamist terrorism or to organize violence, and keep the remaining chapters under close watch, designating any chapter as soon as the evidence shows it has crossed that line. The Brotherhood's ideology and network have produced one terrorist group after another; the record of named spin-offs and chapter conduct is in the Q&A and Rationale below. A legal political wing does not exempt a chapter that is shown to be dangerous. Executive Order 14362 set this chapter-by-chapter process in motion, and four chapters have been designated so far: Lebanon, Egypt, Jordan and Sudan (Muslim Brotherhood chapters designated). I would only designate the dangerous chapters, while watching the rest; those chapters, like the Islamist terror groups they back, are among the enemies met with proactive eradication (Islam and Extremism).

question"Aren't some Brotherhood chapters peaceful political parties?"

Defenders point out that some chapters run as legal political parties and say they have renounced violence. Jordan's Islamic Action Front won 31 of 138 seats in the 2024 parliamentary election. Its secretary general calls it an independent party with "no relationship with any other organisational body" and says it is "committed to order, the law, and the provisions of the constitution," and the Jordanian Brotherhood says it is "committed to its peaceful approach." A party front does not shield a chapter with a record like Jordan's. In April 2025 Jordan's intelligence service arrested 16 people over plots involving explosives, automatic weapons, rocket manufacture, a rocket hidden ready to launch, and a drone project. A week later Jordan outlawed the Brotherhood, closed its offices, seized its assets and raided the Islamic Action Front's headquarters, and the interior minister said members had tried to remove and destroy documents from the Brotherhood's headquarters. In January 2026 the Treasury found that Jordanian Brotherhood members, working with entities abroad, had manufactured rockets, explosives and drones, run recruitment operations and raised money illegally, and designated the chapter for material support to a designated terrorist group. Jordan's chapter had a party that won seats and a peaceful public line while its members were building rockets, and that is exactly why the chapters not yet designated stay under close watch: designation follows as soon as the evidence does. Other chapters keep armed wings of their own. The Lebanese chapter reactivated its armed wing, the al-Fajr Forces, after October 7, 2023 and fired rockets into northern Israel in coordination with Hezbollah. The Sudanese chapter runs an armed wing, the al-Baraa Bin Malik Brigade, whose fighters have carried out mass executions of civilians and summarily executed people based on race, ethnicity or perceived affiliation with opposition groups. The governments closest to the movement treat it as a terror threat: Egypt declared it a terrorist group in December 2013, Saudi Arabia in March 2014, and the UAE listed "the Muslim Brotherhood (MB) organisation and groups" as terrorist in November 2014.

Designating U.S. organizations. The United States ought to designate a domestic organization as terrorist only on its own record of supporting terrorism or organizing violence, by the same evidence test I apply to each Brotherhood chapter, and only the federal government ought to make that designation, so one standard covers the whole country.

Rationale

The Brotherhood's record is wider than any one group. The 9/11 Commission found that Osama bin Laden "relies heavily" on the Egyptian writer Sayyid Qutb, "a member of the Muslim Brotherhood" executed in 1966, who taught that all Muslims as he defined them must take up arms and that any Muslim who rejects his ideas is "just one more nonbeliever worthy of destruction." The State Department records that the Egyptian Brotherhood produced Egyptian Islamic Jihad, which assassinated President Anwar Sadat in 1981 and, led by Ayman al-Zawahiri, merged with al-Qa'ida in 2001; Gama'a al-Islamiyya, whose spiritual leader served a life sentence for his role in the 1993 World Trade Center bombing; HASM, whose 2019 car bomb outside a Cairo hospital killed at least 20 people; and Liwa al-Thawra, which assassinated the commander of the Egyptian army's Ninth Armored Division in 2016 and bombed a police training center in Tanta in 2017. Palestinian Islamic Jihad and Hamas also emerged from the Brotherhood; Hamas's 1988 charter states that it "is one of the wings of Moslem Brotherhood in Palestine." The United States has designated every one of these groups as terrorist.

The record of each chapter is what decides its designation, and the designated chapters have such records. The Lebanese chapter's al-Fajr Forces fired rockets into Israel in coordination with Hezbollah after October 7, 2023. In July 2025 the Lebanese Army dismantled a covert military training camp that included its militants; and its secretary general has pushed for a more formal alignment with Hezbollah's axis. The Sudanese chapter has contributed upwards of 20,000 fighters to Sudan's war, many trained and supported by Iran's Revolutionary Guard. Executive Order 14362 states that a senior leader of the Egyptian chapter called on October 7 for violent attacks on U.S. partners and interests, and the Treasury found that in mid-2025 the Egyptian chapter took funding from a terrorist group's military wing to destabilize the Egyptian government and that, in 2024, would-be fighters needed Brotherhood connections in Egypt to reach Gaza. Jordanian Brotherhood leaders, the order states, have long given material support to Hamas's militant wing. In the United States, a federal jury in Dallas in 2008 convicted the Holy Land Foundation and five of its leaders of funneling about $12.4 million to a designated terrorist organization. The government's evidence showed that the foundation was the chief fundraising arm of a committee the Brotherhood set up in the U.S. for that purpose. Each of these findings is tied to a named chapter or network. A chapter with no such record yet is watched, not designated.

Prisoners of war should get basic protections and treatment similar to how the United States treats its own prisoners, including labor, but foreign captives should be held in a separate military setup. Labor is my preferred placement for them. I would refuse Geneva compliance only where the Convention conflicts with my compelled-labor rule: a prisoner whom a military tribunal finds, under due process, took part in hostilities against the United States has crossed the forfeiture threshold in Section 1 and may be assigned compelled labor on the same terms as domestic prisoners, beyond the Convention's limits on the kinds of work and its pay requirements. A rights violator past that line loses the corresponding protections. A prisoner who has not crossed it keeps every protection the Convention gives.

question"Doesn't departing from Geneva on labor invite enemies to mistreat captured Americans?"

Civilians remain absolutely protected, except foreign actors who have forfeited protection by hostile conduct (Hostile conduct, removal, and entry), and prisoners of war receive basic protections and treatment similar to how the United States treats its own prisoners. The only departure is compelled labor for prisoners a military tribunal finds took part in hostilities. Every other prisoner keeps every protection the Convention gives. The standard is the same one applied at home, not a lower one invented for foreigners. Reciprocity is a practical consideration, but the United States ought not to let a treaty set the terms; U.S. law and the rights framework do.

question"If civilians are absolutely protected and beliefs are never grounds for removal, how can hostile West Bank residents be forced out, and their population barred from entry? Isn't that ethnic cleansing?"

The two rules govern different people. Inside the United States, opinions, beliefs, and speech should never be grounds for removal (Removal of Non-Citizen Residents). That rule should protect people the government governs. Abroad, a civilian should keep full protection unless he forfeits it by his own conduct against the threatened nation (Sovereignty and Borders). Hostility means conduct, not identity: membership in, or material support for, an armed group that targets civilians. Public calls for genocide or ethnic cleansing; or taking part in or organizing violence against civilians. Ethnicity, religion, ordinary speech, and private opinion alone should never count as hostility.

Why the hostile are forced out. A person found hostile has forfeited his protection against the nation he threatens, and that nation has the right and the duty to defend its people from him. Removal should be the remedy that fits the threat. He has shown, by his own conduct, that he will act to harm the nation's civilians. Letting him stay keeps an active threat among the people it targets, and indefinite detention would be a costly, permanent security state, the very thing separation is meant to end. Removal ends the threat at its source without taking his life. Once a court or tribunal finds him hostile, he should be forced out. A later offer to assimilate does not reopen the case, because a promise from someone already proven hostile is not a security guarantee. The nation's security and its duty to its citizens override the negative rights of a foreign security threat, so forcing him out wrongs no one.

Why entry is barred to the whole population. No foreigner has a right to enter another nation. Entry is a privilege the nation grants on its own terms (Sovereignty and Borders). The United States, and any nation such a population threatens, ought to keep a ban list and impose a complete entry ban, by nationality or place of origin, on two kinds of place. The first is any place whose regime or dominant factions wage or celebrate attacks that target civilians for genocide or ethnic cleansing. The second is any place where reliable polling shows 5% or more of the population openly supporting such attacks. When the people who hold power or set the norm openly back genocidal attacks, screening cannot reliably sort the hostile from the rest, because hostility is common, rewarded, and easy to conceal, and one wrong admission can cost lives. The line sits at 5% because even one person in twenty openly backing the slaughter of civilians is a risk no nation's citizens should be made to carry. Waiting for a quarter or a majority would mean acting only after the hostility is overwhelming. The West Bank and Gaza qualify on both counts: Hamas, a dominant faction, waged October 7 and celebrates it, and support for the attack runs far above 5% (PCPSR polling). The full list, including further grounds for a ban, is in Questionable Compatibility.

The list is reviewed every year. A place comes off only when both conditions are met: reliable polling shows open support below 1% in consecutive annual reviews, and no governing authority or dominant faction there still endorses such attacks. The lift point sits far below the trigger so that a small or passing dip does not reopen entry. Support should fall to a level where hostility is rare enough that individual screening can reliably catch it again, and the people in power should have stopped endorsing it. The ban is tied to measured support and to what the authorities there do, not to ethnicity or religion, and it applies to any place that crosses the same line.

Why none of this is ethnic cleansing. Ethnic cleansing drives people from their homes because of who they are. The entry ban removes no one from anywhere. It declines to admit people the nation has no duty to admit, on a measured security risk that applies equally to any place that crosses the same line. Forced exit applies only to individuals a court or military tribunal finds hostile by clear and convincing evidence, the removal standard, not the capital one. Residents who are not found hostile and choose to assimilate stay, whatever their ethnicity or faith. October 7 targeted people for being Jewish. This targets no one for being Palestinian.

Rationale

The Third Geneva Convention of 1949, to which the United States is a party, states that "prisoners of war must at all times be humanely treated" (Article 13), that they "shall not be interned in penitentiaries" except in their own interest (Article 22), and that the detaining power "may utilize the labour of prisoners of war who are physically fit" (Article 49). I depart from the Convention only where its labor limits conflict with the compelled-labor rule, and only for prisoners past the forfeiture threshold. Labor by prisoners of war rests on two grounds. Their detention as captured combatants permits labor, as the Convention itself does for physically fit prisoners (Article 49). Labor is assigned after a military tribunal finds, under due process, that the prisoner took part in hostilities against the United States, consistent with the forfeiture rule in Section 1. Compelled labor for domestic prisoners is set out in Criminal Justice & Security.

Nuclear Weapons and WMD

tldrIn short

Nuclear weapons cannot be uninvented, so I see deterrence as the main safeguard. Enemies should be stopped from getting them by military force when it can be done safely.

Position

Nuclear proliferation is unfortunate but can never be reversed, since progress will always be made with all technologies. I believe mutual deterrence is the only way to prevent catastrophe. Enemies should be militarily prevented from advancing nuclear capability when the United States can do so safely.

question"Isn't preventive military action against proliferation destabilizing?"

Action is taken only when it can be done safely. Allowing hostile regimes to acquire nuclear weapons is far more destabilizing, because deterrence depends on rational actors and some enemies are openly committed to destroying the West.

question"Didn't the 2015 nuclear deal already stop Iran's program?"

It paused parts of the program, on a timer. Under the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action, Iran kept 5,060 operating centrifuges at Natanz and could enrich to 3.67 percent. The Fordow site kept 1,044 centrifuges, with no uranium allowed there for 15 years. The main limits ran 10 to 15 years, and the 15-year limits would have expired on January 16, 2031. A deal that leaves the enrichment machinery in place and lets the limits lapse buys time without ending the capability. That is why enemies are stopped from advancing nuclear capability by force when it can be done safely. By 2025 Iran was enriching at about 60 percent, and the IAEA reported serious non-compliance. Those later facts are why a rented pause is not enough (War with Iran; War with Iran, October 2026).

Rationale

Nine states possess nuclear weapons, and the knowledge cannot be removed from the world. Deterrence has prevented nuclear war among major powers since 1945. Preventing hostile regimes such as Iran from acquiring weapons keeps deterrence workable.

infoWhat is mutual deterrence?

Mutual deterrence is the condition in which each side can inflict unacceptable retaliation on the other, so that neither has an incentive to strike first.
