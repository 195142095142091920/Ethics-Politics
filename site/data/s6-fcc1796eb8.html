Core Principles

Retribution, incapacitation, and the protection of victims' sentient rights ought to come first, with rehabilitation as secondary. I hold that those who murder; commit violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, or human trafficking; distribute hard drugs on a large scale; give or sell any hard drug to a minor; or repeatedly violate the rights of others forfeit the protections they denied their victims. Punishment ought to be swift, certain, and public, and capital punishment ought to apply only where guilt is established beyond any real doubt. Enforcement ought to be heavy, with military-style action against cartels and high-crime threats.

Helping the poor and excusing crime are separate policies. I support the first through the basic-necessities floor and reject the second: offenders are not victims of society, poverty does not excuse a crime, and fusing the two insults the poor who never offend. Defending yourself against an attacker is not aggression (Self-Defense and Defense of Others). Studies that compare siblings, and experiments that hand out money at random, find that the link between low income and crime largely disappears (data below). Critics point to the study finding that 18-year-olds removed from Supplemental Security Income were charged with more crimes. That shows a floor of support keeps some people from turning to crime, which is what my floor is for. It does not make poverty an excuse for the crimes that are committed.

noteCapital safeguards in one place

Every capital sentence passes all of these before it is carried out:

  • Proof. Objective corroboration such as footage and physical evidence, not testimony alone; a case that falls short goes to the non-capital tracks (Evidentiary Standard).
  • Recordings. A recording counts only if its source and chain of custody are proven, and capital convictions and later-found errors are published every year (same section).
  • Trial. Thirty days at most, with no death row (Capital Trials).
  • Independent panel. Three reviewers outside the prosecuting office re-examine the full record before the sentencing date, and any one can take the case off the capital track (same section).
  • New evidence. New objective evidence of innocence gets an expedited review before the sentencing date, and for life on the experimentation track (same section).
  • Openness. Executions are public or openly recorded (Public Executions).
  • Errors. A wrongful conviction brings compensation and accountability (Wrongful Convictions and Compensation).

Evidentiary Standard

tldrIn short

I would execute nobody unless the proof is overwhelming and backed by objective evidence such as video and physical evidence, not just testimony. Because there is no long appeals process, the trial itself has to get it right.

Position

I would allow execution only when guilt is absolutely clear, without a shadow of a doubt. Ordinary proof beyond a reasonable doubt is not enough for a capital outcome. The standard should require corroborating objective evidence: body-camera or surveillance footage, multiple eyewitnesses backed by camera footage, physical evidence such as a body, and similar proof that does not depend on a single witness or on inference alone. Cases that fall short of this bar should not go to the capital track.

question"Innocent people have been sentenced to death. Doesn't any death penalty guarantee that the state will eventually kill an innocent person?"

Opponents cite Gross and colleagues (2014), who estimated that at least 4.1 percent of defendants sentenced to death in the United States are likely innocent. That figure indicts capital punishment as it currently operates, and this standard is built to remove its causes. Most known wrongful capital convictions rested on mistaken eyewitness identification, false confessions, unreliable informants, or flawed forensic testimony. Those are exactly the kinds of evidence a corroboration requirement excludes as a sufficient basis. I treat wrongful execution as a rights violation by the state, with full accountability for those responsible. No human process reaches zero error, and the claim is precise: restricting execution to cases with objective corroboration reduces the error rate far below the current system's.

Abolition does not remove the error. It moves it. The same study found that "the great majority of innocent defendants who are convicted of capital murder in the United States are neither executed nor exonerated. They are sentenced, or resentenced to prison for life, and then forgotten." Years taken from an innocent person can't be returned either, so a critic who holds that any risk of an irreversible wrong forbids a punishment has to forbid prison too. The real question is how low the error can be driven, and the capital standard, the independent panel review, and the yearly error report are built to answer it (Capital Trials).

question"With this many capital crimes and execution on the day of sentence, won't more innocent people die?"

The list of capital crimes is broad, but the capital track is narrow. A crime on the list goes to the capital track only when the proof meets the capital evidentiary standard: objective corroboration such as footage, physical evidence, and witnesses confirmed by recording. A case that falls short goes to the non-capital tracks, however grave the charge. Drug distribution is capital only at large scale, a statutory trafficking quantity or leading a distribution operation, or when any hard drug is given or sold to a minor (Quick Reference). More capital crimes should mean more cases screened against the bar, not a lower bar. The case for the sentence rests on desert, not deterrence (Deterrence).

Speed removes the appeals, so the trial has to do their work, and recordings are now easier to fake. The federal courts' evidence rules committee has warned that AI deepfakes "can be extremely hard to detect" and "can be generated easily and inexpensively by any member of the public." I believe a recording should count toward the capital standard only if its source and chain of custody are proven, and a recording the defense shows may be fabricated should not count unless the prosecution proves it authentic.

The state should also publish every year the number of capital convictions, the evidence each rested on, and every capital conviction later found wrong, so the error rate is measured rather than assumed.

I don't claim zero error. A wrongful execution is a rights violation by the state, with full accountability for those responsible (Wrongful Convictions and Compensation).

  • Advisory Committee on Evidence Rules – Report to the Standing Committee, December 1, 2025 (proposed Rule 901(c) on deepfakes)

Rationale

A swift system with no long appeals is defensible only if guilt is certain at the front end. The longer the period between sentence and execution, the more the system relies on appeals to catch mistakes. The state ought to remove that period, so the burden moves to the trial itself. The evidentiary bar does the work that decades of appeals were meant to do.

Corroboration matters because the main sources of wrongful conviction are subjective. A recording, a body, and independent witnesses who agree with the recording are much harder to fabricate or misread than a single identification made under stress. The bar is deliberately strict. Its purpose is to make the capital track narrow, reliable, and limited to cases where the facts are not in serious dispute.

infoWhat counts as clear-cut evidence?

Evidence that establishes both the act and the identity of the offender without depending on a single uncorroborated source. Examples include body-camera or surveillance footage of the act, multiple eyewitnesses whose accounts are confirmed by camera footage, and physical evidence such as a body. A confession alone, a single eyewitness, or circumstantial inference alone does not meet the standard.

Statutes of Limitations

tldrIn short

In my view murder, violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, human trafficking, large-scale distribution of hard drugs, and giving or selling any hard drug to a minor should be prosecutable no matter how much time has passed, as long as the strict evidence standard is met. Old convictions always count toward the repeat-offender threshold, and restitution and civil claims never expire: restitution is owed until it is paid. Other non-capital crimes should have tiered limits of 3, 6, or 20 years: under $500, $500 up to $10,000, and $10,000 and up.

Position

There should be no statute of limitations for murder, violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, human trafficking, large-scale distribution of hard drugs, or giving or selling any hard drug to a minor. I hold all people liable for their actions, and the passage of time does not restore rights that were forfeited by a deliberate violation. Old convictions ought to count toward the repeat-offender threshold indefinitely, with no time limit on the look-back. Restitution and civil claims should have no time limit either: restitution is owed forever until it is paid. Theft, fraud, and other non-capital crimes should have limitation periods that scale with severity and the value involved. Minor offenses (under $500) may be prosecuted within 3 years, mid-level offenses ($500 up to $10,000) within 6 years, and major offenses ($10,000 and up) within 20 years. The thresholds may be adjusted each year to rise with the national average wage.

question"Doesn't evidence degrade over time, risking wrongful convictions in old cases?"

The protection against that risk is the evidentiary standard, not a deadline. A capital conviction should still require proof to the strict standard in Evidentiary Standard, however old the case.

Rationale

Forfeiture follows from the act, not from how quickly it is prosecuted (Ethical and Philosophical Framework). Federal law already sets no limitation period for offenses punishable by death (18 U.S.C. § 3281). For non-capital crimes, the federal default is 5 years (18 U.S.C. § 3282). Norway scales its limitation periods by the maximum sentence, from 2 years for the least serious offenses to 25 years for the most serious, and France sets 20 years for serious crimes and 6 years for mid-level offenses. The dollar thresholds are set below what the typical full-time worker earns in the same time. The full-time median is $64,064 a year (about $1,232 a week), so $500 is under half a week's pay and $10,000 is about eight weeks. Because I rank retribution and incapacitation above rehabilitation, more serious offenses carry longer periods.

Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty

tldrIn short

Deliberate murder should be punished by death, and so should violent rape, child molestation, child rape, kidnapping, human trafficking, large-scale distribution of hard drugs, and giving or selling any hard drug to a minor proven to the same strict standard. Attempted murder and knowingly putting others at extreme risk of death should be treated the same way, even if no one dies; ordinary accidents should not. Killing or trying to kill a victim or witness should always be capital, and most abuse within families, which rarely meets the capital proof standard, should be punished on the non-capital tracks. I want the sentence fixed for adults, not left to a jury's sympathy, so equal crimes get equal sentences whoever the victim was, and the offender's mental state does not change it. Minors whose crimes are serious enough are sentenced like adults. Offenders pay restitution from their own assets. Current Supreme Court rulings forbid much of this, so I believe those rulings ought to change.

Position

The capital offenses, attempted-murder / extreme-risk track, mandatory adult sentence, juvenile reach, restitution from the offender's own assets, and the conflict with current Supreme Court doctrine are in the In short above — not repeated here.

I would also make four cruelty acts capital when done to a person if they cause lasting injury: extreme violence. Intentional neglect (a chosen pattern of keeping it up); beating without drawing blood; and psychological abuse (withholding food, water, or shelter, not out of neglect but as deliberate punishment). Lasting injury is harm, physical or psychological, that requires ongoing treatment, does not heal within a recovery period measured in months, or leaves permanent damage - including neglect and other acts that leave lasting psychological harm. At the capital tier, lasting injury (including psychological injury) should have to be proven beyond a reasonable doubt through evaluation by a licensed clinician for humans, or a veterinarian or veterinary behaviorist for animals, documenting harm that requires ongoing treatment, persists for months, or is permanent. Below the capital tier, lasting injury should have to be proven by clear and convincing evidence, with the same clinician or veterinary evaluation. Without lasting injury, those acts would fall in the grave tier: three marks at once, reaching the threshold - execution, experimentation, or life with labor, as the victim chooses. Beating means force beyond ordinary discipline: a closed fist, an object, or repeated strikes. A swat on the rear as ordinary discipline is not beating. See Animal Cruelty Law.

I don't believe the offender's mental competence, intellectual capacity, or understanding of the proceedings should reduce the sentence, because none of these change what was done to the victim. Incompetence alone does not lead to execution. An offender who was completely unaware and incompetent ought to be removed from society, imprisoned or confined, because they are too dangerous to be around (Ethical and Philosophical Framework). But some offenders cannot be changed from incompetence and remain a threat: they are violent and show no sign of change. Such a person is a threat to society and should not be kept alive merely for the sake of keeping them alive. I believe they deserve to die. The same holds for an animal in confinement that remains a threat. See Insanity Defense.

Attempted murder should stay on the capital track: I treat a deliberate attempt to kill as murder whether or not the victim dies. So is conduct that knowingly imposes an extreme, unjustified risk of death on others, where the person understands the danger and goes ahead anyway with blatant disregard for life and rights - even if no one is harmed. Street racing through traffic at extreme speed is one example. The test is awareness and severity: the person knew the danger and the risk was grave, not merely careless. Ordinary accidents and ordinary negligence should stay outside the capital track and be handled as lesser homicide.

question"Hasn't the Supreme Court already ruled that mandatory death sentences, executing the mentally incompetent, and executing for rape are unconstitutional?"

Yes. Under current doctrine all three are barred. Woodson v. North Carolina (1976) struck down mandatory death sentences for murder. Ford v. Wainwright (1986) and Atkins v. Virginia (2002) bar executing the insane and the intellectually disabled. Coker v. Georgia (1977) and Kennedy v. Louisiana (2008) bar the death penalty for rape where the victim was not killed. The position here does not pretend otherwise. It is a normative claim about what the law ought to be. Achieving it would require the Court to revisit those holdings or a constitutional amendment. The Eighth Amendment itself does not prohibit capital punishment; Gregg v. Georgia (1976) upheld it. The dispute is over which offenses and which offenders qualify, and that line has been drawn by the Court rather than by the text.

On mandatory sentences the Court's own cases pull against each other. Furman v. Georgia (1972) struck down death sentences left to discretion. Justice Stewart wrote that they were "cruel and unusual in the same way that being struck by lightning is cruel and unusual," falling on "a capriciously selected random handful." North Carolina answered by making death mandatory for first-degree murder, and Woodson struck that down. I side with Furman's diagnosis. The arbitrariness critics rightly condemn lives in discretion, and a mandatory sentence behind a strict evidence bar removes it.

question"Won't other countries refuse to extradite fugitives who face death?"

Many will. Under the U.S.-U.K. treaty, the United Kingdom may refuse extradition for a capital crime unless the United States assures it that the death penalty will not be imposed or carried out, and the United States has agreed to similar terms with France, Poland, Argentina, South Korea, India, and Peru. In Soering v. United Kingdom (1989), the European Court of Human Rights blocked an extradition to Virginia largely because of the years spent on death row. I abolish death row, but the treaty terms stay.

I believe the United States should not give no-death assurances. A capital sentence should not depend on where the offender fled. The United States should renegotiate the treaty clauses that require such assurances, and until they change, I accept that some fugitives will not be returned.

question"Most Western democracies have abolished the death penalty. Why go the other way?"

They have, and I don't treat their practice as the moral standard. A punishment is not right or wrong because the countries nearest to us adopted or rejected it. The standard I use is the victim's rights: a murderer who deliberately ends a life forfeits their own right to life, and the sentence answers what was done to the victim, not what is comfortable for the state (Moral Psychology of Rights & Forfeiture). The real constraint is accuracy, which is why the evidentiary standard comes first (Evidentiary Standard).

question"Does forfeiture let the state add suffering beyond the sentence?"

No. Forfeiture would remove the offender's claim against the sentence the law should set: execution, the experimentation track under the rules in Section 10, or compelled labor. Within that sentence the forfeiture is complete. On the experimentation track the sentence is the research itself, and the subject's suffering in it is irrelevant: the law should set no limit on it (Section 10). They didn't care if their victim suffered, so the law ought not care about their suffering once their rights are forfeited. What forfeiture does not reach is harm outside the lawful sentence. Beatings, deprivation, or cruelty by guards or other inmates that the sentence does not call for remain rights violations against the offender and should be punished, because that harm would serve no sentence the law had set.

question"Isn't the death penalty applied with racial bias?"

The historical record supports the concern. A 1990 review by the Government Accountability Office covered 28 studies. In 82 percent of them, the race of the victim influenced whether a defendant was charged with capital murder or sentenced to death. Those who killed white victims were more likely to face death than those who killed black victims. Legally relevant factors such as prior record and heinousness did not fully explain the gap, and the effect was strongest at the earliest stages, when prosecutors decide whether to seek death and whether to accept a plea. The race of the defendant had a less clear effect. I answer this where the disparity arises. The death sentence should be mandatory once the capital evidentiary standard is met. So neither the prosecutor nor the jury chooses who among the guilty faces death, and plea bargaining cannot take a capital case off that track. Discretion still remains in investigation and charging, so charging decisions in capital-eligible cases should be audited and published by the race of victim and defendant.

question"Won't a death penalty for child rape stop families from reporting abuse, or push offenders to kill the child?"

The concern is serious. Most sexual abuse of children is committed by someone the child knows: in Bureau of Justice Statistics data, family members were 34 percent of the offenders against juvenile victims, and 42 percent for victims aged 6 to 11. In Kennedy v. Louisiana the Supreme Court reasoned that a capital penalty could discourage families from reporting an abuser who is a relative and could remove an offender's incentive to keep the victim alive. That is the Court's reasoning, not a measured finding. No study has measured either effect. I answer the second risk directly: killing or trying to kill a victim or witness is an automatic capital aggravator in every case. I'm also honest about the first. Most abuse inside families leaves no camera footage or independent physical proof, so under the capital evidentiary standard most family cases will be tried on the non-capital tracks, where they are still punished.

question"Why death for crimes where the victim lived?"

Because the sentence answers what was done to the victim, and because of who is left exposed if the offender lives to do it again. Violent rape, child rape, and trafficking leave the victim alive to carry the harm, and the next victim is a person no court has met yet. The risk is measured: in a federal nine-year follow-up of state prisoners released in 2005, released sex offenders were "more than three times as likely as other released prisoners to be arrested for rape or sexual assault (7.7% versus 2.3%)," and they were 5% of releases but 16% of the later arrests for rape or sexual assault.

The two errors are not equal. The capital track opens only when the proof meets the capital evidentiary standard, so the error left on it is small and is reported every year (Capital Trials); a case that falls short still goes to the non-capital tracks. A lighter sentence for a proven rapist or trafficker puts the risk of a repeat on someone who never agreed to carry it. No law can pick out in advance the proven offender who would never strike again, and when it guesses wrong, the next victim pays, not the offender.

Rationale

Under the rights-forfeiture principle, an agent who deliberately violates the most basic negative rights of another cannot claim the full protection of those same rights. Murder destroys the victim's subjective experience entirely and permanently. Rape is among the gravest violations of bodily integrity short of death. The response is matched to the severity of the act: proportionality in my framework means the punishment reflects the seriousness of the violation. For the worst violations, that is the most serious punishment available.

The refusal to exempt incompetent offenders follows from where moral weight is placed. I'm concerned with outward rights violations, not with the inner condition of the violator. The victim is equally dead whether the killer understood the proceedings or not. Execution still requires agency: a person who was completely unaware and incompetent did not act as an agent, so they are removed from society, imprisoned or confined rather than executed. Discretionary sentencing introduces inconsistency: two offenders who commit the same act receive different outcomes depending on the jury, the county, and the quality of mitigation evidence. A mandatory rule treats like cases alike. The historical risk with mandatory statutes, noted in Woodson v. North Carolina, was that juries refused to convict to avoid the sentence. The clear-cut evidentiary standard narrows capital cases to those where guilt is not in serious dispute, which reduces that pressure.

Restitution follows the same logic of reciprocity. The offender, not uninvolved taxpayers, should bear the cost of the harm. Where an offender is executed or serving a long sentence, restitution should be drawn from their own pre-existing bank accounts and assets, and forfeited property and assets should go to restitution, victims first. This is not payment for labor. It is the offender's own existing resources.

Lesser Homicide

tldrIn short

In my view a self-defense killing that went too far but was close to justified should be a lesser homicide with a lighter sentence set by the judge. The prosecution should have to prove intent, and should have to prove any aggravating factor for a capital charge.

Position

I treat a killing in self-defense that was excessive but close to justified as a lesser homicide, with a lighter sentence set by the judge. No duty to retreat and no location limit should stay as they are. The prosecution ought to bear the burden of proving intent for the lesser charge, and of proving any aggravating factor that pushes a killing to the capital track, not just the act itself. Self-defense itself is set out in Section 3, Authority & Civil Liberties.

question"Why not treat any killing beyond justified self-defense as murder?"

A person who was really under attack and went too far did not set out to violate anyone's rights. The aggressor started the encounter. Murder on the capital track requires a deliberate violation. A near-justified killing is still a wrong and should be punished, but more lightly, and the prosecution should have to prove the intent behind it.

Rationale

Current federal law already separates killing with malice from killing without it: manslaughter is the unlawful killing of a human being without malice. The law ought to keep that line for self-defense that went too far, so a near-justified killing is not treated as murder on the capital track. The prosecution carries the burden on intent and on any aggravating factor that would move a killing to the capital track.

Domestic Violence

tldrIn short

I hold that extreme violence, intentional neglect, beating without drawing blood, and withholding food, water, or shelter as punishment against a spouse or family member should be capital if they cause lasting injury. Otherwise they would fall in the grave tier, so each would count as three marks at once: execution, experimentation, or life with labor. Other domestic violence should be aggravated and carry an extra penalty. Capital rules apply as anywhere, and guns are removed only through a red flag court finding.

Position

I believe violence by an aggressor toward their own spouse or family is worse than violence between strangers, because it betrays the people the aggressor is supposed to protect. Murder, attempted murder, violent rape, and kidnapping inside a household should be capital as anywhere else, and so should the four cruelty acts, extreme violence, intentional neglect, beating without drawing blood, and withholding food, water, or shelter as deliberate punishment, when they cause lasting injury. Without lasting injury, these acts, and other grave abuse that does serious harm without killing, often driven by anger, would fall in the grave tier: three marks at once, reaching the threshold: execution, experimentation, or life with labor, as the victim chooses. Beating a spouse without drawing blood should be capital if it causes lasting injury and otherwise should fall in the grave tier. Beating means force beyond ordinary discipline: a closed fist, an object, or repeated strikes; a swat on the rear as ordinary discipline is not beating. These offenders sit below the capital tier of serial killers, murderers, and rapists; their lifelong labor rests on their own forfeiture and is not ownership. Other domestic violence should be an aggravated offense, carrying an extra penalty on top of the penalty for the same act against a stranger. Age of Consent

tldrIn short

I would set the age of consent at 18, except under the close-in-age exception below, and a minor cannot consent to sex with an adult. Young people close in age are exempt: up to two years apart if the younger is under 16, and up to four years if the younger is 16 or 17 and the older is no more than 19. No one 20 or older is ever covered. Violent rape, child molestation, and child rape proven to the strict standard should be punished by death. Statutory rape based on age alone, with no force or violence, should be a serious non-capital crime.

Position

The age of consent should be 18, except under the close-in-age exception below. A minor cannot consent to sex with an adult, and sex with a person who cannot consent is rape. Minors should not be allowed to marry. A close-in-age exception ought to apply only in two cases: when both parties are minors, or when the older party is 18 or 19 and the younger party is 16 or 17. The gap may be no more than two years when the younger party is under 16, and no more than four years when the younger party is 16 or 17. The exception never covers anyone 20 or older. Violent rape, child molestation, and child rape should be capital: where established under the capital evidentiary standard, they should be punished by death, as set out in Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty. Statutory rape, meaning sex with a minor based on age alone with no force or violence, is not capital in my code. It is still a crime and still wrong, and it should go through the regular criminal justice system with a serious non-capital sentence.

question"Should a teenage couple close in age be treated the same as an adult preying on a child?"

No. When both parties are minors, or the older party is 18 or 19 and the younger is 16 or 17, and they are within the allowed gap (two years if the younger is under 16, four years if the younger is 16 or 17), the close-in-age exception applies. The exception never covers anyone 20 or older.

question"Why 18 rather than 16?"

Eighteen is the line for adulthood throughout my framework: minors cannot consent to irreversible decisions about their bodies and cannot marry. A single line avoids a patchwork of ages, and the close-in-age exception covers peers so teenagers are not treated as predators. The rule protects 16- and 17-year-olds from adults, the partners the evidence links to harm: girls under 18 with partners six or more years older had pregnancy rates 3.7 times higher than girls with partners within two years (Rationale below). The two errors are not equal. A line set too high makes an adult wait until a minor turns 18, while peers stay covered by the close-in-age exception; a line set too low leaves a 16-year-old open to an adult of any age.

Rationale

Consent is what separates a lawful act from a rights violation, and I already hold that minors cannot enter certain binding agreements: they cannot marry (Cultural & Social Issues) and are excluded from assisted death. The same principle applies to animals, which cannot consent to sexual activity (Animal Cruelty Law). Federal law currently criminalizes sexual acts with minors aged 12 to 15 by a person at least four years older (18 U.S.C. § 2243). State ages of consent range from 16 to 18.

Research on teen sexual partners finds that harm clusters at age gaps of about five to six years or more and grows stronger the younger the teen. Girls under 18 with partners six or more years older had pregnancy rates 3.7 times higher than girls with partners within two years. No study identifies a single optimal gap. The sliding scale here, tighter for younger teens, is my own line drawn from that gradient, not a directly measured threshold. Most U.S. states with a close-in-age exception use a gap of two to four years, with four years the most common.

Incest

tldrIn short

I would ban all sexual contact between blood relatives, of any degree, including between consenting adults, as a precaution against reproduction. Between consenting adults it is a misdemeanor punished by an income-scaled fine plus labor for both partners. A third offense is one mark. I don't necessarily think incest is unethical in itself, but a child conceived even by accident would have to be carried to term, and legalizing it would lead to more children born to blood relatives, with serious genetic risk. Sex between a parent, step-parent, or similar guardian figure and their adult child is not valid consent. Blood relatives may not marry. Incest involving a minor is criminal under the rules already set out for child molestation, child rape, and statutory rape.

Position

I would ban all sexual contact between blood relatives, of any degree, including between consenting adults, as a precaution against reproduction. I don't necessarily think incest is unethical in itself. But even accidental reproduction, though criminal, would have to be carried to term, because I am anti-abortion and a consensual pregnancy cannot be ended (Abortion and Bioethics). Legalizing it would lead to more children born to blood relatives, with serious genetic risk, which would be harmful to those children and to society. Step family is not under the blood-relative ban.

  • Offense: Consensual sexual contact between adult blood relatives is an offense meant to deter, not a mark. It is a misdemeanor punished by a fine plus labor, with the fine scaled to income so that it deters, and both partners are sentenced. A third offense becomes one mark.
  • Scope: The ban covers all sexual contact, not just intercourse, and every pair of blood relatives, including same-sex pairs and pairs who are infertile or past childbearing age. It is a blanket rule for consistency, not tailored to each couple's chance of conceiving.

  • Children: A child born to blood relatives has full rights, and both parents owe the normal support duties.

  • Parental figures: Sex between a parent, step-parent, or similar guardian figure and their adult child is not valid consent. A relationship that began with one person raising the other carries an added layer of lifetime grooming, or developmental romance, that turning 18 does not erase. A step-parent who joined the family after the child was already an adult is not a parental figure. Sex without valid consent is rape, so these cases fall under the rape rules, and where a parent and adult child are also blood relatives, the rape rule governs rather than the incest fine. Sex the adult child entered willingly, without force or violence, should be two marks, the same as statutory rape. Forced cases follow the violent-rape track in Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty. This applies equally to a step-parent who raised the child. A pregnancy from parental-figure sex the adult child entered willingly does not qualify for the rape exception to the abortion rules (Abortion and Bioethics), since the act was willing even though the law does not count it as valid consent. A pregnancy from forced parental-figure rape keeps the exception.

  • Minors: Incest involving a minor is criminal. A minor cannot consent to sex with an adult (Age of Consent), so a relative who has sex with a minor should be punished under the existing rules: violent rape, child molestation, and child rape proven to the capital evidentiary standard should be capital, and statutory rape based on age alone, with no force or violence, should be a two-mark crime. The close-in-age exception applies between relatives the same as between anyone else. A minor's pregnancy from sex with a relative keeps the abortion rape exception, since a minor cannot consent.
  • Marriage: Blood relatives should not be allowed to marry. Step relatives other than parental figures may (Marriage Law).

Sex without valid consent between relatives is rape, as it is between anyone else.

question"What about the risk to children?"

The risk is real and large for the closest relatives. In four studies of 213 children of first-degree unions (parent and child, or brother and sister) compiled in the National Society of Genetic Counselors' 2002 review, 46% were normal; 11.7% had known recessive disorders, 16% congenital malformations or sudden infant death, 11.7% severe and 14.6% mild intellectual impairment. Those studies have serious ascertainment biases, such as young maternal age, parental disability, and uncertain paternity. In a Canadian study of 21 children identified from a history of incest, 9 (43%) had severe abnormalities. The risk falls sharply with distance: for first cousins the review put the added risk of a significant birth defect at 1.7 to 2.8 percentage points above the general population's. The rule protects the child who would be conceived, who has no say in the risk and, because a consensual pregnancy cannot be ended, would be born to carry it. The two errors are not equal: two consenting adults wrongly barred lose a sexual relationship and keep every other part of their lives together, while a child conceived under the risk carries it for life. The ban also reaches pairs who cannot conceive, the price of one clear line (Rationale below).

question"What about power dynamics and grooming?"

Sex between a parent, step-parent, or similar guardian figure and their adult child is not valid consent, as set out above. Beyond that, consent obtained by force, threat, or from someone unable to consent is not valid consent, and sex with a minor is criminal regardless. Some laws go further for family authority figures: Ohio, for example, makes sex by a parent, stepparent, guardian, or person in loco parentis a crime of sexual battery regardless of age, while not otherwise criminalizing adult incest. In Stübing v. Germany, the German courts found that the defendant's sister, who was 16 when the relationship began and seven years younger, was "considerably dependent" on him, and the German government argued the incest law protected weaker partners from structural imbalance in families.

question"Where is adult incest legal?"

In the United States, New Jersey does not criminalize incest between people 18 or older, and Rhode Island between people 16 or older. Both still bar incestuous marriage. New Jersey repealed its incest crime in 1978, and a 2024 bill (A4524) would recreate it. Ohio prohibits incest only in parent-child type relationships. Most other states criminalize it, with penalties such as up to 5 years in Florida and up to 10 in Pennsylvania. In Europe, according to the comparative survey in Stübing v. Germany, consensual sex between adult siblings is not a crime in France, the Netherlands, Spain, Portugal, Belgium, Luxembourg, Russia, Turkey, and others, while Germany, the United Kingdom, Italy, Sweden, Switzerland, Denmark, and others criminalize it. All the systems surveyed bar siblings from marrying. In 2012 the European Court of Human Rights unanimously held that Germany's conviction of a man for consensual incest with his sister did not violate his right to private life, citing the lack of consensus among member states and a wide margin of appreciation.

Rationale

The ban is a precaution, not a judgment that incest is wrong in itself. Because a pregnancy from consensual sex cannot be ended, any child conceived between consenting blood relatives, even by accident, would be born, and the genetic risk to that child is high for the closest relatives (see the risk figures above). Banning the sexual relationship prevents those conceptions rather than leaving them to chance. The rule is blanket, covering all sexual contact and every pair of blood relatives, including same-sex and infertile pairs, for consistency: a single clear line is easier to know and enforce than case-by-case judgments about fertility. Because the aim is deterrence, not punishment of a rights violation, the offense should be a misdemeanor punished by an income-scaled fine plus labor, becoming a mark only on a third offense. My worry that legalization would lead to more children born to blood relatives is a concern, not a measured finding; I found no reliable data on births to related parents in places where adult incest is legal. A parental figure's lifelong influence over a child voids consent in adulthood, and abuse of a child is among the most serious crimes in my framework.

The opposing case is serious. In Stübing v. Germany, the German Federal Constitutional Court's dissenting judge called eugenic aims an invalid objective for criminal law, and the applicant argued that people at higher genetic risk, such as known carriers, are not forbidden to have children. The Court's majority, and then the European Court of Human Rights, upheld Germany's ban as protecting the family, the weaker partner, and public health. The German government itself said genetic risk alone would not justify the ban but could support it.

Gun Crime and Violence

tldrIn short

I support hot-spot, harsh crackdowns in gang and high-crime areas, and high-rate violent offenders should be incapacitated under full due process outside a declared emergency. Riots and blockades should be shut down immediately. El Salvador-model sweeps belong only inside a declared, time-limited emergency (riots / grave national danger / completely rampant local crime), with on-scene charging, trial, and sentencing. In my view law enforcement exists only to protect victims of rights violations.

Position

High-crime and gang areas ought to receive intensive policing with swift and brutal crackdowns. Incarceration should target the incapacitation of high-rate violent offenders and gang members, with hot-spot policing concentrated where violence clusters, all under full due process, with individual charges, counsel, and trial, outside a declared emergency. Riots, road blockades, and takeovers should be swiftly and brutally shut down. I support zero tolerance for criminals. The only focus of law enforcement ought to be protecting the victims of perpetrators who violate sentient rights.

I believe El Salvador-model sweeps are justified in a declared emergency over riots and political violence like the 2020 riots or major movements taking over the streets. Grave danger to the nation; or areas with extraordinary, completely rampant crime, such as neighborhoods and parts of the country whose violent-crime rates are many times the national rate. Each such emergency should be declared, time-limited, and lapse unless the legislature renews it (Emergency Powers). In those conditions, charging, trial, and sentencing move to the law enforcement officers or military handling the emergency. Outside those conditions, the rules above stay.

Gun violence is concentrated in specific areas and among gang networks, so enforcement is concentrated there. The evidence favors targeting over scale. El Salvador's large-scale arrests of gang members under its state of exception, in place since 2022, show that gang incapacitation can be sustained. Outside a declared emergency, it is carried out here under full due process, with individual charges, counsel, and trial; in those conditions, I accept El Salvador-model sweeps. Official homicides fell to 1.9 per 100,000 in 2024, though that count excludes gang members killed in confrontations, and human-rights monitors documented more than 260 deaths in custody from 2022 to mid-2024. Riots and blockades violate the rights of people and property owners who did nothing wrong, and tolerating them invites more. Directing law enforcement only at those who violate sentient rights keeps state power aimed at actual harm. FBI national crime statistics track reported violent crime and are one enforcement-volume signal for these policies. Safety is judged by victimization (NCVS and similar surveys), hard-to-hide deaths (CDC/vital homicide), and whether people must fortify or avoid ordinary life to stay unharmed — not by reported street crime alone after avoidance.

Race is also misrecorded in the data, mostly by counting Hispanic and other nonwhite people as white. Federal standards treat Hispanic as an ethnicity rather than a race, so most Hispanics are recorded as white, and they have treated Middle Eastern and North African people as white. A 2024 revision makes Middle Eastern or North African a separate category, with agencies given until 2029 to comply. A study linking court and prison records to Census data found that 17% of court defendants and 10% of prisoners carry a race label that differs from their Census identity. Courts recorded only 15–16% of Hispanics as Hispanic, and the most common error was recording a Hispanic person as white. Black people were recorded accurately more than 96% of the time. Researchers who removed Hispanics from national arrest figures found that the Black share of homicide arrests averaged 64% rather than 51% over 1980–2008, and that the Black–white homicide arrest ratio averaged almost 12 to 1 rather than 7 to 1.

Several factors explain part of the gap, but not all of it. In a Chicago study, parents' marital status, immigrant generation, and neighborhood conditions explained over 60% of the Black–white gap in violence, with neighborhood conditions mattering most. The remainder was unexplained. Parental income does not close the gap either. Among men counted in the 2010 Census at ages 27–32, 21% of Black men raised in the poorest 1% of families were incarcerated, against 6.4% of white men. Among those raised in the richest 1%, 2.2% of Black men were incarcerated against 0.2% of white men, the same rate as white men raised at the 34th percentile of parental income. The authors, Chetty, Hendren, Jones & Porter, attribute the gap to environmental factors outside the family rather than ability. They call their findings inconsistent with a cognitive-ability explanation because, at the same parental income, Black women earn about as much as white women while Black men earn far less, and they see no biological reason an ability gap would differ by sex. Incarceration rates are very low for women of both races, so that comparison rests mainly on earnings. The study measured parents' income and, for earnings, their marital status, education and wealth, but not the ability or behavior of the parents or their sons, so it shows that parental income does not explain the gap without settling what does.

Teenage test scores statistically account for much of the incarceration gap. In the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth, Black men were about 3.5 times as likely as white men to have been incarcerated when surveyed, and about 1.8 times as likely once their Armed Forces Qualification Test scores were held equal. Roland Fryer, NBER – Racial Inequality in the 21st Century: The Declining Significance of Discrimination (2010). For murder and rape, the racial makeup of state prisons closely tracks the racial makeup of arrests, and victims' descriptions of offenders closely match arrestees. For drug, weapons and aggravated-assault offenses, which leave more room for discretion, it tracks less closely. Beck & Blumstein, Journal of Quantitative Criminology – Racial Disproportionality in U.S. State Prisons (2018). In federal cases, Black defendants received sentences almost 10% longer than white defendants arrested for the same crimes with the same records, mostly through prosecutors' mandatory-minimum charges. Rehavi & Starr, Journal of Political Economy – Racial Disparity in Federal Criminal Sentences (2014)

Antisocial behavior is moderately heritable within populations, about 41% across twin and adoption studies, but no study has tested whether genes contribute to the Black–white difference in crime, and current genetic scores are on average about 4.9 times less accurate for people of African ancestry than for Europeans. Rhee & Waldman, Psychological Bulletin – Genetic and Environmental Influences on Antisocial Behavior: A Meta-Analysis of Twin and Adoption Studies (2002); Martin et al., Nature Genetics – Clinical use of current polygenic risk scores may exacerbate health disparities (2019). Settling the genetic question, including what causes the group gap in IQ and test scores, needs research the current tools cannot yet do: within-family designs such as comparisons of siblings whose ancestry differs, admixture studies that properly control for environment, and genetic scores that predict equally well across ancestries. Until then, the data can neither show nor rule out a genetic share.

Summary of findings. Settled: for murder and rape, the imprisonment gap largely reflects an arrest gap that victims' reports confirm (Beck & Blumstein). The incarceration gap persists at every level of parental income, and the related earnings gap persists among boys raised in the same neighborhood (Chetty et al.). Disparities inside the justice system, such as longer federal sentences, are real but much smaller (Rehavi & Starr). Contested: why the teenage test-score gap that statistically accounts for much of the incarceration gap exists (Fryer), and how much father absence, peers and street culture cause the gap rather than reflect it. Unresolved: whether genes contribute to the group gap at all, and if so how much.

Arrests and known offenders are not the same as all offending, homicide offender race is unknown in about a fifth of cases, victim surveys exclude homicide, and the FBI's 2021 switch to a new reporting system makes year-to-year comparisons around that time unreliable. Every offender should be judged as an individual, by the same law.

Intelligence, Race, and Crime

No average tells anything about an individual, and every person should be judged by the same standards. Below I separate what is settled from what is contested and what is unresolved, and the bottom line says which is which.

Size and trend. On adult tests of general mental ability, the average Black–white gap is about 1.1 standard deviations, roughly 16 IQ points, though it is smaller in some settings, such as among applicants for more complex jobs. Roth, Bevier, Bobko, Switzer & Tyler, Personnel Psychology – Ethnic Group Differences in Cognitive Ability in Employment and Educational Settings: A Meta-Analysis (2001). Whether it has narrowed is disputed. Dickens and Flynn found Black Americans gained 4 to 7 IQ points on whites between 1972 and 2002; Rushton and Jensen replied that the gap still stands at about 1.1 standard deviations and that the gains came from a selected set of tests; in their rejoinder Dickens and Flynn put the current gap at about 0.3, 0.6 and 0.9 standard deviations at ages 4, 12 and 18. Dickens & Flynn, Psychological Science – Black Americans Reduce the Racial IQ Gap (2006); Rushton & Jensen, Psychological Science – The Totality of Available Evidence Shows the Race IQ Gap Still Remains (2006); Dickens & Flynn, Psychological Science – Common Ground and Differences (2006). School test gaps narrowed most before the late 1980s. On the long-term NAEP reading test, the gap at age 9 fell from 44 points in 1971 to 23 in 2012, and at age 17 from 53 points in 1971 to 20 in 1988 before widening to 26 in 2012; the math gap at age 13 fell from 46 points in 1973 to 28 in 2012. NCES – Digest of Education Statistics, Table 221.85; NAGB – NAEP Long-Term Trend 2012

At equal income. In the 2003 SAT cohort, Black students from families earning more than $100,000 averaged 490 in math and 495 in verbal, about the same as white students from families earning under $10,000 (478 and 480). The study's authors found family income affected scores nearly twice as much for Black students, and note that Black and white families with similar incomes tend to differ in other social and economic ways. Dixon-Román, Everson & McArdle, Teachers College Record – Race, Poverty and SAT Scores (2013). Hereditarian researchers read the persistence of the gap at equal income as evidence that it is not simply a matter of class. Environmental researchers read it as evidence that income alone misses wealth, neighborhood and schooling, the same point the Chetty study makes about incarceration.

IQ and crime within groups. IQ predicts official delinquency at least as well as social class or race does, and independently of both, and delinquents average about 8 IQ points below non-delinquents. Hirschi & Hindelang, American Sociological Review – Intelligence and Delinquency: A Revisionist Review (1977); Herbert C. Quay, NCJRS – Intelligence, in Handbook of Juvenile Delinquency (1987). It is not only that low-IQ offenders get caught: in a New Zealand cohort, delinquents who were never detected scored as low as those who were. Moffitt & Silva, Journal of Abnormal Psychology – IQ and Delinquency: A Direct Test of the Differential Detection Hypothesis (1988). Among 700,514 Swedish men, lower conscription IQ predicted violent convictions. The link shrank between full brothers (from −0.19 to −0.10), so part of it comes from family factors, but most of it remained, and the authors note that genes and traits such as impulsivity could not be separated out. Frisell, Pawitan & Långström, PLOS ONE – Is the Association between General Cognitive Ability and Violent Crime Caused by Family-Level Confounders? (2012)

How much of the crime gap it accounts for. In the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth, 2.4% of white men and 13.1% of Black men of average age were interviewed in a correctional facility, and holding IQ equal cut that difference by almost three-quarters. Herrnstein & Murray – The Bell Curve, ch. 14 (1994). Fryer's analysis of the same survey cut the incarceration ratio from 3.5 to about 1.8 once teenage test scores were held equal (see Race and Crime). These are statistical accounts, not proof of cause: teenage scores partly reflect home, school and neighborhood, so holding them equal also holds equal some of the environment that may drive both scores and crime.

IQ and earnings. AFQT embeds home, school, and neighborhood, and residual gaps on hours and employment remain after controls. Equalizing teenage AFQT scores still accounts for all of the Black–white wage gap among young women and about three-quarters among young men. Among year-round workers in Herrnstein and Murray's NLSY cut, equalizing IQ left Black wages near parity with White wages. A raw group earnings gap is a fact to explain, not proof of present discrimination, and equal-outcomes remedies that ignore measured skills fail on that evidence (Equal outcomes; Merit rule). Neal & Johnson, Journal of Political Economy – The Role of Premarket Factors in Black-White Wage Differences (1996) · Herrnstein & Murray – The Bell Curve, ch. 14 (1994)

Admixture. One study tested whether genetic ancestry predicts IQ among African Americans. In 2,179 African Americans aged 8 to 21 in Philadelphia, more European ancestry predicted a higher general ability score. Controlling for parental education modestly weakened the link, and controlling for genetically predicted skin, hair and eye color did not. A European-derived education polygenic score predicted ability about half as well in African Americans as in European Americans, and the authors estimate it may "naïvely" explain as much as 20–25% of the gap. Lasker, Pesta, Fuerst & Kirkegaard, Psych – Global Ancestry and Cognitive Ability (2019). The result is unreplicated in a nationally representative sample, and the authors themselves say their design cannot rule out many kinds of environmental confounding and that their SES control, parents' education only, is partial. Critics add that ancestry can track unmeasured environment such as wealth, neighborhood and treatment by others. That polygenic scores built from European samples lose much of their accuracy in people of African ancestry (see Race and Crime); and that the paper appeared in a new, low-profile journal. A critique of related work by the same research group called it "a cautionary example of fringe science entering the mainstream." Giangrande & Turkheimer, Perspectives on Psychological Science – Race, Ethnicity, and the Scarr-Rowe Hypothesis (2021)

Flynn and environmental arguments. Test scores rose worldwide by about 2.8 full-scale IQ points per decade across 271 samples from 31 countries between 1909 and 2013, with gains slowing in recent decades. Pietschnig & Voracek, Perspectives on Psychological Science – One Century of Global IQ Gains: A Formal Meta-Analysis of the Flynn Effect (1909–2013) (2015). Gains that large within a few generations show that environment can move scores by amounts comparable to the group gap. Nisbett and colleagues add that adoption from working-class to middle-class homes raises IQ by 12 to 18 points and that the Black–white gap has shrunk by about 0.33 standard deviations, and they conclude that group differences in IQ are best understood as environmental in origin. Nisbett et al., American Psychologist – Intelligence: New Findings and Theoretical Developments (2012); Nisbett et al., American Psychologist – Group Differences in IQ Are Best Understood as Environmental in Origin (2012). Hereditarian researchers answer that IQ is highly heritable within both groups, about 0.61 for African Americans and 0.72 for European Americans in the Philadelphia sample, that the tests measure the same abilities in both groups, and that the gap is concentrated in general ability (Lasker et al.). Heritability within groups does not by itself show what causes a gap between them, and a rise in scores over time does not by itself show that the group gap has the same cause.

Bottom line. Settled: a Black–white gap in average tested ability exists, about one standard deviation in adults and smaller in young children. It persists at equal family income; IQ predicts offending within groups, not only detection; and test scores statistically account for much of the incarceration gap. Contested: how much the adult gap has narrowed, how much of the IQ–crime link is causal, and how much of the gap environment explains. Unresolved: whether genes contribute to the group gap. The only DNA admixture study points toward a partial genetic share but is unreplicated and cannot exclude environmental confounding, and settling the question needs the research set out in Race and Crime. No average tells anything about an individual, and every person should be judged by the same standards.

Insanity Defense

tldrIn short

I hold that mental illness does not undo a rights violation: a mentally handicapped person who kills still commits murder. Whether agency was absent should be decided by a panel of forensic psychiatrists and a judge, not a jury. An insane killer is confined, with labor or an asylum, and treated, with a first check within two years and yearly checks after. Three straight years with no progress means execution. Progress means a decade of confinement, then possible supervised release if neutral clinicians find genuine progress and the release standard is met. The killing counts as two marks, the same as lesser homicide, toward the repeat-offender threshold: as an unchangeable threat to be removed, not as guilt. In my view "incompetent to stand trial" is usually applied too broadly. For the large majority of capital crime the approach is arrest, convict, execute, and these confinement rules apply only to the truly incompetent, which is rare.

Position

I hold that moral agency does not matter for whether a rights violation occurs: a mentally handicapped person who kills still commits murder. Whether agency was absent should be decided by a panel of forensic psychiatrists and a judge, not a jury. An insane person who kills ought to be confined. Those who are able do labor; those who cannot are confined in an asylum. All receive rehabilitation or treatment. The first evaluation comes no later than two years after confinement begins, and progress checks follow every twelve months. Execution follows only when the panel finds no measurable progress at three consecutive annual checks. If the panel is split, or the evidence is incomplete, the default is continued confinement and treatment, not execution; a single dissenting expert blocks execution, as one blocks release. If they make progress, they remain in confinement and serve their sentence: a decade of confinement. When the decade is up, a person who is still deemed viable, rehabilitated, and showing great, genuine progress, as judged by a board of neutral clinicians in the asylum, may be released back into society under supervision for a period. The burden for release sits entirely on the confined person: the panel should have to find by clear and convincing evidence that agency is restored and that the risk of violence is minimal, and a single dissenting expert blocks release. The killing still counts as two marks, the same as lesser homicide, toward the repeat-offender threshold in Repeat Offenders and Forfeiture of the Right to Life. They count because the person is an unchangeable threat to be removed, not as a finding of guilt. An insane offender whose act is not a killing should receive no marks and should be confined in secure treatment until no longer dangerous. The decade-plus confinement and panel release should apply to killings. Insanity is not a defense to the act, but it changes the outcome: a killer found to lack agency is confined and treated rather than sentenced as guilty, and the killing counts toward the repeat-offender threshold as a threat, not as guilt.

"Incompetent to stand trial" is usually applied too broadly, so people who are competent get labeled incompetent. For the large majority of capital crime, I believe the approach should be arrest, convict, execute. For the truly incompetent, the rules above stand: confinement, progress checks, and possible release after a decade. That case is rare.

question"Isn't it cruel to hold the mentally ill responsible?"

A rights violation is a violation whatever the offender's mental state. An insane person who kills should be confined and treated, and should be given the chance to make progress. Release should come only after a decade of confinement and a finding that agency is restored and the risk of violence is minimal. A person who makes no progress should be executed.

question"Doesn't current law forbid executing the insane?"

Yes. Under current doctrine it is barred. Ford v. Wainwright (1986) bars executing the currently insane and Atkins v. Virginia (2002) bars executing the intellectually disabled. The position here does not pretend otherwise. It is a normative claim about what the law ought to be. Achieving it would require the Court to revisit those holdings or a constitutional amendment.

question"If a being without agency forfeits nothing, how can the state execute an insane killer?"

The execution is not forfeiture. It is defensive force against an ongoing, permanent threat. Rights give way to force aimed at the threat itself, and the person defending does not have to absorb serious harm because the attacker lacks culpability (Conflicts Between Rights-Holders). That is not harming an innocent person to benefit another. The insane killer has already killed and remains the source of the danger, so he is the threat, not a bystander. The law first gives him every chance: confinement and treatment, a first evaluation within two years, then yearly checks, with a split panel or a single dissenting expert defaulting to continued confinement. When the panel finds no measurable progress at three consecutive annual checks, the threat should be treated as permanent. Lifelong confinement achieves absolutely nothing. It is a total waste of resources, time, and manpower, spent indefinitely on a threat that will never change. So once permanence is found, the United States ought to remove the threat from society for good, and the case is closed. The people this protects are the nurses, guards, and other patients who share a ward with a killer the panel has found will not change. The years of treatment, the yearly reviews, and the rule that any dissent means continued confinement keep an error here as unlikely as the evidence allows.

Rationale

The confinement, progress, release, and marks rules, including the non-killing case, are set out in the position and the questions above.

Moral agency is irrelevant to whether a violation occurred (Ethical and Philosophical Framework). In practice the insanity defense is rare. An eight-state study found it was raised in one percent of all felony cases, and only 26 percent of those who raised it were acquitted as not guilty by reason of insanity. The Supreme Court held in Kahler v. Kansas (2020) that due process does not require a state to adopt any particular insanity test. Under Ford v. Wainwright (1986), current doctrine holds that the Eighth Amendment prohibits executing the insane.

Juvenile Offenders

tldrIn short

I would set no fixed age of criminal responsibility. How severely a minor is punished should depend on how serious the crime was. Sentences are served as work with training, and a minor whose crime is serious enough reaches the capital tier or three marks like an adult. A capital sentence for a minor first requires a clinician's individual maturity assessment.

Position

There should be no fixed age of criminal liability. I hold that minors have full negative rights and are liable for their actions, and the extent of that liability is driven by the severity of the crime. Custodial sentences for minors, like all custodial sentences, are served as compelled labor, with the vocational and educational training that applies at one or two marks. Juveniles fall under the same rules: a minor whose crime is serious enough reaches the capital tier or three marks like an adult. I would allow no juvenile exception to the mandatory capital rule in Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty. Before a minor reaches the capital tier, a licensed clinician conducts an individual maturity assessment. A minor found not to have acted with an adult's capacity for self-control and judgment is sentenced below the capital tier, by the judge in proportion to the harm.

question"Current law forbids executing anyone for a crime committed under 18. Why allow it?"

Roper v. Simmons (2005) bars the death penalty for offenses committed under 18, and Miller v. Alabama (2012) bars mandatory life without parole for juveniles. I depart from Roper as a normative position, and achieving it would require the Court to revisit that holding or a constitutional amendment. I also depart from Miller: minors whose crimes are serious enough reach the capital tier or three marks under the same rules as adults. The rule protects the victims, who are no less dead or violated because the offender was 17, and the clinician's maturity assessment protects the minor: one who did not act with an adult's self-control and judgment is sentenced below the capital tier. A fixed age errs in one direction only, by treating every minor as immature however the crime was planned and carried out. A minor who plans and commits a murder with an adult's judgment has made an adult's choice, and is sentenced for it.

Rationale

Responsibility under my framework follows agency, and a fixed age line treats a fifteen-year-old who plans a murder the same as one who shoplifts. Tying liability to the severity of the act keeps the response proportional to what was done to the victim. Applying the same rules to minors whose crimes are serious enough follows from liability scaling with the severity of the crime, as described in Ethical and Philosophical Framework. The training provisions in Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor apply most naturally to young offenders, whose working lives are longest.

Critics cite the developmental evidence, which on average points their way: self-control and judgment keep maturing into the mid-twenties, and most serious juvenile offenders stop offending. The Pathways to Desistance study followed 1,354 serious juvenile offenders for seven years and found that desistance tracked gains in psychosocial maturity, especially impulse control. Averages do not decide individual cases, and the position rests on principle. The individual maturity assessment answers the evidence case by case: liability still follows the act, but a capital sentence requires a finding that the particular minor acted with an adult's capacity.

Civil Commitment

tldrIn short

No one should be confined for being homeless or mentally ill. In my view a court may commit a seriously mentally ill person only on clear and convincing evidence that they endanger others' rights or have lost the capacity to survive unaided, starting with outpatient treatment, reviewed every six months, and ending when the danger or incapacity ends.

Position

Being homeless or mentally ill is not a crime, and I don't believe it is by itself grounds for confinement. A person with serious mental illness should be committed only on a judicial finding, by clear and convincing evidence at a prompt hearing with counsel. The court should have to find that, because of the illness, the person (1) has violated, attempted, or credibly threatened to violate the rights of others, or (2) lacks the capacity to understand their situation and is unable to secure food, shelter, or care needed to survive. The least restrictive effective option comes first: court-ordered outpatient treatment before inpatient confinement. Commitment is reviewed by a court at least every six months and ends when the person is no longer dangerous or has regained capacity, the same "until no longer dangerous" rule that governs insane offenders whose act was not a killing (Insanity Defense). A person with capacity who refuses treatment and harms no one ought to keep that choice. Camping and drug use on public property should stay prohibited for everyone under the ordinary rules (Housing, Zoning, and Homelessness).

question"Isn't involuntary commitment a violation of liberty for people who haven't committed a crime?"

The objection targets a rule this framework does not have: liberty cannot be taken because someone is sick, poor, or unpleasant to see. In O'Connor v. Donaldson (1975) the Supreme Court held that a state cannot confine a non-dangerous person who can live safely in freedom, and I agree. Commitment should be allowed only on two grounds that fit its foundation. The first is danger to others, which is protection of other people's negative rights. The second is lost capacity: rights rest on sentience, not agency, so a person who cannot understand their situation is protected the way a child or incompetent adult is (Minors and Incompetent Adults), not punished. A capable adult who refuses help and harms no one is left free.

question"Does court-ordered treatment actually work?"

Critics cite the OCTET randomized trial in England, where community treatment orders did not reduce readmission: 36% of patients were readmitted in both groups over 12 months. New York's assisted outpatient treatment program (Kendra's Law) was linked with fewer hospital admissions: about 25% lower odds during the first six-month order and over a third lower during renewals, in an observational study of 3,576 people. The evidence reads one way: court orders help when they bring intensive services with them, and do little alone. So outpatient orders under my framework come with assigned treatment and case management, and the program publishes its outcomes so it can be judged by results.

question"How many homeless people does this concern?"

On one night in January 2025, 745,652 people were homeless in the United States, and 146,176 were reported as severely mentally ill, about 20%. Among the unsheltered, 67,355 of 266,320 were, about 25%. Most homeless people are not severely mentally ill, which is why mental illness is never a stand-in for homelessness.

Rationale

I protect people from interference by others, so danger to others is the clearest ground for confining anyone. Lost capacity is the second ground because rights rest on sentience rather than agency: a person who cannot understand their situation is protected, as children are, rather than left to die in the street. The clear and convincing standard follows Addington v. Texas (1979), which set it as the constitutional minimum for civil commitment. The evidence on court-ordered treatment favors orders that carry real services, so the state ought to tie every order to them and measure the results.

Repeat Offenders and Forfeiture of the Right to Life

tldrIn short

I believe repeat offenders who keep committing crimes after being punished should lose their right to life once they reach three marks (marks are weighted by the crime, not counted per conviction: a grave crime counts as all three at once), provided they are still active. At least one earlier mark must fall within the ten years before the new crime. The victim chooses among execution, experimentation, or, if they are competent and well behaved, life with labor. When multiple victims disagree, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim. Where the victim has died, the experimentation track is the default unless the victim's close family or friends step forward and choose, and it stays the default if they disagree.

Position

The repeat-offender threshold should be three marks: I hold that at three marks the offender forfeits the right to life. Marks stay on the record permanently, but the automatic threshold applies only to active offenders - when the threshold is reached by accumulating marks, at least one earlier mark should fall within the ten years before the new offense. A grave-tier crime that counts as three marks at once reaches the threshold on its own. The principle: someone who robs and steals again and again, and is caught each time, has shown that the community cannot be protected by ordinary sentences.

Once the threshold is reached, I would allow three outcomes and let the victim choose among them: execution, experimentation on the experimentation track, or life with labor (open only to offenders judged competent and well behaved). When multiple victims disagree, or when the victim is an animal, a judge should decide instead. Where the victim has died, the sentence defaults to the experimentation track unless the victim's close family or friends step forward by the sentencing hearing and choose, and it stays there if they disagree. A later disqualifying act ends the labor status and results in execution. Execution and the experimentation track at the threshold require that the threshold offense be proven to the capital evidentiary standard in Evidentiary Standard. Where it is proven only beyond a reasonable doubt, the outcome is life with labor. Mark-tier definitions, labor rules, organ rule, and related ops are in the Mark tiers note below - not repeated here.

question"Executing someone for theft is wildly disproportionate. How can stealing ever justify death?"

The sentence does not answer the last theft by itself. It answers a pattern. A single theft should be punished with a finite sentence. The capital consequence should attach only after the offender has been convicted repeatedly, served sentences each time, and returned to violating others' rights. At that point the question is no longer what one theft deserves. It is whether the community must keep absorbing an offender who has shown, through repeated choices, that they will not respect the rights of others. Forfeiture in my framework scales with the pattern as well as the act. Repeated predation erodes the trust a free society depends on, and the state's first duty is to protect the innocent from that erosion. The pattern is real: of state prisoners released in 2008, 82% were arrested again within 10 years, and the people who pay for each further chance are the next victims, who were never asked. The ten-year activity rule already leaves out anyone whose record shows they stopped. Experimentation and life with labor also mean execution is not the only outcome at the threshold, and the choice among the three belongs to the victim. When multiple victims disagree, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim. Where the victim has died, the experimentation track is the default unless the victim's close family or friends step forward and choose, and it stays the default if they disagree.

question"Can three ordinary convictions really cost a life?"

Not on ordinary proof alone. Wrongful convictions are documented at scale (Wrongful Convictions and Compensation), and lesser convictions often come by plea, with less scrutiny than a capital trial. A life should not be forfeited on a weaker standard of proof than murder requires. So marks still count toward the threshold. But execution or the experimentation track at the threshold requires that the threshold offense be proven to the capital evidentiary standard: objective evidence such as video and physical evidence, not testimony alone. Where the threshold offense is proven only beyond a reasonable doubt, the offender is removed from society for life under compelled labor instead. The community is protected either way. Death is reserved for cases where the proof leaves no serious doubt.

question"Won't juries refuse to convict if repeat theft can lead to death?"

Critics point to history. Under England's "Bloody Code," stealing goods worth 40 shillings or more from a dwelling house carried a mandatory death sentence until 1827, and juries often valued the goods below the threshold, sometimes implausibly, to spare the defendant. The practice was called "pious perjury" and had broad judicial and public support. Such verdicts declined once the death penalty for those thefts was abolished. That history condemns death for a single theft, which this is not. Death never follows a single theft: it requires three marks after earlier convictions and sentences, so a jury decides only whether this theft was committed, not whether this person dies. And at the threshold the victim chooses among execution, experimentation, and life with labor, so a non-capital outcome remains available. The state ought to track acquittal rates in threshold cases, so any jury that balks shows up in the data.

question"Why does the victim choose, rather than the state?"

The violation was done to the victim, so the person harmed has the strongest claim to decide among the outcomes the law should allow. The state should set the limits: only the three outcomes should exist, life with labor should require a competent, well-behaved offender, and a judge should stand in where the victim cannot decide.

question"Can a victim choose mercy below the three outcomes?"

No. The threshold protects the whole community, not only one victim, from an offender whose pattern shows ordinary sentences do not work. Life with labor is the most lenient outcome available.

The National Research Council found long sentences efficient only for very high-rate offenders. So the threshold is aimed at people who are still offending rather than those who have aged out.

Income-Scaled Fines

tldrIn short

All fines, criminal, civil, and regulatory, should be scaled to the offender's income and wealth, for corporations as well as individuals, so that a fine is felt equally by rich and poor. I'd have individuals pay day fines: the number of days set by the offense, each day worth about half a day's net income plus about 0.05% of net wealth, and every fine sentence includes labor alongside the money, scaled to the severity of the offense. Corporations would pay up to 4% of global revenue, or their gain from the offense if higher, and the CEO would be personally responsible.

Position

All criminal sanctions and fines should be scaled to income, including for corporations. A fixed fine that deters a low-income offender is a trivial cost to a wealthy one, and a fine meant to deter should be felt the same by both. The rule would cover every monetary sanction, criminal, civil, and regulatory. It would not change the mark system, the length of labor terms, or restitution, which is set by the harm done to the victim, not by the offender's means.

  • Basis: Fines should be scaled to income plus wealth, so that someone with large assets but little reported income is not let off lightly.
  • Individuals: Fines would be set in day-fine units and would be a pure percentage, with no fixed floor and no cap. The number of days would be set by the severity of the offense: 1 to 60 for misdemeanors, 61 to 360 for felonies. Each day fine would be about half a day's net income plus about 0.05% of net wealth, with a small deduction for each dependent. At the maximum of 360 days, the wealth share alone would come to about 18% of net wealth.
  • Labor: I believe labor should always be a factor. Every fine sentence for an individual would include a labor component alongside the money, not only as a fallback for non-payment. Labor would scale with severity, and would be hard on crime:
Offense Day fines Labor alongside the fine
Misdemeanor 1 to 60 30 days to 6 months
Felony 61 to 360 6 months to 5 years

Where the offense also carries marks, this labor would be added on top of the mark's fixed-term sentence, not merged with it. The labor from a fine at the two-mark tier would be capped at 5 years. The judge would set the term within the range in proportion to the harm. Minor regulatory fines involving no wrongdoing would be exempt from the labor component.

As a guideline, misdemeanor labor would run about 3 days per day fine, at least 30 days and at most 6 months. Felony labor would run about 5 days per day fine, at least 6 months and at most 5 years.

  • Corporations: A corporation would be fined up to 4% of its global revenue, or its gain from the offense if that is higher, so that a fine would never cost less than the crime earned. I hold the CEO personally responsible, as they are ultimately responsible for the actions of the company: the CEO would serve the labor component and would be personally fined, scaled to their own income and wealth, alongside the corporate revenue fine. The law would default to the CEO because otherwise corporations would establish fault-officers by default who would be the ones to do labor if the corporation is indicted. Naming a designated fault-officer would not shield the CEO. Officers and controlling shareholders who authorized the violation would answer as well (Corporations as Moral Entities). For fines, a CEO who proves they acted to prevent or report the crime would not be held responsible for it. For marks, the CEO's own involvement should have to be proven beyond reasonable doubt, as my framework requires for any mark. The same default would apply to the heads of nonprofits and religious groups (High-Control Groups) and to the CEOs of foreign companies operating in the United States.
  • Civil and regulatory fines: A regulator or civil court should assign each violation a day count by severity within the misdemeanor range, 1 to 60 days, and the value per day should follow the offender's income and wealth like any other fine. Corporate civil and regulatory fines should use the revenue rule: a percentage of global revenue up to 4%, or the gain from the violation if higher. A violation serious enough for the felony range should be prosecuted as a crime.
  • Income on record: The latest income on record should be used, and the offender should have the right to appeal if it is outdated.
  • Non-payment: An unpaid fine would be worked off as additional labor. Refusing to pay or work it off would add a mark. Marks, labor terms, and restitution would not be changed by income.
question"How do countries with income-based fines work?"

Finland and Germany use day fines. In Finland the court sets a number of day fines for the offense, from 1 to 120, and the amount of each is one sixtieth of the offender's average monthly net income after a basic deduction of €255, with a minimum of €6. In 2002 a Nokia executive was fined about €116,000 for riding a motorcycle at 75 km/h in a 50 km/h zone, 14 day fines based on 1999 income boosted by stock options. A court later cut it to about €5,900 using more recent income. Germany sets 5 to 360 daily rates, each based on the offender's average daily net income and set between €1 and €30,000, while leaving the offender the minimum needed to live on.

question"How would this apply to corporations?"

Existing law already scales some corporate penalties to size or gain. The EU's data protection regulation sets fines for the most serious violations at up to €20 million or 4% of a company's total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding year, whichever is higher. The U.S. Sentencing Commission's guidelines for organizations set a base fine as the greatest of a table amount for the offense, the organization's gain from the offense, or the loss it caused, then multiply it by a culpability score.

question"Do income-based fines deter?"

The evidence that exists supports it. In the first U.S. trial, in Staten Island from 1987 to 1989, average fines rose by 25%, yet collection did not suffer: 85% of day-fine cases under new collection procedures paid in full, against 76% of fixed-fine cases the year before. A 2026 study in The Review of Economic Studies, using Finnish speeding tickets that become income-based above 20 km/h over the limit, found drivers did not slow down to avoid the higher fine beforehand, but those who received a fine about €200 larger were about 15% less likely to reoffend over the next six months, an effect driven by high-income drivers that faded after a year.

Rationale

A fine is meant to deter or punish. Its weight depends on what it takes from the person who pays it. A flat amount falls hardest on the poor and barely touches the rich. The same offense is then punished unequally. Scaling to income and wealth would make the sanction equal in effect. Counting wealth would keep people with large assets but low income from paying little. For corporations, the fine would be tied to global revenue, or to the gain from the offense if that is higher. That would keep a fine from becoming just another cost of doing business. The number of days would stay separate from the value of each day. That would keep the seriousness of the offense visible no matter who committed it. Every fine would also carry labor, so no one could simply buy their way out of a sentence. A corporation cannot serve labor, so the person at its head would. The CEO would be ultimately responsible for the company's actions. Personal liability would keep a corporate fine from being an expense paid with shareholders' money while those in charge face nothing. The law would default to the CEO, not to whoever the company names. That would keep corporations from installing fault-officers to take the labor. The reversed burden would be limited to fines. A mark would change a person's standing toward the repeat-offender threshold, so it would require proof of the CEO's own involvement. Fine labor would be added on top of mark labor, not merged with it. That would keep the fine from being free for anyone already sentenced to marks. Finnish evidence suggests larger fines on higher earners do reduce reoffending in the short term. The Staten Island trial found income-based fines could be collected as reliably as fixed ones.

Abolition of Permanent Incarceration and Compelled Labor

tldrIn short

No one should sit in prison for life with nothing to do. I want all prison sentences served as required, generally supervised hard labor on public projects, and sentences for first and second offenses made much longer. First and second offenders would get job training, may work for private employers, who would pay for the work into restitution while the offender is not paid, and could take cognitive-behavioral programs, all without shortening the sentence or using taxpayer money. Grave abuse that causes no lasting injury, such as beating without drawing blood, would fall in the grave tier, so it would count as three marks at once: execution, experimentation, or life with labor. Lesser crimes would be one or two marks, served for a fixed term. The Constitution's ban on involuntary servitude expressly allows it as punishment for a crime.

Position

I would abolish idle permanent incarceration in its entirety. Life sentences served in a cell and indefinite warehousing of prisoners should end; every long-term sentence, including life with labor at the repeat threshold, would be served as work. Every custodial sentence ought to be served as compelled labor, generally under supervision, on government projects such as building, cleaning, and the production of goods. The labor would be hard labor: difficult, demanding work, not comfortable or easy. Sentences for first and second offenses should be greatly increased in length, so that early offenses are not treated as cheap strikes before the threshold. The training, private-employer, unpaid-offender, opt-in rehab, and three-tier (capital / grave / one-or-two-mark) rules are in the In short above - not repeated here. Rights forfeited by an offender should be forfeited for the duration of the sentence, and at the repeat threshold they should be forfeited for life. Rehabilitation should remain secondary to retribution, incapacitation, and victim protection: it would be mandatory on every labor sentence and would run inside it; it would never shorten it, and it would be paid by opt-in donations or private employers, never by taxpayers.

question"Isn't lifelong compelled labor just slavery? Doesn't the Thirteenth Amendment forbid it?"

The Thirteenth Amendment prohibits slavery and involuntary servitude "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted." Compelled labor imposed on a convicted offender falls within that stated exception, so the Constitution does not forbid it. The moral objection is a separate question and deserves a direct answer. Slavery as historically practiced seized people who had violated no one's rights, on the basis of race, and held them and their children as property. Compelled labor here applies only to individuals convicted of violating others' rights, as a consequence of their own choices. Under the forfeiture principle, an offender's claim against compelled labor is lost through the violation, just as a claim to liberty is lost through imprisonment. At the threshold it is one of the victim's three options, alongside execution and experimentation, not an addition to them. Lifelong labor, whether for a grave-tier crime or at the repeat threshold, is still not ownership. The offender has forfeited their liberty for life through their own violations, each proven in court. At the threshold, it is one of the three outcomes that forfeiture of the right to life allows. It is offered only to offenders judged competent and well behaved, and only where the victim chooses it in place of execution or experimentation. Ownership is claimed over people who forfeited nothing, passes to heirs, and reaches the owned person's children. Lifelong labor reaches no one but the offender, ends with the offender's life, and leaves untouched every right the offender did not forfeit. The state holds a sentence, not a person. The offender's labor offsets the cost their crimes have imposed on the public.

question"Doesn't the Eighth Amendment forbid hard labor, public execution, and the experimentation track?"

It may, as courts read the ban on cruel and unusual punishment. I support amending or abolishing the Eighth Amendment as needed so that hard labor, public execution, and the experimentation track are permitted. The same holds for capital punishment of animal murder: Kennedy v. Louisiana limits death for crimes against individuals to crimes that take the victim's life, and no court has held that an animal's life counts, so the amendment ought to say that it does. Treaty conflicts are handled in Global Institutions.

question"If an offender's labor is compelled, isn't the state owning them?"

No. For lesser crimes, the offender forfeited their rights for a term the judge sets in proportion to the harm, with a known end date. When it ends, full rights should return. For grave-tier crimes and at the repeat threshold, where the victim chooses life with labor, the sentence is for life. It rests on the offender's own forfeiture: it reaches no one else and leaves every unforfeited right intact. Ownership is claimed over people who forfeited nothing and passes no judgment on what the person did. Assigning work to the sentenced is not new to limited government either: federal regulation already provides that "each sentenced inmate who is physically and mentally able is to be assigned to an institutional, industrial, or commissary work program."

question"Doesn't adding rehabilitation contradict ranking it below punishment?"

No. The ranking is unchanged: the sentence length, the labor requirement, and restitution are fixed at sentencing, and no program shortens them. Training and therapy take place within the term the offender already serves. They're included because they serve my own aims: an offender who reoffends less creates fewer new victims.

Rationale

Idle incarceration costs the public heavily while giving nothing back to victims or the community. State and local governments spent $87 billion on corrections in 2021. The Federal Bureau of Prisons reports an average cost of $47,162 per federal inmate per year (FY2024), and Vera Institute data put the 2015 state average at $33,274. These averages include fixed costs such as facilities and administration, so removing one prisoner saves less than the average figure. Even so, a life sentence commits taxpayers to housing, feeding, and guarding an offender for decades, which is a burden on people who did nothing wrong.

Compelled labor turns the sentence into productive work that offsets part of that cost. It is not claimed to pay for incarceration in full. The federal model, Federal Prison Industries, receives no annual appropriation and runs as a self-funding revolving fund. In fiscal year 2025, 19,560 inmates worked in the program, and it reported $14.98 million in net income, though it posted a $37.4 million loss from operations, made up by interest and other income. Its output is real but small relative to the cost of the prison system. The stronger case for structured prison work lies in outcomes: the Washington State Institute for Public Policy estimates that correctional-industries programs return about $4,975 in benefits per participant, largely through reduced reoffending.

Longer sentences for early offenses address a gap the threshold would otherwise create. If only the threshold carried serious consequences, the first and second offenses would look cheap by comparison. Raising their cost means every offense carries weight, and the threshold is not the only deterrent in the system. Longer sentences also serve incapacitation and desert, not only deterrence, and they operate alongside the certainty measures in this section: expanded policing, swift trials, and execution on the sentencing date.

Training within the labor term serves victims and public safety. A RAND meta-analysis found that incarcerated adults who took part in correctional education had 43% lower odds of returning to prison, a 13-percentage-point reduction in three-year reincarceration risk. Vocational training raised the odds of post-release employment by 28%. Cognitive-behavioral programs reduced 12-month reoffending from 40% to 30% on average across 58 studies, with the largest effects among higher-risk offenders. Lower reoffending means fewer new victims. Set against an idle cell, compelled labor leaves everyone it touches better off: victims receive restitution, taxpayers who did nothing wrong carry less of the bill, and the offender leaves with a work record and training instead of years of idleness. The work is hard and unpaid because it is part of the sentence.

infoWhat is compelled labor?

Required, generally supervised hard labor, difficult and not comfortable or easy, on government projects (construction, cleaning, production of goods, and similar tasks) performed by convicted offenders as their sentence. It would replace idle time in a cell. For lesser crimes (one or two marks) it would last for a finite term with a known end date. Grave-tier crimes would count as three marks, where life with labor is one of the victim's three options. Past the threshold, for offenders judged competent and well behaved, it would last for life.

Capital Trials and the Abolition of Death Row

tldrIn short

Capital trials should take 30 days at most, and the sentence should be carried out the day it is handed down. I want no death row, so no victim's family waits decades for a sentence already passed. The strict evidence rule is what makes this speed acceptable.

Position

Capital trials should be capped at a maximum of 30 days. I would abolish death row as a concept. The sentencing hearing is held on the same-day sentencing date, so the victim's choice deadline and same-day execution align; the replacement judge's 30 days is the stated exception. There should be no waiting period between sentencing and execution. The sentence ought to be carried out on the sentencing date, except where the offender is placed on the experimentation track under Section 10. It is carried out first for incapacitation and finality, so the threat ends the day guilt is settled; any added deterrence is a hoped-for benefit that research has not tested (Deterrence and the Justification for Capital Punishment). Victim-choice, registry, refusing-judge, and pregnancy exceptions are in the note below - not repeated here. Nobody should be held for years or decades under a death sentence, except on the experimentation track, and nobody is held idle for life as a substitute for one.

noteVictim choice, registry, refusing judge, pregnancy

Between execution and experimentation, the victim has priority in choosing. If the victim does not respond or does not care, research can proceed, with research firms requesting bodies through a registry system. A research firm's request never overrides a victim who chose execution. The victim's choice always wins. The victim should be notified at conviction and should have to choose by the sentencing hearing. No response by then should count as not responding. When multiple victims disagree, or when the victim is an animal, a judge makes the decision instead of the victim. Where the victim of a capital crime has died, as in murder, the sentence defaults to the experimentation track, with research firms requesting the body through the registry, unless the victim's close family or friends step forward by the sentencing hearing and choose between execution and experimentation in the victim's place. If the family and friends disagree, the sentence stays on the experimentation track. The same rule applies at three marks, where they may choose among the three outcomes. A judge should not be permitted to refuse this choice: choosing should be the judge's duty, and a judge who refuses should no longer be a judge. A refusal should send the case neither to the registry nor to execution by default. The refusing judge is removed through the judicial conduct process, and a replacement judge decides the case within 30 days, even if that pushes sentencing past the same-day date. This is an exception to same-day sentencing and execution. A pregnant offender should not be executed, or placed in any experiment that risks the fetus, until the child is born, because the fetus has violated no one's rights (Axiom 3). The sentence should be carried out after the birth, a second exception to same-day execution.

question"Doesn't removing the appeals process just mean executing people before their mistakes can be found?"

The tradeoff is explicit: a much narrower set of capital cases in exchange for finality within those cases. Appeals exist mainly because the trial is not trusted to reach the right answer. In the current system, the gap between sentence and execution often runs to decades. That gap brings cost and uncertainty without a reliable gain in accuracy, since most of the time is procedural. I address accuracy at the trial stage instead: only cases meeting the clear-cut evidentiary standard reach a capital verdict. If the evidence needs years of review to settle, the case does not belong on the capital track. The current wait has victims of its own. Prisoners executed in 2022 had been on death row for an average of 249 months, more than 20 years in which a victim's family waits for the end of a sentence a court has already passed. Finality spares them that, and the independent panel review below runs in every capital case whether or not anyone files for it. The cost of finality is stated plainly in the next answer.

question"What if new evidence of innocence turns up after the verdict?"

Errors do surface late. The Death Penalty Information Center counts at least 203 people sentenced to death in the United States since 1973 who were later exonerated. Many of those cases rested on evidence that looked solid at trial, such as a confession, an eyewitness, or forensic testimony. The law keeps one narrow door open. Between verdict and sentencing date, the defense may bring new objective evidence that was not available at trial, such as DNA, video, physical evidence, or proof that trial evidence was fabricated. It is heard in a single expedited review decided before the sentencing date. If the review finds the new evidence undermines the verdict, the sentence is suspended and the case is retried. No other appeal exists; the independent panel review below is a check the state runs on every capital case, not an appeal. Finality otherwise remains, and that has a cost I'll state plainly: an error built on evidence that looked solid at trial, and that no new objective evidence exposes, will not be caught. Requiring objective evidence for every capital verdict narrows that risk. It does not eliminate it. For an offender on the experimentation track, the same door stays open for as long as the offender lives: new objective evidence gets the same single expedited review, and if it undermines the verdict, the experimentation stops and the case is retried. Exculpatory evidence emerging during experimentation would be a very grave error, which is why the capital track demands extreme proof before anyone reaches it.

question"Who checks the case before the sentence is carried out?"

An independent panel of three should, in every capital case. All three members should come from outside the prosecuting office, with no part in the investigation or the trial, and at least one should be a forensic evidence specialist. Between the verdict and the sentencing date, inside the 30-day trial window, they should each re-examine, in parallel, the full record and every item of evidence the verdict rests on: where it came from, its chain of custody, and whether it meets the capital evidentiary standard (Evidentiary Standard). If any one member finds the standard is not met, the case leaves the capital track and is sentenced on the non-capital tracks. The review is not an appeal: the defense files nothing, it adds no time after the window, and if the standard is met the sentence is carried out on the sentencing date. The same review applies before the experimentation track begins. Once an impartial AI review is proven reliable, it should be added as a further check alongside the panel, not as a replacement for it.

question"Can objective corroboration really be checked within 30 days?"

Yes, because the 30 days cap the trial, not the investigation. Murder has no statute of limitations (Statutes of Limitations), so prosecutors can take as long as they need to gather and test the evidence before they charge. The standard itself is what makes the window workable: footage with a proven source and chain of custody, and physical evidence, are fixed before trial and can be checked by anyone, unlike a disputed confession or a lone eyewitness. A case that cannot be proven to the capital standard inside the window does not belong on the capital track, and goes to the non-capital tracks instead (Evidentiary Standard).

Rationale

Delay defeats both the retributive and the protective purpose of the sentence. A punishment carried out twenty years after the crime is disconnected from the act, burdens victims' families with decades of hearings, and costs the public far more than the sentence itself. The current system keeps the severity of the death penalty while removing its certainty and speed. That combination fails on every count: it is not swift, it is not certain, and it is enormously expensive.

A fixed trial window with immediate execution keeps the formal finding of guilt by a court, which the rule of law requires, while removing indefinite delay. Organs should be usable from offenders on either track: at execution on the sentencing date, or from offenders on the experimentation track, as set out in Section 10. Combined with the evidentiary standard, it limits the death penalty to the cases where speed is justified because the facts are not in serious dispute. Capital trials under current rules have run well past 30 days (a 1988 Sacramento Bee review put the average at 79 days). So the cap depends on the narrower capital track: only cases meeting the clear-cut standard qualify.

The cost of delay is concrete. The Federal Bureau of Prisons puts the average annual cost of holding one federal inmate at $47,162 for fiscal year 2024. Across 45 states, Vera Institute data put the average at $33,274 per inmate in 2015. Every year a condemned prisoner spends waiting adds that cost again, on top of the cost of the appeals themselves.

Deterrence and the Justification for Capital Punishment

tldrIn short

Studies cannot say whether the death penalty deters, partly because the current system is so slow and uncertain. My reforms aim to make punishment fast and certain, which is what deterrence research says matters most. I justify the death penalty mainly because offenders deserve it and can never harm anyone again, not because of deterrence statistics.

Position

The empirical research on whether capital punishment deters murder is, at best, insufficient to support a conclusion either way. That is not evidence that the death penalty fails to deter. It is evidence that the studies cannot tell. The weakness of the findings largely reflects the system being studied: slow trials, decades of appeals, and uncertain outcomes. The reforms set out above (swift trials, no death row, execution on the sentencing date, and public visibility) are designed to make the punishment certain and swift, which are the conditions under which deterrence is most expected to operate. My justification for the death penalty does not depend on a deterrence claim. It rests on retribution, incapacitation, and the forfeiture of rights by those who commit the gravest violations.

question"If the research can't show that the death penalty deters, isn't the case for it gone?"

No. The National Research Council's 2012 review found that the existing studies are "not informative" about whether capital punishment increases, decreases, or has no effect on homicide rates, and recommended they not be used to inform policy in either direction. A finding that the research is uninformative is not a finding of no effect. The case for capital punishment here was never built on deterrence statistics. It rests on desert and protection: a murderer has forfeited the right to life, and an executed offender cannot harm anyone again. Deterrence is a possible additional benefit, not the foundation.

question"Doesn't the evidence say severity barely deters, so why make punishment harsher?"

Critics cite the deterrence research, which the National Institute of Justice summarizes plainly: the certainty of being caught is "a vastly more powerful deterrent than the punishment," and "increasing the severity of punishment does little to deter crime." That finding concerns severity in today's slow, uncertain system, and it does not touch the grounds my sentences rest on. They rest mostly on incapacitation: an executed or confined offender cannot violate anyone's rights again. They also rest on desert, meaning punishment matched to what the offender earned by the crime, regardless of whether it deters, and on rights forfeiture. Its deterrence strategy is certainty: heavy funding for police and investigation, swift trials, and fixed sentences that do not depend on discretion. My punishments are also far more severe than anything the research has studied: public executions, hard labor, and the experimentation track. The existing research has not tested punishments of this kind, and I expect them to add to the deterrence benefit.

question"Don't some studies show that each execution saves several lives?"

Some papers have claimed large, precise deterrent effects, but those claims have not held up. Donohue and Wolfers (2005) re-ran the leading estimates and found they were extremely fragile: small changes in sample, specification, or method produced dramatically different results, and they concluded there is "profound uncertainty — even about its sign." The National Research Council's 2012 review reached the same practical conclusion: the existing studies are not informative about whether capital punishment increases, decreases, or has no effect on homicide rates, and should not be used to inform policy in either direction. This position does not rest on a body count of lives saved. It rests on desert, incapacitation, and forfeiture, as in the questions above.

Rationale

The National Research Council panel identified recurring flaws in the deterrence literature. Studies failed to account for the effect of non-capital alternatives such as life imprisonment. They relied on untested assumptions about how potential offenders perceive the risk of execution. Their results shifted with small changes in statistical specification. The panel concluded that this body of work cannot settle the question.

The broader deterrence research points in a consistent direction. Daniel Nagin's reviews conclude that the certainty of apprehension and punishment has far more consistent support as a deterrent than the severity of punishment. The American death penalty as currently practiced is severe but neither certain nor swift. Execution is rare, comes decades after the crime, and is surrounded by uncertainty about whether it will happen at all. Under those conditions weak deterrence findings are what the theory would predict. Historical evidence also suggests visibility matters: Phillips (1980) found that homicides in London dropped in the weeks following heavily publicized executions, though the effect was short-lived and later weeks showed a rebound that appeared to offset it. Bailey and Peterson's monthly U.S. analysis of execution publicity from 1950 to 1980 found no deterrent effect. The position does not rest on deterrence.

Critics read the null findings as proof that the death penalty does not deter. They show only that the existing studies never tested a swift, certain, public one. The case stands on other grounds. Retribution gives the offender what the violation deserves. Incapacitation ensures the offender never violates anyone's rights again. Reciprocity holds that those who reject the basic framework of non-aggression cannot claim its full protections. These justifications hold regardless of what future deterrence research shows.

infoWhat is the difference between certainty and severity of punishment?

Certainty is the likelihood that an offender will actually be caught and punished. Severity is how harsh the punishment is if it happens. Deterrence research generally finds that certainty has a stronger and more reliable effect on behavior than severity. A harsh punishment that is unlikely or delayed for decades deters less than people expect.

Wrongful Convictions and Compensation

tldrIn short

I treat convicting an innocent person as the state violating their rights. When a court or pardon board formally clears someone on new evidence, the prosecution should pay compensation. Ordinary acquittals are not compensated. Officials who caused the error through misconduct must be held liable.

Position

A wrongful conviction is a rights violation by the state. I believe compensation is owed when an official finding, either vacatur of the conviction by a court or a determination by a pardon board, establishes the person's innocence based on evidence that emerged after the trial. The prosecuting authority should pay that compensation. An ordinary acquittal is not a finding of wrongful conviction and carries no compensation. Officials whose misconduct caused the conviction, such as fabricating or hiding evidence or coercing a confession, ought to be held liable under the higher standard applied to those with great responsibility. A wrongful execution should carry full accountability for those responsible.

In my view offenders lose their rights when they violate others'. They should serve a fixed labor term set at sentencing without pay. Private-employer payments for the work and opt-in donations repay the victim, and their assets are used if they are serving a long sentence or executed. Taxpayers pay nothing except through voluntary donations.

Position

question"What happens when an offender cannot pay?"

The sentence should be a fixed labor term set at sentencing. The offender should not be paid. What private employers pay for the work, together with opt-in donations, should go toward the debt. An unpaid balance should not extend custody. Where the offender is serving a longer sentence or is executed, the offender's own pre-existing bank accounts and assets should be used, and forfeited property and assets should go to restitution, victims first. This is not payment for labor. The cost should never shift to taxpayers who had no part in the crime.

Rationale

The harm belongs to the person who caused it, so the duty to repair it does too. Forfeiture theory holds that those who violate others' rights lose the claim to the protections they denied. Federal law already recognizes the principle in part. The Mandatory Victims Restitution Act requires restitution for many federal crimes of violence and property offenses, and the Crime Victims' Rights Act guarantees victims full and timely restitution as provided by law. The position extends that principle by applying the offender's own pre-existing accounts and assets, forfeited property, private-employer payments for prison work, and opt-in donations to repayment and keeping uninvolved taxpayers out of it. Most offenders have few assets (federal prosecutors classed 91% of $110 billion in outstanding restitution as uncollectible in 2016), so private-employer payments for prison work and opt-in donations carry most of the load where assets fall short.

infoWhat is restitution?

Restitution is payment ordered from an offender to a victim to cover losses caused by the crime, such as medical costs, lost income, and damaged or stolen property. It differs from a fine, which is ordinarily paid to the state.

Restorative Justice Secondary to Justice

tldrIn short

I believe the justice system is first about protecting victims and the public and giving offenders what they deserve. Reconciling offenders with victims or reintegrating them would come second and would never change the sentence. Victims should be repaid by the offender. A victim may choose to meet the offender, but the meeting would never change the sentence.

Position

I believe restorative justice is secondary to justice. Reconciliation between offender and victim, victim-offender mediation, and community reintegration may have a place, but they are not what punishment is for, and they would never come ahead of it. The criminal process exists first to protect the innocent, give offenders what their violations deserve, and remove ongoing threats. Restorative practices should run only inside a sentence already matched to the violation; they would never reduce or replace the sentence, the labor, or restitution. Victims would be owed restitution from the offender first, not a process designed around the offender's restoration. A victim may choose to meet the offender, for example to ask questions or hear an apology; the meeting would be the victim's option and would not alter the sentence. Reduced reoffending that follows from compelled labor is a welcome side effect, not a purpose of punishment.

question"Doesn't restorative justice reduce reoffending and give victims a voice that punishment ignores?"

The state's criminal process is organized around justice, not around mediation. Supporters of restorative justice cite a Campbell review of face-to-face conferencing, which found a modest reduction in repeat offending, larger for violent crime, along with higher victim satisfaction and fewer post-traumatic stress symptoms among victims. Those gains do not require reorganizing justice around mediation, so the state keeps those programs inside the sentence, where conferencing can run without reducing it. Victims are given a direct material remedy through restitution paid from the offender's own assets, and the protection of the community is placed at the center of the process. Restorative programs that make the offender's reintegration the central purpose of the system put it in the wrong order. I place the victim's rights and the safety of everyone else first. Restoration comes second, inside the sentence, and the mandatory rehabilitation on every labor sentence is part of that second place.

Rationale

Under my framework, a violation of negative rights calls for a response matched to the violation. Restorative approaches move the focus from what was done to the victim toward the future of the person who did it. The protective and retributive purposes of punishment are the basis of the system, so restoration can follow only inside a sentence already matched to the violation, never in place of it. A meeting chosen by the victim is a different matter. In randomized trials where victims chose to meet offenders, more victims were satisfied with how their cases were handled (60% versus 46%), and they reported fewer post-traumatic stress symptoms and less desire for revenge.

Police Power and Accountability

tldrIn short

Police should get more money, more officers, and more respect, but officers who break the law should be punished more harshly than ordinary people. Outside investigators should handle misconduct, and every officer who deals with the public would wear a body camera. I would abolish qualified immunity. A good-faith defense would apply when the officer, in a split-second decision, relied on training, a supervisor's order, or a reasonable reading of unclear law, and would fail if the officer ignored clear precedent or obvious warning signs. Force should be necessary and proportionate, and today's rules are only a starting point. Patrol officers of either sex should have to meet the same high physical standard.

Position

Police should be heavily funded and expanded, and officers well paid and well respected in proportion to the danger and responsibility of the work. That status ought to carry a higher standard: officers who break the law should face greater criminal liability and punishment than ordinary citizens. Funding, cameras, independent investigation, and the physical patrol bar are in the In short above - not repeated here.

I would abolish qualified immunity as currently formulated. In its place, a plaintiff should have to show that the officer's conduct was objectively unreasonable given what the officer knew at the time. A good-faith defense would apply when the officer, in a split-second decision, relied on training, a supervisor's order, or a reasonable reading of unclear law. It would fail if the officer ignored clear precedent or obvious warning signs. Current use-of-force standards are a starting point for reform, not the final word - Graham v. Connor and Tennessee v. Garner set the constitutional floor, not the ceiling. Force should be used only when necessary to stop a rights violation or an imminent threat, and only in proportion to it. Deadly force ought to be used only when necessary to protect life from an imminent threat of death or serious injury. Officers would use de-escalation and less harmful means when safe and feasible. Departments would plan operations to minimize the need for force; every use and display of a firearm would be reported. Patrol should require one high bar of physical fitness and strength for all officers. Those who do not meet it would be assigned to verbal confrontations in the field and administrative work, and female officers in this group would also handle interactions with women.

question"Does higher liability for officers conflict with heavy funding and support for police?"

No. Pay, respect, and power are paired with stricter accountability, not shielded from it. Qualified immunity as currently applied under the "clearly established law" test lets officials escape personal consequences for rights violations, so I would abolish it and replace it with an objective-reasonableness standard and a narrow good-faith defense. I treat strong policing and strict liability as parts of the same commitment: enforcement exists to protect sentient rights, and an officer who violates them is held to account more severely, not less.

Rationale

Policing is the main instrument for protecting sentient and property rights in daily life, and an understaffed or poorly paid force cannot provide that protection. The authority to use force is also the authority most open to abuse, so accountability ought to grow with power. Independent investigation removes the conflict of interest in departments judging their own officers. Body cameras create a record that protects both the public and officers acting lawfully; a randomized trial in Rialto, California found that shifts with cameras saw fewer use-of-force incidents than shifts without them; complaints fell sharply during the trial year but did not differ significantly between camera and control shifts. A larger trial of 2,224 officers in Washington, D.C. found no detectable effect on either, so I rest the case on the record itself, not on a change in behavior. The Supreme Court's test in Graham v. Connor asks only whether force was objectively reasonable to an officer on the scene, and that test alone is insufficient. State force should stop a rights violation and be necessary and proportionate, in line with the European Court of Human Rights' "absolutely necessary" standard and the UN Basic Principles, which allow lethal force only when strictly unavoidable to protect life. Federal records have missed more than half of police killings, so the current standards cannot be shown to be adequate. Wording alone does not change outcomes: California's 2019 "necessary" standard showed no measurable reduction in deadly force. Practice does: in a randomized trial in Louisville, de-escalation training cut use of force by 28%, and requiring officers to report every time they point a gun was linked to fewer deaths. A 2026 difference-in-differences paper on agencies that adopted that reporting rule found no detectable effect on fatal shootings; its confidence interval rules out large reductions but not moderate ones. That later test does not show the earlier association is false, and I keep the requirement. Patrol work can require physically overpowering resisting suspects, so physical standards are set by the demands of the job.

infoWhat is qualified immunity?

Qualified immunity is a judge-made doctrine that shields government officials from personal civil liability under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 unless their conduct violated "clearly established" statutory or constitutional rights that a reasonable person would have known. It grew from Pierson v. Ray (1967) and took its modern form in Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982). It concerns civil suits, not criminal prosecution.

Police and Law Enforcement

tldrIn short

I want police to get more money, more officers, and more respect, and officers who break the law should be punished more than ordinary citizens. Body cameras ought to be required everywhere. The worst crime areas would get militarized enforcement under zero tolerance, and theft and burglary should be treated severely.

Position

Police should be heavily funded and expanded, and the responsibilities and dangers of the job very well paid and well respected. Officers who break laws ought to be held to higher criminal liability and punishment than regular citizens, and the same should apply to elected officials. I would mandate body cameras nationwide. Ultra-high-crime areas should receive militarized police enforcement, and high-crime areas increased enforcement with proper protections and punishments to destroy crime, under zero tolerance. Theft and burglary ought to be treated severely, and any erosion of a high-trust society by criminals and bad actors is addressed harshly. The full policing framework is set out in Criminal Justice & Security.

question"Does militarized policing threaten civil liberties?"

Militarized enforcement should be limited to ultra-high-crime areas and comes with proper protections and punishments, including body cameras and higher criminal liability for officers who break the law. Criminals infringe on the sentient rights of others, and their victims are owed the utmost protection under the law. The civil liberties at stake are first of all the victims'.

Rationale

Criminals infringe on the sentient rights of others, and their victims deserve the utmost protection under the law. A force with the funding, numbers, and standing to do that job is a precondition for the rest of my framework. Each additional police officer prevents an estimated 0.06 to 0.1 homicides, roughly one life saved for every 10 to 17 officers hired, and in per capita terms the lives saved are about twice as large for Black victims as for White victims. The same study found that added officers make more low-level "quality-of-life" arrests, with per capita increases 2.5 to 3 times larger for Black civilians, while arrests for serious index crimes fall, with per capita drops 4 to 6 times larger for Black arrestees. More enforcement of low-level offenses is part of what a larger force is for: it is the harsh response to erosion of a high-trust society that this framework calls for. Pairing that power with higher liability and recorded interactions keeps it accountable. Concentrating the heaviest enforcement where crime is worst directs state power at the places where rights violations actually happen.
