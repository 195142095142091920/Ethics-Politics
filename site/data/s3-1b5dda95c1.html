Core Principles

I stand for strong individual liberties and property rights, with government limited to protecting sentient rights, public safety, and order. I want opt-in mechanisms where possible and harsh accountability for criminals and officials, and I am skeptical of overreach but support effective enforcement.

Individual Liberty vs State Power

tldrIn short

I believe the government should leave people alone unless they are actively harming others or about to do so. That is my core rule.

Speech should stay free except for real threats and calls to immediate violence. Being watched while you are out in public is normal. Being tracked everywhere by permanent databases is not something we should accept lightly.

Liberty comes first. The state ought to step in only to stop rights violations, and it has to justify every step it takes.

Position

The state may use force only when it is necessary to stop people from violating the negative rights of others - mainly force, fraud, or the direct imposition of serious non-consensual harm. Liberty ought to be the default. Any mandate, speech restriction, or surveillance power should have to clear a high bar: it must be necessary, narrowly aimed at the actual problem, and temporary when the threat is temporary. Personal choices end when they cross into violating someone else’s rights, not merely when they create some diffuse or indirect effect.

question"Doesn't almost every choice affect someone else? Where does that leave the line?"

Almost every choice has some effect on others, which is why indirect effect is not the test. The test is whether the choice violates someone's negative rights through force, fraud, or the direct imposition of serious non-consensual harm. A choice that others dislike, or that changes a social environment they would prefer to keep, does not meet that test. A choice that injures, defrauds, or directly endangers someone does.

Rationale

I treat rights as side-constraints, not as goals that can be balanced away whenever a net benefit appears. The state does not get to override liberty simply because a policy is believed to be good for people overall. It acts when one person is violating, or is about to violate, another’s rights. The practical test used is simple: Is someone violating (or imminently about to violate) another’s negative rights? Is the response necessary and proportionate? If the answer to either question is no, the state should stay out.

Speech

Speech follows the same logic. The line is drawn at true threats and incitement to imminent lawless action. Once the state starts policing ideology, offense, or “misinformation,” it is no longer protecting rights, it is managing thought. That is incompatible with free inquiry and a limited role for government. The excluded-ideologies rule polices no one's speech or thought: it governs the state's own positions and the building of institutions, under one published test (Excluded ideologies).

Surveillance

Public surveillance sits in a middle position. There is no reasonable expectation of privacy against ordinary observation while walking down the street. Continuous bulk tracking and permanent facial-recognition databases are different; they concentrate power and change the relationship between citizen and state even if no traditional “search” occurs. NIST testing of face-recognition algorithms found false-positive rates that often differed by factors of 10 to 100 across demographic groups. I accept normal public-order policing while requiring tighter limits on the more sweeping technological versions.

infoWhat does proportionate response mean?

The force or punishment used in reply to a rights violation is matched to the severity and nature of the violation: enough to stop it, deter it, and reflect the seriousness of what was done, but not wildly excessive relative to the harm.

Government Structure and Checks

tldrIn short

In my view government exists to protect rights and must justify every expansion. The branches should stay separate, new rules carry the burden of proof, and agencies cannot go beyond the laws that authorize them.

Position

I hold government power to the same test applied to every use of coercion: it exists to protect sentient rights and must justify each extension. Separated legislative, executive, and judicial powers should be retained so that no single branch can expand state authority unchecked. The burden of proof ought to rest on any new regulation, not on the people regulated, and administrative rules should carry no more authority than the statutes that authorize them. Emergency authority should lapse automatically unless the legislature renews it. When a federal action is unconstitutional, the ruling should protect everyone's rights the same way, through class actions or fast Supreme Court review, so a right never depends on which district a person lives in. Single district judges should not set national policy by themselves.

question"Doesn't a limited government struggle to act when needed?"

No. A limited government acts decisively within its core functions: defense, policing, courts, and the protection of rights. The limits apply to expansion beyond those functions, which should be justified rather than presumed.

Rationale

The Constitution vests legislative power in Congress, executive power in the President, and judicial power in the courts (Articles I–III). In Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024) the Supreme Court overruled Chevron deference, holding that courts must exercise independent judgment on whether an agency acted within its statutory authority. That aligns with the rule that the burden of justification rests on the regulator, set out in Domestic Policy & Governance.

Term Limits

tldrIn short

I oppose term limits on any elected office. Every elected office should run on a three-year cycle, with re-election on the table based on performance. Federal offices need a constitutional amendment to match that structure, including the presidency and Congress.

Position

I oppose term limits on any elected office. Re-election should always be on the table based on performance: if they do a good job, voters can re-elect them and they continue; bad performance ends it. Somebody who is successful can stay indefinitely based on the people's want. This applies to all elected offices: the presidency, Congress (representatives and senators), governors, and every other elected position. Election cycles should be three years for every elected office. Keeping re-election always available kills lame-duck corruption, because the incentive to stay clean never drops; there is always another election coming.

question"Don't term limits stop career politicians and corruption?"

No. Officials who cannot run again are lame ducks, and the accountability evidence runs the other way: Brazilian mayors who could seek re-election diverted about 27% fewer resources than mayors in their final term. Studies of U.S. state legislative term limits find turnover and a shift of influence toward governors, agencies, and lobbyists rather than a proven drop in corruption. Accountability comes from voters facing another election on a short cycle, not from a hard exit date.

question"What about presidents who refuse to leave?"

Supporters of limits point to evasion: of 234 presidents in 106 countries who reached the end of their term limit since 2000, at least a third tried to stay on. That record argues for my system. Courts mostly failed to stop those attempts, and the ones that failed were stopped mainly by popular resistance, the same check a three-year election cycle puts in voters' hands at every election. The United States ought to keep that check live rather than create the lame-duck final term in which the audit evidence above finds more misuse of funds.

question"Doesn't the Twenty-Second Amendment already limit the presidency?"

Yes under current law. This position replaces that structure: the presidency, like every other elected office, has no term limit and runs on a three-year cycle. Putting that in force for federal offices requires constitutional amendment, including changing or repealing the Twenty-Second Amendment and aligning House and Senate terms with the three-year cycle. Under U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton (1995), states cannot add congressional term limits on their own.

question"Does the whole Senate turn over at once, and what about judges and state offices?"

No. Each Senate term lasts three years. The Senate stays staggered in thirds, with one class up every year on the three-year cycle, so there is always a fresh electoral check without the whole chamber turning over at once. Elected judges are on the same three-year cycle. States adopt the three-year cycle by changing their state constitutions, starting at the next regular election after adoption.

Rationale

Accountability that never turns off beats a forced last term. Ferraz and Finan (2011) found Brazilian mayors eligible for re-election misappropriated about 27% fewer resources than lame-duck mayors in randomized federal audits. Alt, Bueno de Mesquita, and Rose (2011) found U.S. governors eligible for re-election outperformed term-limited governors on growth, taxes, spending, and borrowing costs. State legislative term-limit studies find higher turnover and a shift of influence toward governors and the bureaucracy, not a measured anti-corruption gain. So I reject adding term limits and shorten the cycle to three years so voters can act while the incentive to stay clean is still live. Changing presidential and congressional terms requires amendment; Thornton bars states from adding congressional qualifications on their own.

Federalism

tldrIn short

In my view defense, borders, and immigration belong at the federal level. Schools should be local with per-child state funding. Rights must apply the same everywhere, and programs ought to be opt-in rather than imposed from above.

Position

Powers ought to be placed at the level closest to the people that can carry them out. National defense, foreign policy, immigration, border enforcement, and national election-integrity standards (ID, authentication, counting method) should be federal; states and localities should not be able to obstruct them, which is why I would abolish sanctuary policies. Schools should be run locally, with each state funding about $10,000 per child that follows the child to any public school in the state, and the federal Department of Education abolished. Programs funded by opt-in money follow the same principle: participation is chosen, not imposed from the top. Policing should be local by default. The federal government should reinforce or take over local police only where local authorities fail to stop violent crime or riots, under a time-limited emergency that Congress must renew. The District of Columbia, as a federal district, is Congress's direct responsibility.

question"Doesn't local control produce unequal outcomes between places?"

Some variation is expected and acceptable. Rights protections apply equally everywhere. What varies is how localities choose to deliver services, and people and families can move or choose among providers.

Rationale

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states or the people the powers not delegated to the United States. Immigration and naturalization are federal powers under Article I, Section 8, and in Arizona v. United States (2012) the Supreme Court held that federal immigration law preempts conflicting state measures. The education structure is set out in Domestic Policy & Governance.

Emergency Powers

tldrIn short

Emergency powers must be necessary, narrow, and temporary. I would have them expire automatically unless the legislature votes to renew them, keep free speech working, and stop the state from keeping extra powers once the danger passes. During an emergency, charging, trial, and sentencing should move to the law enforcement officers or military handling it, so the people caught inside it are protected while it lasts.

Position

I hold emergency powers to the same test as any mandate: necessary, narrowly aimed at the actual threat, and temporary. Every emergency declaration and every power exercised under it should expire automatically on a fixed date unless the legislature votes to renew it. Renewal ought to require a fresh justification on current evidence. In an emergency, charging, trial, and sentencing move to the law enforcement officers or military handling it (Gun Crime and Violence). In a declared state of emergency involving riots or violent crime that local policing cannot contain, the military and police handling it may impose and carry out a death sentence on the spot. The emergency does not lower the capital evidentiary standard: the officer should have to be certain of the act to that standard before acting. Every on-the-spot sentence should be recorded, including body-camera footage, and an ordinary civilian court should review the record after the emergency ends; an officer who carried out a sentence the record does not support ought to be charged as for murder. Speech should stay protected except for true threats and incitement to imminent violence. I reject open-ended emergency powers and the permanent expansion of state authority after the acute danger has passed.

My view: Emergencies add latitude: in an emergency, charging, trial, and sentencing move to the law enforcement officers or military handling it.

question"Can a government respond fast enough to a real crisis under these limits?"

Yes. The executive can act immediately when a severe, direct threat appears. The limits govern duration, not speed: the powers lapse on their own unless the legislature affirmatively renews them, so extraordinary authority cannot continue by inertia.

question"How are on-the-spot death sentences necessary and narrow?"

They should be allowed only inside a declared emergency over riots or violent crime that local policing cannot contain, and that emergency expires on a fixed date unless the legislature votes to renew it, so the power ends with the danger. It is narrow in who it reaches: only a person the officer is certain, to the full capital evidentiary standard, committed a capital act in front of the emergency response. The emergency speeds up the process, not the standard of proof. It is checked after the fact: every case is recorded on body camera, an ordinary civilian court reviews it once the emergency ends, the recording is published, and an officer whose sentence the record does not support is charged as for murder. A high-crime area that has no declared emergency gets intensive policing and full due process, not on-the-spot sentences.

The standard is stricter than today's law. Under Tennessee v. Garner (1985), an officer may already use deadly force against a fleeing suspect on "probable cause to believe that the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury." My rule requires certainty to the capital standard, a recording, and a murder charge where the record falls short. The people it protects are the ones a riot reaches first: residents, shopkeepers, and bystanders who cannot leave and cannot wait for order to return. The two errors are not equal. An officer who acts without a record to support it is charged as for murder, while an offender who kills in front of the response and is left free to keep going takes lives that no later trial gives back. An officer certain to the full standard can still be wrong, and an on-the-spot sentence cannot be undone; the recording, the civilian review, and the murder charge keep that rare and punish it.

Places of Worship in Emergencies

An emergency does not let the government close places of worship or ban gatherings in them as a class. Ordinary laws still apply to any building, such as fire codes, structural danger and active crime scenes. Worship may not be treated worse than comparable secular gatherings, and no general emergency order may single it out. If I were voting on Wisconsin Question 2, I would vote yes.

Rationale

Under the National Emergencies Act, a declared emergency ends only by joint resolution or presidential proclamation, or if the President fails to renew it each year; Congress is directed to meet every six months to consider termination. The default favors continuation, and emergencies accumulate: as of October 2, 2026, 51 declared national emergencies were in effect. Automatic expiry with legislative renewal reverses the default, so the burden rests on continuing a power rather than on ending it.

Property Rights

tldrIn short

I believe that once ownership is properly established, it is meant to last. The state may take property only for narrow public purposes and must pay full value. Ordinary zoning can exist, but rules that destroy the real value of ownership without payment should be treated as takings. Two cases fall outside that protection: one is barred by amendment, and in the other the owners forfeited it. Under the excluded-ideologies amendment, which uses the categories of the oath standard, places promoting anti-American or anti-constitutional causes, such as Nazi, communist, socialist, or Islamic sites, are barred from being built, rebuilt, expanded, or relocated, and no new mosques may be built (Excluded ideologies). Under the Sanctuary Plan, those industries would be shut down at once, with no compensation to owners or shareholders, because animal-industry firms forfeited their property protections by using their assets to violate animals' rights. That forfeiture is one-time, and the general property rule covers everything else (Sanctuary forfeiture).

Intellectual property should be protected for practical reasons even though it rests on thinner foundations than physical property.

Owners should be able to use necessary force to stop theft or intrusion. Lethal force is available only when the intrusion is violent or carries a clear threat to persons or the security of the home. Pure non-violent theft is handled through restitution and criminal penalties, not lethal force. As population grows and time passes, adjustment should happen through voluntary exchange, denser development, and new value creation - not through political resetting of existing titles. I treat secure property rights as a basic condition for investment, agency, and a high-trust society.

Position

I rank property rights among the strongest negative rights. Once ownership is legitimately acquired, it is permanent and may be defended with proportionate force. Eminent domain should be limited to narrow public-use cases with full compensation. I don't treat ordinary zoning as a taking, but regulation that eliminates the economically beneficial uses of property, or destroys the great majority of its value, is a taking and should require compensation. The first exception is the bar on building, rebuilding, expanding, or relocating places that promote anti-American or anti-constitutional causes, the four excluded ideologies of the oath standard - Nazism, communism, socialism, and Islam, all on the same terms; examples are a Nazi gathering place with its symbols, an anti-American communist stronghold, or an Islamic center or school; new mosques are also barred outright. The bar falls on the place and the symbols built into it, never on a person's belief, speech, or home practice (Excluded ideologies; Oath of Office and Perjury).

The second is the Sanctuary Plan: those industries should be compelled to shut down their animal operations, with no compensation to owners or shareholders. Animal-industry firms forfeited their property protections by using their assets to violate animals' rights, so this is a one-time forfeiture, not a break in the property right. The full scope, conversion, land, levy, and worker rules are set out in the Sanctuary Plan and in Sanctuary forfeiture. The general rule still stands for all other property.

Intellectual property's foundations are thinner than physical property's, but I would protect it for practical reasons. As population grows and time passes, adjustment occurs through voluntary exchange, development, and new value creation rather than political resetting of titles.

question"If ownership is permanent, doesn't that lock in past injustices and leave later generations with nothing?"

Permanence applies to legitimately acquired property. A title that rests on force or fraud was never legitimate, and the rights violation behind it can be answered through ordinary legal process. Later generations are not left with nothing: as population grows, adjustment comes through voluntary exchange, development, and new value creation. What I reject is political redistribution of titles that were legitimately acquired.

question"Can an owner use property to harm the neighbors?"

No. Property is a negative right and stops where it violates others' rights. Pollution that harms others or imposes a severe, non-consensual risk of serious harm on them makes the polluter liable (Pollution and Liability).

question"Why protect intellectual property if its foundations are thinner?"

It is protected for practical reasons of incentive and coordination, and subject to reasonable limits, rather than as a natural right equal to physical property.

noteWhy no legal objection applies

My excluded-ideologies amendment covers Nazism, communism, socialism, and Islam, the four doctrines that meet the test in Excluded ideologies, but only for the bars on building, rebuilding, expanding, or relocating places and on holding office. For those bars it sets aside the First Amendment's speech and free exercise protections and the Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act (RLUIPA), and it blocks any objection on viewpoint-discrimination or religious-liberty grounds. A constitutional amendment overrides any earlier provision or statute that conflicts with it, so no such objection can be raised against these bars. Everywhere else, speech and belief stay protected: holding or peacefully advocating any of these views carries no legal penalty.

Rationale

Secure property rights protect the ability to plan, invest, and build without constant fear that what has been created can be taken by force or by political decision. Evidence across countries and centuries shows that clearer, more stable ownership tends to produce more investment and higher incomes. Where ownership is insecure, people invest less, informal arrangements multiply, and conflict over resources becomes more common.

The idea that all land begins as government property until it is sold is set aside. The state’s role is to recognize and defend legitimate holdings once they exist, not to act as the original owner of every piece of territory. Once title is justly acquired, permanence is the default. Permanence does not freeze use. Owners remain free to sell, lease, subdivide, or redevelop. As population grows, rising demand raises the return to more intensive use. Land held in low-value ways faces growing market pressure to change hands or change use. Ownership protects the right to decide. It does not protect any particular use from competition.

infoWhat counts as legitimate holdings / just acquisition?

Legitimate (or “just”) acquisition means the holding was obtained without violating others’ negative rights. The main routes are:

  • First acquisition of something previously unowned (classic homesteading / effective occupancy and improvement).
  • Voluntary transfer from a prior legitimate owner (sale, gift, inheritance, exchange).
  • Rectification in limited cases where a clear prior rights violation can be traced and corrected (stolen property returned, etc.).

What is not legitimate acquisition:

  • Conquest or seizure by force
  • Fraud
  • Political grant that simply takes from one private party to give to another
  • “We voted to take it” without a narrow public-use justification and full compensation

I do not require a perfect historical genealogy back to the first human. In practice it operates with a strong presumption in favor of long-settled, currently recognized titles and a high bar for disrupting them. The key test is whether the current claim rests on a chain of voluntary transfers or original appropriation rather than on ongoing aggression or pure political allocation.

Eminent domain stays an exception. It is acceptable only for clear public-use projects and only with full compensation and strong safeguards. Expanding it to private economic development or general revenue goals turns a limited tool into a political transfer mechanism. I reject that expansion.

Zoning sits in the middle. Some basic coordination rules can reduce conflict without destroying ownership. When regulation becomes so heavy that formal title remains while the economically beneficial uses of the property are eliminated, or the great majority of its value is destroyed, the regulation functions as a taking and requires compensation.

Intellectual property rests on weaker ground than physical property. Ideas can be used by many people at once, and the boundaries are conventional. Protection is still maintained because limited exclusivity gives people reason to create and helps coordinate complex production. Because the justification is practical, the length and scope of protection remain open to evidence and adjustment.

Defense of property would follow ordinary proportionality. An owner should be allowed to use the force needed to stop the violation. Lethal force would be justified when the intrusion is violent or carries a clear threat to persons or the security of the home. Pure non-violent theft or conversion would not authorize lethal force. It would be handled through restitution and scaled criminal penalties. Once the immediate threat ends, the response would shift to recovery of the loss and proportionate punishment. This limit would govern an owner's immediate defense. The state's sentencing of repeat offenders after conviction is a separate matter, set out in Criminal Justice & Security. Proportionality would be applied at that forfeiture decision, not again within the sentence (Section 10).

As time passes and population increases, the state does not promise every person a land allotment. It keeps peaceful acquisition and voluntary exchange open, and it refuses to treat existing ownership as a pool that may be reallocated whenever demographic pressure rises. Larger populations are supported by denser development, technological improvement, and the creation of new forms of value - all of which function better when ownership is secure. Societies that have maintained relatively stable property rights while allowing markets in land and housing have generally absorbed population growth with rising average living standards. Systems that make ownership politically revisable in the name of access have more often produced both insecurity and poorer overall results.

Strong property rights shrink the realm of political allocation, encourage investment, and support a high-trust order. Weakening them for short-term flexibility or redistributive convenience reintroduces the uncertainty the institution exists to limit.

Self-Defense and Defense of Others

tldrIn short

I believe people may use force to protect themselves or others anywhere, with no duty to retreat. An aggravated, avoidable killing is murder and goes to the mandatory capital track. Near-justified self-defense should be a lesser homicide with a lighter sentence set by the judge.

Position

I believe a person may use force to stop an attack on themselves or on another person. There should be no duty to retreat and no location limit: the right applies at home, in a vehicle, and in public alike. Whether a killing in a claimed act of self-defense is lawful turns on an intent test. A killing that was aggravated and avoidable is murder and goes to the mandatory capital track. "Avoidable" means the attack had already been stopped or could have been stopped without killing; it never means the defender could have retreated. A killing in near-justified self-defense is a lesser homicide, with a lighter sentence decided by the judge. Defense of property follows the separate rule in Property Rights.

question"Doesn't removing the duty to retreat everywhere invite needless killings?"

No. The absence of a duty to retreat does not protect a killer who acted with intent beyond defense. An aggravated, avoidable killing is murder and should carry the mandatory capital sentence, and a killing that came close to justified self-defense but fell short should still be punished as a lesser homicide.

question"Doesn't the evidence show stand-your-ground laws increase homicides?"

The evidence leans that way. RAND's review rates as supportive the evidence that stand-your-ground laws increase total and firearm homicides. The position does not rest on that evidence; it rests on principle. Running should never be the first option for a person facing an attack, and a threat should never feel free to intimidate because its target is expected to retreat. The intent test is the safeguard: an aggravated, avoidable killing is still murder on the capital track.

question"Who has to prove whether a killing was self-defense?"

The prosecution. It should have to prove intent for a lesser-homicide charge, and should have to prove any aggravating factor that moves a killing to the capital track, not just the act itself (Lesser Homicide).

Rationale

As of January 1, 2025, 35 states had stand-your-ground statutes or statutes extending castle doctrine beyond the home, according to RAND. RAND also reports supportive evidence that these laws are associated with increases in total and firearm homicides; the intent test answers that risk by treating an aggravated, avoidable killing as murder under the mandatory penalty in Criminal Justice & Security. Qualification for gun ownership and red flag laws are set out in Cultural & Social Issues.

Freedom of Association

tldrIn short

People, clubs, and religious groups should be free to choose whom they associate with. Association should stay free only so long as it violates no one's rights. Joining or organizing a group to commit or plan rights violations, such as a conspiracy, gang, or organized violent group, is not protected (Terrorism and Domestic Extremism). No one may be held in a group against their will. Employers and businesses open to the public should not be allowed to hire, fire, serve, or treat anyone differently for race, sex, sexual orientation, or another protected trait, in either direction. There should be no quotas or DEI preferences, though wide advertising is fine, with narrow carve-outs (Merit-rule carve-outs). Businesses should still be free to decline to create expressive work they object to. Outside employment and public business, I consider compelling association coercion. It needs the same high justification as any other use of state force.

Position

I hold that private individuals and voluntary organizations have the right to decide whom they associate with in personal, religious, and club life. They should be free to include or exclude on any basis there. Employment and public business are different. Hiring, firing, pay, promotion, and service in a business open to the public should not turn on race, sex, sexual orientation, or another immutable or protected trait. Treating a person worse or better for what they are is a rights violation (Equal Treatment in Employment and Business). The rule should be symmetric. It should bar preferences and quotas, including private DEI preferences, as firmly as exclusion, with narrow carve-outs (Merit-rule carve-outs). Voluntary outreach that does not affect selection, such as advertising widely, should remain legal. Sex means biological sex: male, female, and rare intersex conditions. Gender identity is not a legal category. Outside those traits, employers and businesses would keep wide freedom to choose on merit, conduct, and fit. A business may decline to create expressive work it objects to. I reject broad mandates that police ordinary preferences in private social life.

questionDoesn't this lead to segregation, or discrimination?

Legal segregation and private freedom of association are not the same thing. Historical Jim Crow was primarily state-enforced separation: laws that required racial division and often forbade private integration. A pure association rule does the opposite. The state may neither compel association nor compel separation. In personal, religious, and club life, private parties may exclude or include and the law stays neutral. In employment and public business, one neutral rule bars treatment by protected traits in either direction.

Markets and reputation raise the cost of widespread exclusion. Forced association can produce physical proximity without the trust that makes integration valuable. Every anti-discrimination regime still excludes. They simply transfer the decision from the rights-holder to the state. I reject state-mandated segregation. I permit voluntary sorting in private association. I treat exclusion or preference by protected traits in employment and public business as a rights violation.

question"Could a business refuse service because of race?"

No. A business open to the public may not refuse a customer because of race, sex, sexual orientation, or another protected trait. An employer may not refuse a worker for those reasons either. A business may still decline to create a message or expressive work it objects to, whoever asks, as the Supreme Court held in 303 Creative v. Elenis. Private clubs, religious bodies, and personal relationships remain free to choose their members. The state itself may never compel separation.

Rationale

Freedom of association follows from the same negative-rights logic applied to liberty and property. If people own their labor, their time, and their property, they must be free to decide the terms on which they share them. Forcing a private party to associate, serve, or employ against their will is a form of compelled labor or compelled use of property. That compulsion is justified only when it is necessary to stop a rights violation. It is not justified when it is intended to produce a preferred social pattern. Employment and public business are where I find such a violation. An employer or business that rejects or prefers a person for an immutable or protected trait judges them by group rather than by merit or conduct. Stopping that is the justification for the rule.

The historical and practical case for wide association rights is straightforward. Voluntary sorting allows cooperation among people who share values, risk tolerances, or purposes. It reduces conflict. It supports high-trust environments. It lets different communities test different norms. When the state overrides those choices in the name of inclusion or equity, it substitutes political allocation for private coordination. That often generates resentment and evasion rather than genuine integration.

Anti-discrimination rules are not rejected in every form. Rules that prohibit violence, fraud, or clear safety hazards fit inside the negative-rights frame. Refusing to serve someone who is actively threatening others is an example. In personal, religious, and club life, rules that treat ordinary preference or cultural affinity as illegal do not fit. In employment and public business, preference or exclusion by protected trait is barred because it judges people by group, including statistical discrimination by group. Once the law begins policing the reasons for private choices rather than the presence of force or fraud, it expands beyond protecting rights and into managing social outcomes. That expansion is inconsistent with limited government and with the priority given to negative rights.

Public accommodations and common carriers would follow the protected-trait rule like any public business. Beyond those traits, a business holding a genuine bottleneck position on essential services can be held to further narrow non-discrimination conditions. The burden would be on the state to show necessity and narrow tailoring.

Membership organizations, religious groups, and social clubs would keep the full right to choose their members. Religious bodies would choose their own clergy and religious roles. The right to exclude is what makes the right to include meaningful. Without it, association becomes a state-managed status rather than a private decision.

In short, I treat freedom of association as a core negative right. The state may intervene to stop rights violations. It may not intervene to force people into relationships they do not want.

Privacy Rights

tldrIn short

To me, privacy means control over access to one’s body, home, private spaces, and personal information. Others should not get to enter, search, or collect without consent or a clear, limited legal process. Ordinary observation in public is acceptable. Turning public presence into permanent, identifiable records should be limited to public protection by the government and require opt-in for companies. Companies may offer services that involve data sharing, but the terms must be honest and the ability to decline must be real. Government access should be targeted and process-based, not open-ended. Privacy protects against intrusion while still allowing focused investigation when rights are actually being violated.

Position

Privacy protects a person from non-consensual intrusion into their body, home, personal spaces, and personal information. The default ought to be control: others, including the state, should not get access without consent or a clear, limited justification tied to stopping rights violations. Ordinary observation in open public spaces carries no reasonable expectation of privacy. Entry into private spaces, searches of persons or belongings, and collection of personal data should all require either consent or proper legal process. Mass, open-ended collection of data or permanent surveillance systems that convert public presence into lasting records face a higher bar.

question"Doesn't strong privacy protection just help criminals and block legitimate security needs?"

Privacy is not a right to hide criminal activity. Targeted investigation based on probable cause, warrants for specific places or data, and ordinary public observation remain available. The limit is on bulk, preemptive, or indefinite collection and on entering private spaces without process. Strong default privacy raises the cost of casual intrusion and mission creep while still allowing focused enforcement when force, fraud, or serious harm is actually at stake.

Rationale

Privacy, in both its traditional and informational forms, protects the ability to maintain a personal domain free from unwanted interference. Without it, agency shrinks. If the state or private actors can enter the home, search the person, or compile detailed records of private life at will, the individual no longer sets the terms of access. That is a form of control even when no physical injury occurs.

The classic core remains the home, the person, and private papers or effects. Entry or search in these domains requires consent or a specific, evidence-based legal process. General warrants, open-ended authorization, or routine intrusion without individualized suspicion are incompatible with the right. This is ordinary negative-rights protection applied to physical space and personal boundaries.

Public space is different. What a person knowingly exposes to open view, like walking on a street, speaking in a park, or attending a public gathering, carries no reasonable expectation of privacy against ordinary observation. The problem arises when technology turns transient public presence into permanent, searchable, identifiable records. Continuous bulk recording, facial-recognition databases, and long-term tracking change the character of public life. They concentrate power and create lasting exposure that ordinary observation never did. The initial setting is public, but that shift justifies tighter limits.

Informational privacy follows the same logic. Personal data is not simply “out there” for the taking. Collection by the state or by companies requires clear notice and genuine consent, or a proper legal process when consent is absent. Hidden collection, vague terms, or designs that make refusal impractical undermine the condition that makes consent meaningful. Where a service is difficult to avoid, the demand for transparency and workable choices is higher. Data-driven services are not prohibited. It requires that they rest on agreement rather than default extraction.

State power receives the strictest application. Targeted collection under warrant for specific investigations fits inside rights-based enforcement. Open-ended bulk collection, indefinite retention of ordinary activity, or repurposing data for unrelated goals does not. Once detailed records on the population become routine, the capacity for abuse grows and becomes hard to reverse. A 2022 FISA Court opinion found that FBI personnel ran more than 278,000 noncompliant queries of Section 702 data, including queries tied to protest participants and campaign donors.

Privacy is therefore handled as a side-constraint on intrusion. It protects the home, the person, private spaces, and personal information from non-consensual access. It does not guarantee invisibility in public, nor does it block focused investigation of actual rights violations. The practical goal is to keep control with the individual by default while still permitting necessary, limited responses when force, fraud, or serious harm is at issue.

Due Process and Rule of Law

tldrIn short

Everyone should answer for what they do, and people with more power answer to a higher standard. Assets can be taken only to settle broken contracts or unpaid debts between private parties. I view bail with suspicion, and judges ought to be vetted more strictly so that repeat offenders are stopped.

Position

I hold all people liable for their actions. Higher elected officials and those with great responsibility should be offered more in salary but held to higher standards. Assets should be forfeitable when contracts are broken or debts go unpaid between private actors. I view the bail system with skepticism. Judges ought to be vetted far more stringently so that punishment is applied appropriately, because I treat repeat offenders causing great damage as something that should never happen. Death penalty and incarceration positions are set out in Criminal Justice & Security.

question"Does limiting asset forfeiture to private disputes leave the state without tools against criminals?"

No. Forfeiture here is a remedy between private parties: a broken contract or an unpaid debt allows the injured party to recover assets. Punishment of criminals is handled through the criminal sentencing, restitution, and compelled-labor rules in Criminal Justice & Security, where offenders pay their victims from their own accounts and assets.

Rationale

Equal liability under law is the foundation of a high-trust society, and power that escapes accountability invites abuse. Greater authority therefore carries a stricter standard. Asset forfeiture in the civil-enforcement sense has drawn constitutional limits, including the Supreme Court's holding in Timbs v. Indiana that the Excessive Fines Clause applies to the states; restricting forfeiture to private contract and debt disputes keeps the remedy tied to an actual injured party. Federal pretrial release law already allows detention of defendants who pose a danger, which the Court upheld in United States v. Salerno. Repeat offending is a documented pattern in national recidivism data, which is why judicial vetting and sentencing aim at preventing it. Among prisoners released from 34 states in 2012, 62% were rearrested within three years and 71% within five years.

Asset forfeiture is the legal transfer of property to another party because of its connection to a wrong. Civil forfeiture lets the government take property tied to suspected crime, often without a conviction. The position here allows forfeiture only to satisfy broken contracts or unpaid debts between private parties.

Pretrial Detention and Bail

tldrIn short

Defendants should be released before trial unless a judge finds they are dangerous or likely to flee. In my view money must not decide who stays in jail, and those held must be tried within 90 days or released while the charge stands.

Position

An accused person has not been convicted and keeps their rights until a court finds otherwise. Pretrial detention should be allowed only on an individualized judicial finding that the person poses a serious danger to others or a real risk of flight, made at a prompt hearing with counsel. Detention should not be set by ability to pay. Detained defendants ought to receive swift trials so that detention does not become punishment before conviction.

question"Won't releasing defendants endanger the public?"

Those who pose a serious danger are detained. The rule removes wealth as the deciding factor: a dangerous person with money is not released, and a harmless person without money is not held.

question"How swift is a swift trial?"

A detained defendant should be brought to trial within 90 days of arrest. Only the defendant should be able to ask for more time. The prosecution and the court should not be able to extend the deadline on their own. If the state misses it, the defendant should be released pending trial under ordinary conditions, and the charge should stand. Federal law already sets a similar clock. The Speedy Trial Act requires an indictment within 30 days of arrest and a trial within 70 days of indictment or first appearance, though it excludes many periods of delay and its remedy is dismissal rather than release. Detention never becomes an open-ended punishment before conviction.

Denying Bail for Serious Offenses

A constitution may let judges deny release for the most serious charges, such as capital murder, terrorism and rape, as long as each denial is the judge's own finding about the particular defendant, made at a hearing with counsel and given in writing. No charge leads to detention automatically, and the 90-day trial limit still applies. Tennessee Amendment 1 allows courts to deny bail for a listed set of serious offenses and requires judges to record their reasons. If I were voting, I would vote yes.

Rationale

At midyear 2023, 70% of people held in local jails had not been convicted of the crime they were held for. In United States v. Salerno (1987) the Supreme Court upheld federal pretrial detention based on a judicial finding of dangerousness after an adversarial hearing, which is the structure this position adopts. Speed matters because people admitted to jail from July 2022 to June 2023 spent an average of 32 days in custody before release.

Role of Juries

tldrIn short

I would require a unanimous jury of at least twelve to convict, in civil and criminal cases alike. A guilty plea cannot avoid the mandatory death penalty, and every plea offer should be made on the record. Juries can nullify, but a nullification acquittal in a mandatory-death-penalty case may be retried once before a different jury.

Position

Unanimity. A unanimous jury should be required to convict. Reciprocity limits the protection a rights violator receives in sentencing and treatment once guilt is established. It does not lower the procedural bar for establishing guilt.

Plea deals. Pleading guilty to murder should not reduce or avoid the mandatory death penalty. A plea may affect only non-capital elements of a sentence. Every plea offer should be made on the record, with the offered terms disclosed to the court.

Civil juries. The same unanimity rule applies in every case, civil or criminal. Civil cases keep their lower standard of proof, but not a lower verdict threshold.

Jury nullification. Juries have the power to nullify. Judges should instruct juries to follow the laws given.

Retrial after nullification. If a jury acquits through nullification in a case carrying a mandatory death penalty, the state should be able to retry the case once before a different jury. This knowingly departs from the Fifth Amendment double jeopardy rule, under which an acquittal is final. Successive trials for the same act are not new: under the dual-sovereignty doctrine, which the Supreme Court declined to overturn in Gamble v. United States (2019), a state and the federal government may each prosecute the same conduct (Cornell LII – Gamble v. United States (2019)). My exception is narrower: one retrial, only after a finding of nullification, only in mandatory-death-penalty cases, before a different jury held to the same unanimity rule and capital standard.

Jury size. Twelve should be the minimum jury size, required alongside the unanimity rule. Six-person panels should not be permitted.

question"Doesn't a unanimity rule let a single holdout block justice?"

The rule protects the innocent first. A conviction can lead to the capital track, so the standard of proof has to be met for every juror, not most of them. A hung jury allows a retrial. A wrongful conviction cannot be undone after execution.

noteRevision threshold

If acquittals found to be nullification, the same finding that allows the one retrial, reach 5% of jury verdicts in mandatory-death-penalty cases, sustained over a five-year window, the nullification stance is revisited: from allowing nullification with judges not disclosing it, toward judges instructing juries that they may not nullify. Until that threshold is met, the current nullification and retrial rules stand. For context, federal juries acquitted in about 11% of jury trials in fiscal 2024 (159 of 1,491 defendants) and 12% in fiscal 2023 (184 of 1,596), against 14% in fiscal 2018. Those figures cover every offense and every reason for acquittal, including evidence that fails to convince, which is the system working. Counting all acquittals would likely trip the trigger before any nullification occurred, so only nullification acquittals count.

Rationale

Twelve-person juries are more accurate and more representative. In Saks's 1977 mock-jury studies, as reported in Ballew v. Georgia, 12-person juries reached the correct verdict 83% of the time, compared with 69% for 6-person juries, in the student experiment, and 71% against 57% in the experiment with former jurors. In Ballew v. Georgia, the Supreme Court calculated that a group making up 10% of the community would be missing from 53% of 6-person juries; the same math puts that figure at about 28% for 12-person juries. A 1997 meta-analysis of 17 studies (Saks & Marti) found no significant difference in verdicts by jury size, but larger juries included more minority members and deliberated longer.

Epistocratic Republic and Qualified Franchise

tldrIn short

Most people treat a universal vote as what makes a country free. I don't. I support a constitutional republic with a qualified (restricted) franchise, also called an epistocratic republic or a limited democracy rather than a full democracy. The net-taxpayer and no-welfare rules are the stake qualification, and a voter license test on issues, civics, and laws is the competence qualification, so the laws that bind everyone are chosen by people who understand them. Ranked-choice voting and the Constitution stay.

Position

I believe the United States should be a constitutional republic with a qualified (restricted) franchise. Epistocracy means rule by the knowledgeable, so the system is also an epistocratic republic, or a limited democracy rather than a full democracy. Elections, ranked-choice voting, and the Constitution would all stay. What would change is who holds the franchise. On top of voter ID and the felony bar (Voting and Elections), every voter should meet two qualifications:

  • Stake qualification: only net taxpayers would vote, and people living on personally directed transfers would not, because the franchise decides other people's money (net fiscal voting bar; welfare voting bar). This is a tax or stake qualification, close to what Aristotle called timocracy and to the property and taxpaying qualifications of the early American states.
  • Competence qualification: every voter would hold a voter license, earned by passing the voter license test on issues, civics, and the laws they vote on. This is the epistocratic part. The test would be a literal knowledge exam that gives you a voting license: passing it means you are qualified to make these decisions on behalf of the people. It would test deeper knowledge of:
  • all the candidates on the ballot;
  • the structure of government, including Congress (the Senate and the House);
  • the parties and how parties work;
  • some history of the administration;
  • the different policies that have been in place.

Passing it is not enough on its own: you also need to be a net positive taxpayer (the stake qualification above; net fiscal voting bar). If you are unqualified and cannot answer these questions, I don't believe you deserve to be voting, because you are not qualified to.

Together they require a stake in the system and a working knowledge of it. Of the classical constitutions, that is closest to Aristotle's polity (politeia), the mixed constitution between rule by the few and rule by the many.

Both qualifications need constitutional amendments. Federal law has banned literacy tests and similar tests nationwide since 1970, permanently since 1975, and the courts treat voting as a fundamental right, so the voter license belongs in the Constitution, not in a statute that a later Congress or a court could undo.

The fight will be over the design of the test, and the history earns that. Southern states used literacy tests, applied at local officials' discretion, to keep Black citizens from voting. So the test has to meet the same standard of evidence I apply everywhere else (Meta-Ethics): its questions should be neutral, public, and repeatable, with factual answers anyone can check, never opinions. Who writes it, how it is audited, and how pass rates are published are set out in Voting and Elections.

question"Why epistocracy instead of direct democracy?"

I want a more intelligent voter base. Too many voters today are completely uninformed, and I believe the current voter base, and the democracy we have, is a complete failure. Direct democracy has one real strength: Swiss voters reject most proposals put to them, which makes the initiative a check on the legislature rather than a replacement for it. That is why I keep initiatives, but only for qualified voters (Qualified Citizen Initiatives). Beyond that check, I prefer epistocracy for three reasons:

  • Majorities can vote away rights. Swiss men rejected women's federal suffrage in 1959, and Swiss voters approved a ban on building minarets in 2009. A majority vote cannot authorize violating an individual's negative rights, and that limit applies to every majority, including American ones (Qualified Citizen Initiatives; Sharia as State Law; Political Philosophy).
  • Voters should know what they decide. Those who fail to meet the standard of knowledge of the government and the laws they vote on should not vote, so the people choosing the government understand it (Voting and Elections).
  • The franchise decides other people's money. Initiatives decide taxes and transfers, and voting on a transfer involving yourself is a massive conflict of interest, so the net-taxpayer rule applies to them with equal force (net fiscal voting bar; Qualified Citizen Initiatives).
question"Why should anyone need a license to vote?"

For the same reason we license drivers, doctors, and pilots: their choices put other people at risk. A driver endangers the people on the road. A voter helps choose the laws, taxes, and wars that bind everyone, including people who voted the other way or could not vote at all. Jason Brennan calls this the competence principle: it is unjust to force high-stakes decisions on people when those decisions are made incompetently or by an incompetent body. I accept it. If we ask a pilot to prove what they know before flying passengers, I believe it is reasonable to ask a voter to prove what they know before helping govern the country.

The knowledge gap is real. In the 2025 Annenberg Constitution Day Civics Survey, 70% of U.S. adults could name all three branches of government, and 13% could not name any. Asked which rights the First Amendment protects, 79% named freedom of speech, but fewer than half named any of the other four. The voter license test asks for that kind of knowledge, and for knowledge of the laws voters decide (Voting and Elections).

A license is not a judgment of anyone's worth. Everyone without one keeps every right a voter has except the vote, and the test is free and retakable, with free prep courses. The two errors are not equal. A capable citizen wrongly left off the rolls loses one vote among millions until a free retake, and keeps every protection the law gives. An uninformed vote helps set the taxes, laws, and wars that bind everyone, including the people who studied and the people who could not vote at all. A capable citizen who skips the test goes unheard by choice, since the test, the prep, and every retake are free.

question"If not everyone can vote, is it still a democracy?"

Not a full one, and I don't claim it is. It keeps elections, ranked-choice voting, and a written constitution, so it is a republic, but its franchise is qualified rather than universal. The accurate name is a constitutional republic with a qualified franchise, or an epistocratic republic. The United States began with a qualified franchise too: many founding-era state constitutions limited the vote to people who owned a set amount of property, and some to those who paid taxes.

No franchise is universal, including today's. The 26th Amendment protects the vote of citizens "eighteen years of age or older," and no state lets young children vote, because they are presumed not yet able to judge what they would decide. The question has never been whether to qualify the franchise, only how. Age is a proxy for competence. The voter license measures it directly, person by person, instead of inferring it from a birthday (Voting and Elections).

question"Isn't a voter license just a literacy test with a new name?"

Literacy tests were real tools of disenfranchisement, and I don't dispute that. They were left to local officials who chose whom to test and how to grade. The voter license test removes that discretion: one public question bank, factual answers anyone can check, the same test for everyone, free and retakable, with free prep courses, and with pass rates published by group (Voting and Elections). The country already gives a test of this kind to its newest citizens. The naturalization civics test draws 20 questions from a published list of 128, with an official study guide, and an applicant passes with 12 correct. A naturalized citizen has already shown the knowledge I would ask of every voter.

question"Qualified franchises have a dark history: literacy tests, poll taxes, property rules. Why would this one be different?"

I believe that history should be faced, not waved away. Louisiana's "interpretation test" required an applicant to "give a reasonable interpretation" of any clause of the state or federal constitution, and the Supreme Court found it "vested in the voting registrars a virtually uncontrolled discretion as to who should vote and who should not" (Louisiana v. United States, 1965). The Voting Rights Act of 1965 outlawed literacy tests as a prerequisite to voting. The Twenty-fourth Amendment barred poll taxes in federal elections, and Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections (1966) struck them down in state elections, holding that "wealth or fee paying has, in our view, no relation to voting qualifications." The early American states limited the vote to property owners, and some to taxpayers.

I don't defend any of those. I don't accept the alternative either. Universal democracy as it runs today produces rotted politics: power swings back and forth, and elections devolve into demographic voting blocs with no care for substance or for economic, social, or political theory. It is unfair to informed citizens to be ruled by people who know nothing about what they vote for. So the question is how to qualify the vote without repeating those abuses. Each abuse had a cause, and the design removes it:

  • Discretion. Registrars chose whom to test and how to grade. Here there is one question bank, published in advance as a public study guide with free prep courses, factual answers anyone can check, the same test for everyone, free and retakeable, and any citizen can challenge a question in court as biased (Voting and Elections).
  • Hidden targets. Southern literacy tests and poll taxes were used to keep Black citizens from voting. No rule here looks at race, religion, or ancestry. Pass rates are published by race, sex, age, and party, and if a group fails at a markedly higher rate and fixing biased questions cannot close the gap, the test is suspended.
  • A price on the ballot. A poll tax charged people to vote. No one pays anything to vote here. The stake rule asks whether a person pays more in tax than they receive in personally directed transfers, and anyone can decline such a transfer to keep the vote (net fiscal voting bar).
  • Easy entrenchment. Those rules came by state law and local practice. This one comes only through Article V, its definitions sit in the amendment itself, a change in tax or transfer law cannot change anyone's franchise status until after the next general election, and the amendment lapses unless it is re-ratified every 20 years.
  • Losing more than the vote. Jim Crow disenfranchisement was part of a system that also denied Black citizens the equal protection of the law. Here everyone without the vote keeps every right a voter has except the vote itself, and no majority, qualified or not, may vote to violate an individual's negative rights.

Harper is also why both qualifications need a constitutional amendment: under today's Constitution, the stake rule would not survive.

question"Aren't the franchise criteria just stipulated? Why these, and what would change them?"

Where they come from. Voting is not a negative right. It is a share in the coercive power the state holds over everyone else, so I hold it to my rule for all coercion: it is justified only as far as it protects rights (Political Authority and Coercion). Two things make a voter a danger to other people's rights. One is a conflict of interest over their money, which the stake qualification removes. The other is deciding laws without knowing them, which the competence qualification removes. The test also has to meet my standard of proof: factual, public, repeatable questions anyone can check.

The rivals.

  • Universal suffrage lets majorities vote away rights and lets voters decide transfers to themselves.
  • Property or wealth qualifications measure the wrong thing. A renter who pays more in tax than they receive has the stake, and a landowner living on transfers does not.
  • J. S. Mill's plural voting gave extra votes by occupation or degree. Those are proxies for knowledge. I judge each voter directly, by a test, not by a group they belong to (Group Statistics and Individual Judgment). Mill held both of my qualifications in the same chapter: "that the receipt of parish relief should be a peremptory disqualification for the franchise," and that no one should vote "without being able to read, write, and, I will add, perform the common operations of arithmetic."

What would change them. Both rest on principle, not on a promised outcome, so a study of election results would not overturn them. What changes is how they apply. The stake bar lifts in any year a person is again a net taxpayer. Test questions are audited every cycle, pass rates are published by race, sex, age, and party, and any question one group fails at a markedly higher rate is reviewed for bias. If the commission cannot close such a gap by fixing biased questions, the test should be suspended until it can.

question"How could a qualified franchise be adopted legitimately?"

Only through Article V, under today's rules. An amendment has to be proposed by two-thirds of both houses of Congress, or by a convention called on the application of two-thirds of the state legislatures, and ratified by three-fourths of the states, 38 of 50. Every past change to who may vote went the same way, including the 15th, 19th, and 26th Amendments. A qualified franchise ratified by those supermajorities would be as legitimate as the universal franchise it replaced. Imposed any other way, by statute, executive order, or force, it would not be, and I would not support it. I don't expect it soon: the world I wish for may never be reality, so I favor directional moves that close the distance, such as voter ID, though they are not the ideal itself.

Rationale

Aristotle sorted constitutions by whether one, a few, or many rule, and by whether they rule for the common advantage. The correct form of rule by the many he called polity, and he describes it as a mixed constitution combining features of democracy and oligarchy, and where possible aristocracy, the best attainable for most cities. In the Nicomachean Ethics he names a constitution based on a property qualification "timocratic, though most people are wont to call it polity." The stake qualification is that property element in modern form, and the competence qualification adds the element of knowledge. Jason Brennan's Against Democracy (2016) is the modern case for epistocracy: it argues that democracy should be judged by its results and that no one has a fundamental right to a share of political power.

Section 4(a) of the Voting Rights Act of 1965 suspended literacy tests and similar devices for five years in the jurisdictions it covered, mostly in the South. The 1970 amendments extended the ban to every state until August 6, 1975, and the Supreme Court upheld it in Oregon v. Mitchell (1970). The 1975 amendments made the nationwide ban permanent. The ban is statutory: in Lassiter v. Northampton County Board of Elections (1959) the Court upheld a literacy test that was fair on its face. But the Court has called voting "a fundamental matter in a free and democratic society" (Reynolds v. Sims, 1964) and scrutinizes restrictions on it closely, and Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections (1966) struck down wealth conditions on voting. That is why both qualifications belong in the Constitution, where neither a later statute nor a court can undo them.

Voting and Elections

tldrIn short

Voters should have to show ID, mail-in ballots must be fully authenticated and encrypted, felons cannot vote, and only net taxpayers should vote. I support a constitutional amendment that would require passing a fair voter license test to vote. Political money ought to be limited to individual donations, and corporations do not get the voice of people.

Position

Voter ID should be federally mandated. Mail-in voting ought to be reformed for complete authentication of the voter and transmitted through encrypted channels with world-class cybersecurity. Felons should not vote; a felony is any offense worth two or more marks, or any capital-tier crime (Felony and misdemeanor). Only net taxpayers should vote. A person is a net taxpayer for a year when two conditions both hold for the prior completed tax year: taxes paid exceed personally directed transfers received, and income-tax liability is positive; the bar lifts when a later year shows net taxpayer again.

Personally directed transfers means cash, food, housing, or non-emergency medical support funded by general taxation and paid into the person's budget — today's SNAP, TANF, SSI, housing vouchers, non-emergency Medicaid, and refundable tax-credit cash-outs, and under this framework the last-resort allowance, the earnings-only top-up, and any remaining means-tested floor. A refundable credit cash-out is only the amount paid when income-tax liability would otherwise be ≤ $0. The portion that offsets positive liability is tax reduction, not a transfer received. Non-refundable relief — exemptions, deductions, and non-refundable credits, including the mothers' income-tax exemption — is not received. Taxes paid means federal income tax, the employee share of payroll / FICA attributable to the worker, and state income tax. The employer share of FICA is remitted by the employer and does not count on the paid side of the franchise scorecard. Income-tax liability means federal and state income-tax liability after ordinary non-refundable credits, measured before the mothers' income-tax exemption: that exemption does not by itself fail the positive-liability gate when pre-exemption liability is positive. Payroll-only with zero pre-exemption income-tax liability does not clear the gate. Grandfathered Social Security and Medicare, unemployment insurance, veterans' benefits, private insurance, charity, mutual aid, emergency shelter, emergency medical care, K-12, roads, defense, and other universal or shared public goods do not count as transfers on the received side. Excluding grandfathered Social Security and Medicare from the received side does not waive the positive-liability gate: an SS-only retiree or other payroll-only adult with $0 pre-exemption income-tax liability fails, because the franchise tracks current net contribution to the tax system that funds transfers, not lifetime payroll history. The bar is a conflict-of-interest rule, not a punishment and not a rights forfeiture: the franchise decides other people's money, and people who are not net taxpayers cannot cast the deciding votes about it (Welfare and Entitlements).

The bar should be enacted by constitutional amendment, the same path as the voter license test, and it lifts when a later completed tax year shows the person is again a net taxpayer. The unit is the tax-filing unit. Spouses who file jointly share one net position and one liability status for that year, so a stay-at-home spouse on a joint return that is a net taxpayer with positive liability votes. I don't treat voting as a universal right. A constitutional amendment should require voters to pass a bipartisan, unbiased, thorough voter license test of political and social knowledge. Those who fail to meet the standard of knowledge of the government and the laws they vote on should not vote. Campaign finance ought to be reformed because money in politics is dangerous and disadvantages small candidates. Corporations do not have the voice of people, contrary to Citizens United, and finance should be limited to individual donations rather than organized donation campaigns. These limits apply to contributions to candidates and campaigns, not to independent speech.

question"Is the voter license test a return to Jim Crow literacy tests?"

Critics point to literacy tests, which were administered selectively to exclude Black voters and are banned by federal law today. That record condemns discretionary local administration, which is exactly what this test removes: it is bipartisan, unbiased, and applied to everyone through a constitutional amendment, not through local officials. Its purpose is that those choosing the government understand the government and the laws they vote on, and it rests on that principle, not on a promised outcome.

question"Why can't felons vote, even after their sentence?"

Some consequences of forfeiture outlast the sentence by rule, and the bar on felons voting is one of them (General Rules of Forfeiture). A felony here means any offense worth two or more marks, or any capital-tier crime. A misdemeanor, worth one mark or a fine without a mark, does not bar voting (Felony and misdemeanor).

question"Why can only net taxpayers vote?"

Because the franchise decides other people's money. A person who is not a net taxpayer — whose personally directed transfers meet or exceed the taxes they pay, or who owes no income tax — and who then votes on the taxes and programs that move that money is voting on a transfer involving themselves. That is a massive conflict of interest. The bar lasts only while the prior completed tax year fails the net-taxpayer test, and it is not a punishment for poverty or a claim that non-payers lack judgment — it is an eligibility rule that removes the conflict. The rule protects the people who pay from having the amount set by those who receive it, and it protects recipients too, since the benefit does not depend on how anyone votes. The two errors are not equal. A person wrongly kept off the rolls loses the vote only until a completed tax year shows otherwise, and keeps every benefit and every right; a vote cast on one's own transfer helps set what is taken from others for one's own benefit. The law already draws this line for federal judges, who must step aside where they have "a financial interest in the subject matter in controversy."

question"Isn't that a poll tax by another name?"

No. A poll tax charged a fee to vote and was struck down in Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections. This rule charges nothing. It restricts the franchise to net taxpayers for the prior completed tax year, and it lifts when a later year shows net-taxpayer status again. It still goes through constitutional amendment, the same path as the voter license test, because franchise conditions of this kind should be fixed in the Constitution rather than left to statute.

question"What about Social Security and Medicare?"

Grandfathered Social Security and Medicare do not count on the received side of the comparison. Those are contributory benefits people already paid into, not personally directed transfers chosen from general revenue — collecting them is not itself a franchise disqualifier (Social Security and Medicare Transition). That exclusion does not waive the other half of the test. Positive pre-exemption income-tax liability is still required. An SS-only unit with $0 pre-exemption liability fails even with no SNAP, TANF, last-resort, or other personally directed transfer on the received side. The franchise tracks current net contribution to the tax system that funds transfers, not lifetime payroll or transition-levy history.

question"Can a retiree living only on Social Security vote?"

Not on Social Security alone. Grandfathered Social Security is not counted as received, so drawing it does not by itself fail the transfers side. The liability gate still applies: the prior completed tax year should show positive federal-plus-state income-tax liability measured before the mothers' income-tax exemption. An SS-only retiree — or any payroll-only adult — with $0 pre-exemption liability does not vote that year. If the same return shows positive pre-exemption liability for any ordinary reason (taxable portion of benefits, other taxable income, or a joint return that clears the unit's liability gate), the liability half is met and the rest of the net-taxpayer test applies as usual. Lifetime FICA or transition-levy payments do not substitute for current-year liability. The bar lifts when a later completed tax year shows net-taxpayer status again.

question"How is net-taxpayer status measured?"

From the prior completed tax year. Personally directed transfers received that year — cash, food, housing, non-emergency medical support funded by general taxation, including the last-resort allowance, the earnings-only top-up, and refundable credit cash-outs — are summed and set against federal income tax, the employee share of payroll / FICA, and state income tax paid that year. The employer share of FICA is not counted. A refundable credit cash-out is only the amount paid when income-tax liability would otherwise be ≤ $0. Amounts that offset positive liability do not count as received. Non-refundable relief (exemptions, deductions, non-refundable credits), including the mothers' income-tax exemption, is not received. Non-emergency Medicaid is counted at the program's average cost per enrollee for the year. The unit should also show positive federal-plus-state income-tax liability measured before the mothers' income-tax exemption. That exemption does not by itself zero the gate when pre-exemption liability is positive. Payroll alone with zero pre-exemption income-tax liability fails. Sales and property taxes are not in the tax side. K-12, roads, defense, and other shared public goods are not on the received side. The tax-filing unit is the unit: joint filers share one net position and one liability status. Eligibility for elections in a given year uses the prior year's result.

question"Don't the family credits and mothers' exemption knock parents off the voter rolls?"

No, not by tax reduction alone. The child credit, birth credit, and mothers' exemption exist to raise births under lower immigration. Non-refundable relief is not a transfer received. Refundable credits count on the received side only as cash-outs — the dollars paid when liability would otherwise be ≤ $0 — not as the portion that offsets tax still owed. The mothers' exemption does not by itself fail the positive-liability gate when pre-exemption liability is positive. SNAP, TANF, the last-resort allowance, the earnings-only top-up, and other personally directed transfers stay on the received side in full. A unit that is mostly cash-out or welfare still fails the net-taxpayer test. A working parent whose family package is tax reduction against positive liability does not lose the vote for that reason alone.

question"Does a stay-at-home spouse lose the vote?"

No, not for unpaid home work alone. Spouses who file jointly share the tax-filing unit's net position and liability status. If the joint return is a net taxpayer with positive income-tax liability, both spouses vote. Private dependence on a spouse's earnings is not a claim on other taxpayers. A spouse who files alone with zero income-tax liability fails the positive-liability gate.

question"Don't limits on political money restrict free speech?"

The limits apply to contributions to candidates and campaigns, not to independent speech. Finance should be limited to individual donations because corporations do not have the voice of people, and money in politics disadvantages small candidates.

question"Doesn't voter ID suppress turnout, especially among minority voters?"

The best evidence says it does not. A nationwide study of 1.6 billion voter-file observations from 2008 to 2018 found that strict ID laws had "no negative effect on registration or turnout, overall or for any group defined by race, gender, age, or party affiliation." The same study found no effect on fraud, actual or perceived, so ID is justified here as a verification standard for every ballot, not as a fraud cure. The voter license test is a different matter: unlike ID, it is designed to exclude voters who fail it, and pass rates may differ by group. That is why its pass rates are published broken down by race, sex, age, and party, and any question that one group fails at a markedly higher rate is reviewed by the bipartisan commission for bias.

question"Who writes the voter license test?"

A commission should write and maintain it. Each major party should hold an equal number of seats, an equal number of members should be drawn by lot from licensed voters who belong to neither major party (registered voters, for the first commission), and the chair should be drawn by lot from that group. Any change to the question bank should need a two-thirds vote, so no single faction can rewrite the test. The question bank should be published in advance as a public study guide with free prep courses, questions should test facts about the structure of government and the laws voters decide on, never opinions, and the test should be free and retakeable. Any citizen should be able to challenge a question in court as biased. The commission should audit the question bank each cycle, and pass rates should be published so anyone can check whether the test is excluding a group.

question"Couldn't whoever controls the test or the net-taxpayer rule shut out opponents and entrench an oligarchy?"

That is the real danger of any qualified franchise, and the design is built against it.

  • The test. A commission writes it, with equal seats for each major party, an equal number of members drawn by lot from licensed voters in neither, and a chair drawn by lot from that group. Changing the question bank takes a two-thirds vote. The question bank is public in advance, the questions are facts, never opinions, and the test is free and retakeable, with free prep courses. Any citizen can challenge a question in court. Pass rates are published by race, sex, age, and party, and if the commission cannot close a gap one group fails at a markedly higher rate, the test is suspended until it can (Epistocratic Republic and Qualified Franchise).
  • The net-taxpayer rule. I believe the definition should be written into the amendment itself, not left to statute, so no majority can redraw it. Changing it should take a new Article V amendment: two-thirds of both houses and three-fourths of the states.
  • The feedback loop. Qualified voters elect the lawmakers who set taxes and transfers, and taxes and transfers decide who qualifies. A majority could try to move opponents off the rolls by changing the zero bracket or handing them transfers. So a change in tax or transfer law should not change anyone's franchise status until after the next general election, and anyone should be able to decline a personally directed transfer to keep the vote.
  • Elite overreach. A qualified electorate is bound by the same side-constraints as any other. No majority, qualified or not, may vote to violate an individual's negative rights, and everyone without the vote keeps every right a voter has except the vote itself (Epistocratic Republic and Qualified Franchise). The franchise decides who chooses among lawful policies, not whose rights count.
  • Re-ratification. I believe the amendment should lapse unless it is re-ratified every 20 years under Article V: two-thirds of both houses and three-fourths of the states. If it is not re-ratified in time, universal suffrage returns at the next general election.
  • Adoption. The whole system comes in only through Article V, and every condition sits in the Constitution, where courts enforce it.

No mechanism is incorruptible, universal suffrage included, since majorities there can vote themselves other people's money. The test is whether abuse can be seen and stopped. Published pass rates, the suspension rule, court challenges, and fixed constitutional text make it visible and hard to do quietly. How many adults would qualify under both rules has no estimate yet (Needs research: share of adults who are net taxpayers and would pass the test).

Rationale

Elections are only legitimate if every vote is cast by an eligible, verified voter. The Supreme Court upheld photo ID requirements in Crawford v. Marion County Election Board, and states have adopted ID rules widely. Nationwide panel data from 2008 to 2018 found strict ID laws did not lower registration or turnout for any group; ID is justified here as a verification standard for every ballot, not as a cure for measured fraud. The Court held in Richardson v. Ramirez that states may disenfranchise felons under the Fourteenth Amendment. A second eligibility rule follows the same logic as keeping the franchise off people who have not shown knowledge of the government they vote on: only net taxpayers cast votes about other people's money, because people who are not net taxpayers have a direct financial stake in how large personally directed transfers are and how heavy the tax burden is. The bar is temporary and aimed at that conflict, not at poverty as such. Measurement details — the tax-filing unit and joint returns, what counts as paid and received, the mothers' exemption, and grandfathered Social Security and Medicare — are set out in the position and the questions above (Births and Family Formation). The paid side counts only taxes the worker remits as income tax or as the employee share of payroll — matching how a W-2 reports them. Arguments that the employer share is economically incident on wages are economics, not the eligibility formula; the franchise scorecard stays auditable. Because Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections struck down poll taxes, and because wealth-linked franchise conditions are the kind of rule that belongs in the Constitution, the bar is proposed by constitutional amendment rather than statute. Federal law currently bans literacy tests nationwide, which is why a knowledge requirement is proposed through constitutional amendment rather than statute. The requirement rests on principle; the literacy-test history is why it is built around bipartisan authorship, a public question bank, and audit. Citizens United removed limits on independent corporate political spending; limiting finance to individual donations restores elections to individual citizens.

Election Administration

tldrIn short

Elections should require ID, authenticated mail ballots, and clean voter rolls, with money limited to individual donations. I want the rules set before each election.

Position

Elections should be administered to ensure that only eligible voters vote and that every valid ballot is counted. Voters should show identification, mail-in ballots should be fully authenticated and encrypted, and people convicted of felonies ought to be removed from the rolls because they cannot vote. People who are not net taxpayers are likewise removed under the conflict-of-interest rule in Voting and Elections, and restored when a completed tax year shows they are net taxpayers. Political money should be limited to individual donations to candidates and campaigns. Election procedures ought to be fixed in law before each election rather than changed during it.

question"Doesn't strict administration make voting harder?"

Identification and authentication protect each lawful vote from being diluted by an unlawful one. Every eligible citizen can meet the requirements, and confidence in the count is what makes the result legitimate.

Federal Enforcement Near Elections

tldrIn short

In my view federal enforcement around an election must follow rules fixed before it. In the last 60 days there should be no bulk ballot holds, no eligibility questioning at voters' homes without a court order, and no overt steps against candidates unless a judge finds an immediate need.

Position

Election procedures ought to be fixed in law before each election rather than changed during it (Election Administration), and the same should hold for federal enforcement around an election. Rolls should be cleaned by matching government records through each state's process before voting begins, and each person who registered or voted unlawfully should be prosecuted on individual evidence. Within 60 days of a federal election, no agency should demand that ballots be set aside in bulk, or send agents to voters' homes to question them about their eligibility, unless a court orders it for a named person on individual evidence. Overt steps against a candidate, such as searches, arrests or subpoenas that become public, also wait until after the election unless a judge finds an immediate threat to safety or evidence about to be lost; covert investigation continues throughout. These limits should be written in statute, so they apply the same way whichever party holds power.

Rationale

The Justice Department's practice in recent history has been to advise prosecutors against overt investigative steps involving members of Congress or candidates within 60 days of an election. It is a practice, not a statute. In October 2026 a federal court enjoined election-related field interviews of likely citizens in nine states, and the Justice Department asked a court to hold back more than 20,000 California mail ballots before counting.

Ranked-Choice Voting

tldrIn short

I would require ranked-choice voting for every election in the country, federal, state, and local. It runs on paper ballots, published rounds, fixed rules, and clear notice that partly ranked ballots can be exhausted. Its effects on turnout and polarization are mixed, and it does not guarantee a majority of all ballots.

Position

The United States ought to require ranked-choice voting for every election in the country: federal, state, and local. Because the Constitution gives Congress power over federal elections but not over state and local ones, the United States ought to enact the requirement through a constitutional amendment, as with the voter license test (Voting and Elections). It should run on paper ballots, every round of the count should be published, the rules are fixed in law before the election (Election Administration), and voters are told plainly that a ballot ranking only some candidates may be exhausted before the final round.

question"Doesn't ranked-choice voting guarantee a majority winner?"

No, only a majority of ballots still counted in the final round. Burnett and Kogan examined more than 600,000 ballots from four local elections and found that in every one the winner received less than a majority of all ballots cast, because exhausted ballots dropped out of the count. That limit is why exhaustion is disclosed to voters rather than hidden.

question"Isn't it too confusing for voters?"

The evidence from Maine says mostly not. In the 2018 race for Maine's 2nd Congressional District, the Republican led the first round 46.33% to 45.58% and the Democrat won after transfers, 50.62% to 49.38%. Only 621 ballots, 0.21% of the total, were exhausted through apparent confusion. My voter license test (Voting and Elections) also means every voter has shown knowledge of the structure of government.

question"Does it raise turnout or reduce polarization?"

Critics say ranked-choice voting has not delivered what its backers promised. On polarization the gain is modest: the National Conference of State Legislatures summarizes research on Maine as showing only a modest reduction. On turnout the critics are on weaker ground: a 2024 study using administrative turnout records found substantially higher turnout and more direct voter contact where it is used, while earlier work by Kimball and Anthony found negligible turnout effects outside the runoffs it replaces (Needs research: the turnout gain rests on one 2024 study). Neither result decides it. I would not require it for promised effects. I would require it because it lets voters rank every candidate and ends the spoiler problem.

question"Doesn't a national requirement override the states?"

It does, by design. Seventeen states had banned ranked-choice voting by 2025, and a national standard replaces those bans. The rules I call for already set national standards for how votes are cast and verified, such as voter ID and authenticated mail ballots. How votes are counted is part of the same integrity standard, and a vote should not count differently depending on the state it is cast in. States keep running their own elections under that standard (Federalism). Voters have also shown support where it was tested: Alaska's 2024 measure to repeal its system failed by 743 votes after a recount, 50.12% to 49.88%. Alaska voters face a second repeal attempt, Ballot Measure 2, on November 3, 2026 (Ballot measures, November 3, 2026).

question"Can't a decoy candidate with the same name split the vote?"

Under ranked-choice voting a decoy can't split a vote the way it can under plurality voting. A voter who ranks both candidates loses nothing, because a ballot for an eliminated candidate moves to the voter's next choice. The danger left is voters ranking only the decoy. That is handled before the election with clear ballot labels and a ballot-access rule against candidacies filed to deceive (Ballot Access and Deceptive Candidacies), not by holding up a count afterward (Suit to hold Alaska's Senate certification).

Rationale

The United States ought to fix the integrity rules for every election, such as identification, authentication, and rules set in advance, and the counting method is part of those rules, so ranked-choice voting should be required everywhere. Ranked-choice voting also reduces the spoiler problem that keeps small candidates from competing, a concern I already raise about money in politics (Voting and Elections). Against that, it does not guarantee a true majority of all ballots, its counts take longer, and its measured effects on turnout and polarization are mixed. I accept those trade-offs for a count that lets every voter rank every candidate.

Primary Elections

tldrIn short

I see parties as private associations that choose their nominees by their own rules. A state may instead hold one all-candidate primary only if at least four candidates advance to a ranked-choice general election. I would not allow a top-two primary.

Position

I hold that a political party is a private association, and its members should decide how it picks its nominees (Freedom of Association). A state may run a party's primary for it, but it may not force a party to accept nominees chosen by non-members. A state that prefers one primary open to every candidate may hold one, as long as at least four candidates advance to a general election decided by ranked-choice voting (Ranked-Choice Voting), as in Alaska. A top-two system leaves the general-election ballot with two names, which empties ranked-choice voting of any meaning and often leaves voters with two candidates from one party. If I were voting on Massachusetts Question 3, I would vote no.

Ballot Access and Deceptive Candidacies

tldrIn short

I believe a candidacy filed to deceive voters, such as a stand-in with the same name, can be kept off the ballot under a rule written before the election and decided by a court before ballots are printed. Once votes are cast, they must be counted as cast.

Position

Ballot access should be open to any good-faith candidate. The United States ought to let election officials refuse a candidacy filed to confuse voters, such as one that copies an incumbent's ballot name, party and branding, under a standard fixed in law in advance, with a prompt court review that finishes before ballots are printed (Election Administration). Ballots should list every candidate's full legal name and party. Once voting begins, the result ought to be counted as cast; no official or court should hold up certification on a guess about what voters meant. Ranked-choice voting limits the harm of any decoy that gets through (Ranked-Choice Voting).

Qualified Citizen Initiatives

tldrIn short

States and localities may offer citizen initiatives and referendums. I support a constitutional amendment creating a national initiative for federal statutes and amendments, reaching the ballot with signatures from 1% (statute) or 2% (amendment) of qualified voters within 18 months. Measures should pass by more than half of the votes cast with no minimum turnout, and amendments also need a majority of the states, 26 of 50, each counted by its own voters. No initiative can override protected rights, and every measure faces court review. Only voters who meet the full election stack - ID, the voter license test, net-taxpayer status, and no felony bar - may sign and vote, under rules fixed in advance, and outcomes rest with those who vote.

Position

I support citizen initiatives and referendums at the state and local level, and a national citizen initiative for federal statutes and constitutional amendments, enacted through a constitutional amendment. Each state should decide whether to offer its own initiatives and set its own signature thresholds. No initiative, state or national, should be able to override the rights I protect, such as negative rights, equal treatment, and due process. Every measure is subject to court review like any other law (Due Process and Rule of Law). Only voters who meet the same eligibility stack that governs every election may sign petitions and vote on measures: voter ID, the voter license test, net-taxpayer status for the prior completed tax year, and no felony bar (Voting and Elections; net fiscal voting bar; Election Administration). Initiatives decide taxes and transfers; the conflict-of-interest rule that bars non–net-taxpayers from candidate elections applies with equal force here. The rules for each measure are fixed in law before signatures are gathered. In this section, "qualified voters" means that full stack. A national initiative reaches the ballot with signatures from 1% of qualified voters in the last federal election for a federal statute, or 2% for a constitutional amendment, gathered within 18 months. These thresholds are my own choice, scaled from the Swiss model rather than taken from it. A measure passes by a simple majority of votes cast, more than half, with blank and spoiled ballots not counted and no minimum turnout. A constitutional amendment by initiative needs a double majority: a national popular majority plus a majority of the states, 26 of 50. A state counts toward that majority when its own voters approve the measure, as Swiss cantons do.

question"Don't citizen initiatives let majorities vote away minority rights?"

It can, and Switzerland shows it. Swiss men rejected women's federal suffrage in 1959 by 66.9% and approved it only in 1971, with 65.7% in favor. The canton of Appenzell Innerrhoden voted it down again in 1990, and the Federal Supreme Court imposed it on 27 November 1990 under the constitution's equality clause. In 2009 voters approved a ban on building minarets with 57.5% of the vote. That record is why initiatives here cannot override protected rights and stay subject to court review.

question"Won't voters simply pass anything that is put to them?"

The Swiss record says no. Of 237 federal popular initiatives put to a vote since 1891, only 26 were accepted by the people and the cantons, about 11%. Voters reject most proposals, which makes the initiative a check on the legislature rather than a replacement for it.

question"Doesn't low turnout let a small group decide?"

No. Average turnout in Swiss federal votes in 2009 was 46.1%, though the minaret vote that year drew 53.76%. There is no minimum turnout: a measure passes by more than half of the votes cast, with blank and spoiled ballots not counted. The goal is that the vote is held and open to every qualified voter — every person who clears the full election stack — even if only a small share takes part. Those unwilling to vote are subject to the choices of those who do, and the outcome rests with the participants.

question"How can there be a national initiative under the Constitution?"

Only through a constitutional amendment. Article I vests federal legislative power in Congress, and Article V allows amendments only through Congress or a convention of the states, so the national initiative is created by an amendment ratified in the ordinary way. Once in place, it lets citizens propose federal statutes and constitutional amendments directly. A proposal reaches the ballot with signatures from 1% of qualified voters in the last federal election for a statute, or 2% for an amendment, gathered within 18 months. These figures are my own choice, scaled from Switzerland's 100,000 signatures in 18 months, not a Swiss rule.

question"Wouldn't a national initiative let big states outvote small ones?"

Not on the Constitution. An amendment by initiative needs a double majority: a national popular majority and a majority of the states, 26 of 50, as Switzerland requires a majority of the people and of the cantons. A state counts toward that majority when its own voters approve the measure, just as the result of the popular vote in a canton determines that canton's vote. Article V already protects the states' role by requiring three-quarters of them to ratify amendments proposed by Congress. A federal statute by initiative needs a national popular majority and stays subject to the same rights limits and court review.

Vote Thresholds for State Amendments

Each state sets its own amendment process, but more than half of the votes cast should decide, which is the same rule that ought to govern national measures. Requiring 60% lets 40% plus one block what most voters chose. Protected rights are guarded by court review (Due Process and Rule of Law), not by supermajorities. My national rule makes amendments harder in a different way: they need a majority of the states as well as a majority of the votes. If I were voting, I would vote no on North Dakota Measure 1 and no on South Dakota Amendment L.

Rationale

Switzerland's system is the most developed model. A popular initiative needs 100,000 valid signatures within 18 months to force a national vote on a constitutional amendment. An optional referendum needs 50,000 signatures within 100 days to put a new law to a vote, and every constitutional amendment requires a double majority of voters and cantons. In the United States, 26 states provide a statewide initiative, referendum, or both. In Pacific States Telephone & Telegraph Co. v. Oregon (1912), the Supreme Court declined to hear a claim that Oregon's initiative process violated the Guarantee Clause, treating the question as one for Congress, so state initiatives stand. Initiatives fit my federalism and my preference for power held close to citizens (Federalism). The Swiss record also shows the risk: majorities have voted on the rights of minorities, and only a court restored women's vote in one canton. The initiative therefore ought to come paired with firm rights limits and the same voter qualifications that apply to every election: ID, the voter license test, net-taxpayer status, and the felon bar (net fiscal voting bar). At the national level the Constitution gives no initiative power today. So the United States ought to create one by amendment, with signature thresholds of 1% and 2% of qualified voters in the last federal election, my own figures scaled from the Swiss model. The double majority for amendments by initiative, 26 of 50 states counted by each state's own vote, keeps the states' role that Article V protects. Turnout is not a condition of validity: a vote open to every qualified voter is decided by more than half of the votes cast by those who take part.
