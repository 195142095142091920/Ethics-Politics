Core Principles

I stand for strict separation of religion and government in the United States. Religions that do not violate sentient rights should be tolerated; those that do should be restricted or banned in their harmful aspects. Private conscience and association ought to be protected, and public institutions should remain secular.

Role of Religion in Government

tldrIn short

Religion should stay out of government, and officials must not turn scripture into law. Adherents of the four excluded ideologies (Nazism, communism, socialism, and Islam) are barred from the sworn authority roles, and the state endorses no religion. I'd keep historic religious monuments but put up no new ones. Public schools should have no prayer. Private schools do as they like.

Position

Religious doctrine should never be involved in a governmental body, and in the United States religion should be neither mandated nor involved in government. Elected officials who are religious may be influenced ideologically and philosophically by their faith, but no one should be forced to follow their scriptures, and religious mandates should not become law. Religious symbols already on public property may remain for historical significance, and historically significant religious structures should not be taken down, but new ones should not be added. Prayer should be excluded from government-run public schools. Private schools may do as they wish on religious practice. The state ought to stay religion-neutral: the excluded-ideologies test applies to any doctrine, religious or secular (Excluded ideologies), and the mosque rules that follow from it are set out in Religious Freedom.

My view: You are free to be a moderate, practicing Muslim, but I would not allow a Muslim into an authority role, and the state should never endorse Islam. The constitutional amendment designating excluded ideologies bars adherents of Islam, Nazism, communism, and socialism from authority roles, and covers all four on the same terms for the bar on building, rebuilding, expanding, or relocating their places (Property Rights; Excluded ideologies). Authority roles are the sworn positions listed under Oath of Office and Perjury, from public office and policing to the courts and K-12 curriculum authority. An accountant, grounds worker, or other non-authority public employee is not under that bar. I judge every other candidate by their record and commitments: support for state-enforced religious law, or for purging the press or police, rules a candidate out for my vote, whatever their faith.

question"Does keeping historic religious symbols on public land contradict strict separation?"

No. Separation forbids the state from imposing religion, not from preserving its own history. A historic monument does not compel belief or practice. Adding new religious symbols would be a fresh act of government endorsement, which is why existing ones stay and new ones are not added.

Rationale

The First Amendment bars establishment of religion and protects its free exercise. The Supreme Court held in Engel v. Vitale that state-sponsored prayer in public schools violates the Establishment Clause, and in American Legion v. American Humanist Association that a long-standing cross memorial could remain on public land given its history. The position follows both: no state-directed religious practice, and no removal of historic monuments.

Oath of Office and Perjury

tldrIn short

I treat authority roles - elected or appointed office; law enforcement, military, and intelligence; judges and prosecutors; immigration and other adjudicators; K-12 public curriculum authority - as sworn positions. The oath rejects ideologies hostile to the country. Nazis, communists, socialists, and Muslims cannot truthfully swear it for an authority role, so open adherents of any of the four are barred from those roles under the excluded-ideologies amendment. Ordinary non-authority public employment stays open.

Lying on that oath should be perjury, a grave crime. Adherence is defined by something checkable, like a sworn statement or the group's own membership records; ancestry alone never counts. For a group with no membership records, adherence is shown by a sworn statement or the person's own public profession of the ideology, never ancestry alone. A high-risk origin means harder scrutiny, not an automatic bar. The oath is the filter, and free speech stays untouched.

My view: The country should be strong, with shared values. People who hold authority roles — elected or appointed office; law enforcement, military, and intelligence; judges and prosecutors; immigration and other adjudicators who decide status or benefits; and K-12 public curriculum authority — are servants of the people in power, not every grounds worker or accountant on a public payroll. Those authority roles are sworn positions: each takes an oath on the Constitution rejecting ideologies hostile to the country, and falls under the same perjury laws. The purpose is to keep an assimilated, high-trust society with shared values intact by keeping those ideologies out of power. A barred ideology is one that is dangerous, antithetical to the fabric of the country, unconstitutional, and disrespectful of rights, judged by one published test (Excluded ideologies). Nazis, communists, socialists, and Muslims do not fit: no adherent of any of the four can truthfully swear the oath for an authority role, so an open adherent should be barred from those roles, and a hidden one who swears it commits perjury. Adherence is defined by something checkable, like a sworn statement or the group's own membership records; ancestry alone never counts. For a group with no membership records, adherence is shown by a sworn statement or the person's own public profession of the ideology, never ancestry alone. Group origin, meaning place or faith of origin and never ancestry, sets the lens and intensity of questioning: a high-risk origin means harder scrutiny of doctrine, association, and adherence, not an automatic bar. The oath is the final filter. Eligibility after leaving returns after a formal renunciation that the group's own process recognizes, with perjury covering lies. For a faith with no central membership body or exit process, such as Islam, the renunciation is a sworn public renunciation, and lying in it is perjury (Group Statistics and Individual Judgment). Lying on that oath should be a grave criminal charge: perjury for seeking an authority role. Freedom of speech is preserved for everyone; the office and building bars are civic disabilities, not penalties (Excluded ideologies). The office disability does not bar adherents from ordinary non-authority taxpayer jobs.

Method and aim: My goal is that Islam, along with barred ideologies like communism, socialism, and Nazism, does not proliferate and dies out over time. I want that to happen through environment, not force. There are no roundups, and no individual's negative rights are disrespected. I do not want to remove Muslims or communists from the country by force. I want conditions where these ideologies cannot spread: no new mosques, no state endorsement, no place for them in authority roles, no building, rebuilding, expansion, or relocation of places that promote them (Property Rights), and full freedom of speech and private belief — including ordinary non-authority public employment. A high-trust society with shared values, built on negative rights and rights-respecting institutions, will produce this change over time.

question"If speech is absolute, how can professing an ideology bar someone from office?"

I believe free speech should protect what a person says from punishment, and here it does: no one is fined, jailed, or silenced for professing Nazism, communism, socialism, or Islam, and the government's condemnation is its own speech, not a limit on anyone else's (Cultural Stance). An authority role is not a right. It is a grant of state power, and it comes with an oath to uphold a rights order these four ideologies reject. A person's own public profession counts only as evidence of what they would be swearing against. The bar takes away no negative right; it withholds a power. That is a real cost to open adherents, and I accept it, because handing state power to an ideology that would use it to strip others of their rights is the larger threat to liberty. The people it protects are the ones who stand before that power: the woman in a custody hearing, the apostate before an immigration officer, the non-believer stopped by a police officer. A person wrongly barred loses one kind of job and keeps every right and every other career; a sworn officer who acts on a doctrine's rules on women, apostasy, or non-believers takes rights from people who had no say, the same imbalance that sets the admission presumption (Group presumption). The bar comes only through constitutional amendment, which overrides Article VI's religious-test clause for this one disability (Excluded ideologies).

Religious Freedom

tldrIn short

Religious bodies and private people should choose their associations freely, and businesses may decline expressive work on conscience, but no business open to the public may refuse a person for a protected trait. The government ought to act only when rights are violated or consumers need protection. I would ban religious practices that harm others, such as FGM and honor violence. Freedom of religion exists, but I support a constitutional amendment designating the excluded ideologies, religious or secular, as dangerous to the fabric of the nation. Four meet the test today: Nazism, communism, socialism, and Islam. Christianity, Judaism, other faiths, and atheism do not, so they are not barred. Muslims are free to practice as moderate believers in their homes; no new mosques may be built, and existing mosques are capped at today's number with no expansion, relocation, rebuilding, or state support; the state does not endorse Islam.

Position

Private individuals and religious bodies should choose their associations freely, including on religious conscience, and religious bodies should choose their own clergy and religious roles. A business may decline to create expressive work that violates its owner's conscience, for any customer. But a business open to the public may not refuse a person because of race, sex, sexual orientation, or another protected trait (Authority & Civil Liberties). Government should step in only when sentient rights are violated or to regulate for consumer protection. Religious practices that violate sentient rights, such as female genital mutilation, honor violence, and polygamy where it harms rights, ought to be banned outright.

My view: You are free to be a moderate, practicing Muslim and to practice in your home if you would like, but you cannot build mosques for a religion antithetical to Western cohesion, and the state should not endorse it. No new mosques may be built. Existing mosques may stay open but may not expand, relocate, or rebuild, their number is capped at today's, and none receives state support. Home practice covers the household and invited guests. A house run as a regular public place of worship, or an Islamic center whose main use is worship, counts as a mosque. Existing Islamic schools and centers may stay open, but new ones, and the rebuilding of old ones, fall under the same bar as places that promote Nazism or communism (Property Rights). Freedom of religion exists, but the Constitution should be amended to designate excluded ideologies, religious or secular, as dangerous to the fabric of the nation. It covers only Nazism, communism, socialism, and Islam on the same terms for the bars on building, rebuilding, expanding, or relocating places and on holding office (Property Rights; Excluded ideologies).

Method and aim: the same as under Oath of Office and Perjury: environment, not force, with no roundups and no removal from the country.

question"Why cap mosques instead of leaving Islam to persuasion?"

The cap is the least force I could find that still acts. No one loses a home, a job outside authority roles, the right to pray at home, or the right to argue for Islam, and every existing mosque stays open. What the cap stops is new institutions that pass the doctrine's rules on apostasy, women, and non-believers to the next generation (Excluded ideologies). The people it protects are the ones those rules fall on first: women, children raised in the faith, and anyone who leaves it. If I am wrong about how much those institutions matter, a peaceful congregation shares an existing mosque or prays at home. If I am right and nothing is done, the cost falls on people who never chose the doctrine. The bar falls on the institution, not on any one congregation's intentions, so peaceful congregations cannot build new places of worship either, and the same bar falls on places that promote Nazism or communism (Property Rights).

question"Does protecting religious conscience permit discrimination?"

Not against persons. A business may decline a message or project it objects to, such as a design, whoever asks. It should not be allowed to turn a customer away for who they are. Religious bodies should choose their own members and clergy. Their commercial businesses should follow the same rule as any business open to the public.

question"Can a church hire clergy by its own faith rules even if that excludes by sex?"

Yes. Religious groups choose their own clergy and religious leaders by their faith, under the ministerial exception the Supreme Court unanimously recognized in Hosanna-Tabor v. EEOC (2012). The exception covers ministers and religious leaders only. Ordinary jobs at religious groups, such as janitors and accountants, stay under the equal-treatment rule.

Rationale

Religious liberty protects belief and practice, but not practices that harm others. The Supreme Court held in Reynolds v. United States that religious belief does not exempt conduct from general criminal law, and federal law criminalizes female genital mutilation of minors. Masterpiece Cakeshop shows the ongoing conflict between conscience and public-accommodation law; the law ought to protect refusal of expressive work, as the Supreme Court did in 303 Creative v. Elenis, while barring refusal of persons by protected trait.

Religious Exemptions

tldrIn short

I believe religion gives no exemption from basic taxes. Churches should pay taxes like everyone else, and paying taxes gives them no extra say in government.

Position

There should be no religious exemptions from the bare minimum of public taxes that everyone pays. Religious status should create no exemption; general rules such as the zero income-tax bracket apply equally to everyone. Churches and religious organizations should pay taxes like any other institution. Paying taxes gives them no right to influence government or hold power to push doctrine, because church-state separation in law comes before tax collection, and the rule of law and sentient-rights protections hold more strongly over any taxpaying individual, organization, or corporation.

question"Would taxing churches give them a claim to political influence?"

No. Every taxpayer funds the government without gaining the right to direct it. Churches would stand in the same position as any business or institution: paying their share under law while remaining barred by church-state separation from imposing doctrine.

Rationale

Equal treatment under law requires that religious organizations carry the same obligations as others. Churches are currently exempt under federal tax law, and the Supreme Court upheld property-tax exemptions in Walz v. Tax Commission, though exemption is a legislative choice rather than a constitutional requirement. Removing religious exemptions applies one rule to all citizens.

Conflict, Persecution, and Hijra

tldrIn short

question"What is Hijra, and how did Islam spread?"

The Hijra is Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina in 622 to escape persecution. It marks year one of the Islamic calendar, which Caliph Umar introduced in 639. After Muhammad's death in 632, the Rashidun and then the Umayyad caliphs spread Islamic rule largely by military conquest. Umayyad armies reached Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent, and in 711 crossed into Spain and took most of the Iberian Peninsula. Muhammad went to Medina on the invitation of its feuding clans, whom he had been asked to reconcile, and there negotiated the Constitution of Medina, which made the Muslim community a political entity for the first time.

The Quran makes migration a religious duty for believers who are oppressed where they live. Of those who stayed behind under oppression, it says the angels will ask: "Was not the earth of Allāh spacious [enough] for you to emigrate therein?" and continues, "For those, their refuge is Hell" (4:97), excepting the oppressed "who cannot devise a plan" (4:98). It promises: "whoever emigrates for the cause of Allāh will find on the earth many [alternative] locations and abundance" (4:100), and it ties political loyalty to migration: "those who believed and did not emigrate - for you there is no support of them until they emigrate" (8:72). A hadith graded sahih by al-Albani says: "Migration will not end until repentance ends, and repentance will not end until the sun rises in the west" (Sunan Abi Dawud 2479). Another, in Sahih al-Bukhari, says: "There is no Hijra (i.e. migration) (from Mecca to Medina) after the Conquest (of Mecca), but Jihad and good intention remain" (Bukhari 2783).

Classical jurists built on these texts by dividing the world into the abode of Islam (dār al-Islām) and the abode of unbelief or war (dār al-ḥarb). According to the legal historian Khaled Abou El Fadl, the Mālikī school held that Muslims must not reside in non-Muslim lands and must emigrate from them, while other schools allowed residence where Muslims could practice their faith, and the Ḥanafīs rejected a general duty to emigrate. Abou El Fadl concludes that the juristic discourse "was not dogmatic and does not lend itself to essentialist positions." The exegetes Quraish Shihab and Nāṣir Makārim Shīrāzī also read hijra as leaving sin as well as physical migration, citing the hadith that "a Muhajir (emigrant) is the one who gives up (abandons) all what Allah has forbidden" (Bukhari 10).

Current events show this idea of Hijra in practice: the uptick of Muslims in the EU, in office, and in the USA. We see very clear trends of Muslim migration and a changing demographic. Pew estimated Europe's Muslim population (the EU's then 28 members plus Norway and Switzerland) at 19.5 million, or 3.8%, in 2010 and 25.8 million, or 4.9%, in 2016. About 60% of that growth came from net migration of about 3.5 million. Births exceeded deaths by more than 2.9 million, and slightly more people left Islam than joined it. Pew projects a Muslim share of 7.4% in 2050 even with zero migration, 11.2% if regular migration continues, and 14% if the 2014–2016 refugee flows continue, with Sweden reaching 30.6% in that high scenario. In the U.S., the first Muslim member of Congress, Keith Ellison, took office in 2007. The 119th Congress has four Muslim members, all in the House, or 0.8% of members. In January 2026, Zohran Mamdani became New York City's first Muslim mayor. U.S. population figures are in Public Opinion Trends.

Sharia as State Law

tldrIn short

I would protect private religious practice in the home. No new mosques, and existing ones capped (Religious Freedom). Sharia enforced as state law should not be protected, because it punishes belief, speech, and consensual private conduct and gives people unequal legal standing by sex and religion. In my view these rules violate negative rights whatever their source.

Position

A Muslim who follows religious rules in private life at home, in worship, diet, prayer, or voluntary religious arbitration that civil courts can review, should not be penalized, within the mosque limits in Religious Freedom. My objection is to the state enforcing religious law on people who have not consented or who have harmed no one. Where governments enforce sharia as criminal and family law, the record includes death or prison for leaving Islam or insulting religion, stoning, flogging, and amputation as fixed (hudud) punishments, criminal penalties for consensual same-sex relations, and rules that give women and non-Muslims lesser legal standing. Each of these punishes belief, identity, or speech instead of harm, or uses force against a person who violated no one's rights.

question"Isn't this an attack on Islam itself?"

This section's objection is aimed at laws, not at believers. The judgment of Islam as a religion is set out in Islam and Extremism. Within my framework, a Muslim keeps freedom of private worship at home, speech, and association, though no new mosques may be built and existing ones are capped, and a Christian, Jewish, or secular legal code that did the same things would fail the same test. What fails is any state, under any religion, that punishes apostasy, blasphemy, or consensual private conduct.

question"Muslims in many countries support sharia. Isn't overriding them undemocratic?"

Partly. In Pew's 2013 survey, majorities in many countries favored making sharia official law. But a majority vote cannot authorize violating an individual's negative rights. That limit applies to every majority, including American ones (Political Philosophy). Pew also found that most supporters of making sharia official law do not believe it should apply to non-Muslims, and views on specific punishments vary widely by country.

question"Doesn't sharia also protect rights, for example property, contracts, and some inheritance rights for women?"

Only where it overlaps with rules that stand on their own. Classical Islamic law has detailed rules on contracts and property, and in some cases women inherit equal or larger shares. Where a rule matches what consenting parties would agree to, or protects person and property, it is acceptable on its own merits. My objection is limited to rules enforced by the state that punish belief or consensual conduct or make legal rights depend on sex or religion.

question"Isn't criticizing another culture's legal system Western imperialism?"

No. The standard is the same negative rights applied to everyone, not Western custom. The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights said Brunei's 2019 Syariah Penal Code would enshrine "cruel and inhuman punishments that seriously breach international human rights law." I hold Western governments to the same test, as with my own limits on surveillance, speech restrictions, and religious favoritism.

Rationale

The state ought to protect persons from force and fraud and ought not to punish belief, identity, or speech; the two civic disabilities for excluded ideologies are not punishments. State-enforced sharia does so in documented ways. A 2022 Pew analysis found that in 2019, 22 countries had laws against apostasy, 13 of them in the Middle East and North Africa, and that 18 of the region's 20 countries criminalized blasphemy. Blasphemy laws also exist in Europe and the Americas, so blasphemy laws are not unique to sharia, but apostasy laws were found only in Africa, the Middle East, and Asia-Pacific. Brunei's 2019 revisions provided death for adultery, sodomy, and insulting the Prophet, public flogging for abortion, and amputation for theft, according to the UN human rights office. ILGA World reports that 65 UN member states criminalize consensual same-sex acts, and that 7 legally prescribe death, with legal uncertainty in 5 more. Many, though not all, of these base the penalty on sharia. In Saudi Arabia, a UNDP review found that sharia inheritance rules apply, under which a daughter in the common case receives half a son's share, while noting cases where women receive more.

Survey data show that support is real but uneven. In Pew's 2013 survey, support for making sharia official law ranged from 8% in Azerbaijan to 99% in Afghanistan. Among those who favored it, 86% in Egypt and 76% in Pakistan supported death for leaving Islam. This is why my country-level immigration presumption is tied to where Islamist movements govern or hold majority support, not to a country's religion as such. The separate presumption for individual Muslim applicants is set out in Islam and Extremism.

Each listed rule fails a test I apply everywhere. Apostasy and blasphemy laws punish belief and speech, which I protect up to true threats and incitement (Free Speech and Censorship). Hudud punishments impose mutilation for property crimes and death for consensual acts. Same-sex criminalization punishes consensual conduct that harms no one (LGBTQ+ and Identity Issues). Unequal inheritance and testimony rules make legal standing depend on sex or religion rather than on conduct.

The Islamic Faith in Practice

tldrIn short

I oppose Islamism and the Islamic faith as classically taught and widely practiced. Its mainstream legal tradition gives non-Muslims and women lesser standing and prescribes death for leaving the faith, and surveys show many Muslims still hold these views. Muslims keep their rights to life, liberty, and property and may practice privately at home, with no new mosques and existing ones capped (Religious Freedom). They should not be allowed to serve in an official capacity (Role of Religion in Government).

Position

My opposition to Islam extends beyond the political project of ruling by Islamic law (Islam and Extremism) to the faith itself. It does not mean only state enforcement (Sharia as State Law). I'm an agnostic atheist and accept no scripture as moral authority (Metaphysics). I judge every religion's teachings by the same test: whether they respect each sentient individual's equal negative rights. Measured that way, several central teachings of mainstream Islam fail. These include texts and classical rulings that rank believers above nonbelievers, give men authority over women, and treat leaving the faith as a capital offense. Surveys show these teachings still shape the views of large shares of Muslims today. I hold this judgment openly, while protecting every Muslim's freedom to believe and speak and to worship privately at home, and judging individual Muslims by their own conduct; the only consequences of adherence are the two civic disabilities for excluded ideologies.

question"Muslims are diverse, and many reject these teachings. Isn't this unfair to them?"

Muslims differ, but that does not change the judgment. Pew found that support for making sharia official law ranged from 8% in Azerbaijan to 99% in Afghanistan, and that outside sub-Saharan Africa most Muslims surveyed say a woman should decide for herself whether to wear a veil. Some contemporary scholars argue that the Quran sets no worldly punishment for apostasy, pointing to "There shall be no compulsion in [acceptance of] the religion" (2:256). I judge the tradition by its classical rulings and by what large shares of believers report, not only by its most liberal interpreters. I judge individual Muslims by their own conduct.

question"The Bible has harsh texts too. Why single out Islam?"

Other scriptures contain harsh texts too, and I reject them on the same grounds, since I accept no scripture as moral authority. The difference is in present practice, not in the texts alone. Pew found that the 22 countries with apostasy laws in 2019 were all in Africa, the Middle East, and Asia-Pacific. The Library of Congress found eight Muslim-majority states that make apostasy a capital crime. Pew's surveys show that majorities of Muslims in many countries support such rules. I apply one standard and report where it's failed most often today.

question"Isn't this bigotry toward Muslims?"

No. Bigotry is hostility toward people for who they are. This critique is aimed at doctrines, the way I also criticize Christianity, communism, and my own opponents' ideas. It does not call for any legal penalty for being Muslim, believing, or worshipping privately at home. The two civic disabilities for excluded ideologies, the authority-role bar and the building bar that caps mosques, are not penalties. Muslims keep free speech and equal treatment under the law, apart from those two disabilities (Religious Freedom). A free society should allow criticism of every religion, including the largest ones.

question"Doesn't taqiyya mean Muslims are permitted to lie to non-Muslims?"

It permits concealment. Taqiyya is the precautionary concealment of one's faith under threat of persecution, based on Quran 3:28 and 16:106. It is stressed mainly by Shia Muslims, who were a persecuted minority. According to the Encyclopaedia Iranica, Sunni scholars accept it more narrowly, chiefly under compulsion, while Shia jurists allow it more widely, including in relations with other Muslims, and some recognize forms for keeping secrets or easing tensions with non-Shia. A doctrine that permits concealing one's faith is a real exception to candor.

Rationale

Nonbelievers. The Quran directs believers to "fight against those who do not believe in Allāh... from those who were given the Scripture... until they give the jizyah willingly while they are humbled" (9:29). Classical law turned this into dhimmi status. Jews, Christians, and later others received protection of life, property, and worship in exchange for the jizya tax, and Britannica notes that it was paid "as a mark of their submission." Restrictions on dress, occupation, and residence were often applied, according to The Oxford Dictionary of Islam. Historians at the University of Pennsylvania describe the Pact of ʿUmar, part of Islamic law by the ninth century, as laws meant to "emphasize the social superiority of Muslims." Britannica notes that jizya is no longer collected in modern Muslim nation-states. I count that as progress, but the classical rule ranked people by religion.

Apostasy. The classical Sunni and Shia schools agreed that a sane adult male who voluntarily renounced Islam should be executed. They differed on details, such as whether he must first be invited to repent and whether female apostates could be executed or only imprisoned. The Library of Congress found in 2014 that Afghanistan, Brunei, Mauritania, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Sudan, the United Arab Emirates, and Yemen expressly make apostasy a capital offense. Few death sentences have been carried out, and repentance often voids the conviction. Among Muslims who favor making sharia official law, Pew found 86% in Egypt and 76% in Pakistan supported death for those who leave Islam.

Women. The Quran states that "men are in charge of women," that "righteous women are devoutly obedient," and that husbands may, as a last step, "strike" wives they fear are defiant (4:34). It gives a son "the share of two females" in inheritance (4:11). For debt contracts it requires "two witnesses from among your men," or "a man and two women" (2:282). It also allows a man up to four wives (4:3), with no equivalent right for women. In Pew's 2013 survey, at least half of Muslims in 20 of 23 countries said a wife must always obey her husband, and about nine in ten or more did so in every South and Southeast Asian country surveyed. Views on divorce and inheritance were more divided.

Breadth of support. These views are held by ordinary believers across whole countries, not only by Islamist movements. In Pew's 2013 survey, most Muslims in South Asia, Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and the Middle East and North Africa favored making sharia the official law of their country, including 91% in Iraq, 89% in the Palestinian territories, 86% in Malaysia and Niger, 84% in Pakistan, 82% in Bangladesh, and 72% in Indonesia. Among those supporters, at least half backed executing people who leave Islam in six of the 20 countries with adequate samples, including 82% in Jordan, 79% in Afghanistan, 66% in the Palestinian territories, and 62% in Malaysia. Muslims who pray several times a day were more likely to favor sharia as official law.

Compulsory covering. Pew notes that all the main schools of Islamic law mandate that women veil, although the Quran itself does not specifically require a veil. Where that law is state law, covering is compulsory. Iran's Islamic Penal Code criminalizes appearing in public without the hijab or in "improper hijab," punished by fines and imprisonment, and the UN fact-finding mission on Iran found that the arrest of Jina Mahsa Amini, who died in morality-police custody in 2022, was based on those laws. Afghanistan's Law on the Promotion of Virtue and Prevention of Vice, approved by the Taliban leader in July 2024, states that "covering the entire body of a woman is mandatory," requires women to cover their faces from unrelated men, and requires a woman who leaves her home to conceal her voice, face, and body.

Children. While some modern writers and apologists claim Aisha was older, this was very likely not the case. Sahih al-Bukhari, which Sunni Muslims accept as second only to the Quran as a source of religious law, and Sahih Muslim, the other of the two most trusted Sunni collections, report in separate narrations that the Prophet married her at six and consummated the marriage at nine (Bukhari 3894, 5133, 5134, 5158; Muslim 1422). In Aisha's own account she was called in from playing on a swing with her friends, prepared by the women of the house, and handed over to him, "and at that time I was a girl of nine years of age" (Bukhari 3894). She also played with dolls in his presence, which the commentary cited from Fath al-Bari explains by her being a little girl who had not yet reached puberty (Bukhari 6130). The scholar Jonathan A.C. Brown, as summarized in a 2023 academic study, finds that pre-modern fiqh and hadith scholarship never questioned these reports and that the four mainstream Sunni schools of law permit a father to marry off a young daughter. Kecia Ali, reviewing the early biographies, finds they typically give six or seven at the contract and nine, occasionally ten, at consummation. The revision is modern: Brown traces the first Muslim challenge to the Egyptian writer Abbas Aqqad in the twentieth century, after Egypt began limiting the marriage age in the 1920s. The Quran calls the Prophet "an excellent example" for believers (33:21). I count a teaching that holds up marriage to a child as a model as a direct violation of a child's rights.

Taqiyya. The Oxford Dictionary of Islam defines taqiyya as "precautionary denial of religious belief in the face of potential persecution." The Encyclopaedia Iranica adds that Sunni scholars accept the principle but restrict it, while Shia scholars allow it more widely. I treat a doctrine permitting concealment as a real exception to candor.

My response is the same as for any belief system I reject. I argue openly, protect the believer's life, liberty, and property, and refuse to let any doctrine, religious or secular, override an individual's negative rights.

Ritual Slaughter and Animal Sacrifice

tldrIn short

I would ban ritual slaughter and animal sacrifice outright for every faith. Stunning rules do not matter because the ban is total.

Position

I would ban ritual slaughter outright, along with animal sacrifice. Religion should create no exemption from the rules protecting sentient beings, so no faith may kill animals as part of a ritual. Because the ban is total, rules on stunning or slaughter method are irrelevant to it. Animal agriculture itself should be banned; lab-grown meat is the goal and the replacement, and it may not meet demand at the same cost right away as it scales up over time (Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat; lab-grown meat).

question"Isn't an outright ban targeting particular faiths?"

No. The ban should apply to every faith alike, and no religion should be singled out or exempted. Animals hold rights under my framework, and a religious purpose does not change what is done to the animal.

Rationale

Federal law now treats ritual slaughter as humane by statute (7 U.S.C. § 1902(b)), a religious carve-out the ban removes. In Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah (1993) the Supreme Court struck down ordinances that singled out animal sacrifice while permitting other killing; a ban that applies to every faith equally is designed to avoid that defect. Animal protections are set out in Bioethics, Progress, & Technology.

Christianity and Western Heritage

tldrIn short

I view Western heritage and moderate Christianity as good, and I see a Western society shaped by moderate Christianity as culturally better than an anti-Western Islamic one. Scripture still should not be taught in public settings, and I reject extremism in any religion.

Position

Scripture should not be mandated to be taught in any public setting, but I believe Western heritage and Christian values are generally positive. The nation's identity was built on one of God and on the values of religious freedom. Christianity is positive in its moderation and dangerous in its extremism. As a cultural judgment, a Western society shaped by moderate Christianity is absolutely superior to an anti-Western Islamic society.

question"Does calling a Christian society superior conflict with government neutrality toward religion?"

No. The judgment is about which societies best respect sentient rights and liberty, not a policy of state religion. Government should stay neutral and not teach scripture. The comparison expresses my cultural assessment, and I reject extremism within Christianity on the same terms as any other.

Rationale

I measure cultures by how well they protect individual rights, liberty, and responsibility. I credit Western societies shaped by moderate Christianity with developing religious freedom, separation of church and state, and individual rights, which are the values I defend. Keeping scripture out of public instruction preserves the separation that made that freedom possible.

Atheism, Secularism, and Faith

tldrIn short

The government should stay neutral toward religions that respect rights. Any part of a religion that violates rights ought to be banned, and I want no blasphemy laws.

Position

Government ought to be neutral toward religions that are not in battle with sentient rights. If any religion, for any reason, battles against sentient rights, that aspect of the religion should be banned. I prefer separation of religion and government. There should be no blasphemy laws. Religions designated as excluded by constitutional amendment, as dangerous to the fabric of the nation, should not be covered by this neutrality (Religious Freedom). Today that is Islam alone; Christianity, Judaism, other faiths, and atheism keep full neutrality (Excluded ideologies).

question"Does banning parts of a religion violate religious freedom?"

Religious freedom covers belief and practice that leave others' rights intact. Only the aspects of a religion that violate sentient rights should be banned. The rest of the faith ought to remain free. No belief is protected when it is acted out against another sentient being.

Rationale

Neutrality protects every faith equally as long as it does not harm others, except religions designated as excluded by constitutional amendment, and the same sentient-rights test applies to every religion. Blasphemy laws protect beliefs from criticism, which conflicts with my free-speech position. Under Employment Division v. Smith, neutral laws of general applicability may be enforced even when they burden religious practice, which supports banning rights-violating practices.

infoWhat is a blasphemy law?

A blasphemy law punishes speech or acts considered insulting to a religion, its figures, or sacred objects.

Religious Upbringing and Children

tldrIn short

I believe parents can raise children in their faith and choose religious schools or homeschooling. Religion must be no defense for medical neglect, abuse, forced marriage, or genital mutilation.

Position

I believe parents may raise their children in their faith, teach them its beliefs, and choose religious schooling or homeschooling. Children hold full negative rights from the start, so religious belief gives no license to violate them. Withholding necessary medical care from a child in danger, physical abuse, forced marriage, and genital mutilation should be banned regardless of religious motive, as with any other rights-violating practice. The state ought to act when a child's rights are violated, not because of the content of a family's belief.

question"Doesn't this put the state between parents and their faith?"

No. Belief, worship, and religious teaching are untouched. The state intervenes only at the same point it would for any family: when a child is being harmed.

Rationale

A review of 172 children who died between 1975 and 1995 after their parents withheld medical care in reliance on religious rituals found that 140 of the deaths were from conditions for which survival with medical care would have exceeded 90%. In Prince v. Massachusetts (1944) the Supreme Court held that parents may be free to become martyrs themselves but are not free to make martyrs of their children, upholding child-protection law against a religious claim. The ban on rights-violating religious practices is set out in Atheism, Secularism, and Faith, and the rights of minors in Ethical and Philosophical Framework.

High-Control Groups

tldrIn short

Groups should never be punished for what they believe. The institutions of excluded ideologies fall under the two civic disabilities, which are not penalties. In my view force, fraud, coerced labor, and abuse must be prosecuted, and members can always leave.

Position

Belief should never be penalized, and no group should be punished for what it teaches; the two civic disabilities for excluded ideologies are not penalties. Outside them, restrictions ought to attach only to conduct: confinement, threats, violence, coerced labor, fraud, and the abuse of minors. A religious, political, or other group that commits those acts should be prosecuted like any other organization, and its leaders are responsible for violations they direct. For fines, the head of a nonprofit or religious organization answers by default, as a CEO does (Income-Scaled Fines). Members who wish to leave should be free to do so.

question"Can a group be stopped without punishing what its members believe?"

Yes. You charge the acts. Holding someone against their will, taking money by fraud, or forcing labor is a crime whether or not the group calls itself a church. What a member believes in private would be left alone. Belief does count in one place: an open Nazi, communist, socialist, or Muslim cannot truthfully take the oath for an authority role, so the values test would keep them out of office, policing, the military, the courts, and the other sworn posts (office bar). Nobody would be jailed or fined for a belief.

Rationale

Federal law already criminalizes obtaining labor or services by force, threats, serious harm, or abuse of legal process (18 U.S.C. § 1589), without regard to the group's beliefs. The rule that belief alone carries no criminal penalty appears in Criminal Justice & Security; the authority-role bar is in the office bar.
