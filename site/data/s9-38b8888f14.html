Core Principles

I support a practical, gradual transition guided by free-market innovation and sentient-rights considerations. Policy should avoid disruptive mandates and taxpayer-funded subsidies. It ought to prioritize human (and herbivore) welfare over apocalyptic framing while conserving nature where it does not conflict with rights or development.

Climate Change

tldrIn short

Climate change is real and nearly all recent warming is human-caused (IPCC), but it is not apocalyptic under the IPCC AR6 scenario ranges I treat as the central case (serious harm, not civilization collapse). Any shift to greener infrastructure should be slow and market-driven, with no mandates and no source forced off early, so energy supply is not disrupted. Animal agriculture ought to be banned and shut down immediately as a rights violation, which also cuts a material share of human-caused warming (~12% FAO livestock agrifood systems, 2015 baseline).

Position

The climate varies naturally, but the IPCC finds that essentially all warming since the late 1800s is human-caused. The threat is real but not apocalyptic: under AR6, long-term (2081–2100) global warming versus 1850–1900 is very likely about 1.3–2.4°C in a low-emissions case (SSP1-2.6, best estimate 1.8°C), about 2.1–3.5°C in an intermediate case (SSP2-4.5, best estimate 2.7°C), and about 3.3–5.7°C in a very high-emissions case (SSP5-8.5, best estimate 4.4°C). Likely global mean sea-level rise by 2100 versus 1995–2014 is about 0.32–0.62 m (SSP1-2.6), 0.44–0.76 m (SSP2-4.5), and 0.63–1.01 m (SSP5-8.5) under medium-confidence ice processes. That is serious, uneven harm - coasts, heat, extremes - not human extinction or civilization collapse as the central projection. Low-likelihood ice-sheet outcomes under very high emissions can go higher; if credible evidence shows extreme unjustified death-risk to future sentient beings, greenhouse-gas limits should tighten under Pollution and Liability, still without energy mandates or early forced shutdowns of reliable supply. Policy ought to support long-term wellbeing of sentient individuals without upending energy supply chains. Any transition to greener infrastructure should come slowly, through markets and private innovation, with no mandates and no early forced shutdowns (Grid Reliability and Permitting). Ending animal agriculture, which should be banned as a rights violation and shut down immediately (Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat), also cuts livestock agrifood-system emissions; the ban rests on rights, and the climate gain is a side effect.

question"If climate change is real, why not act faster?"

In my judgment, rapid transition would disrupt energy supply chains that sentient beings depend on, causing harm of its own. That is a judgment about risk, not a measured cost estimate. A slow, market-driven shift addresses the problem without that disruption.

question"Doesn't carbon pricing cut emissions?"

Carbon-pricing advocates cite a 2024 meta-analysis of 80 evaluations covering 21 schemes, which found emissions cuts of 5% to 21% in the 17 schemes with significant effects, or 4% to 15% after correcting for publication bias. A cut bought with a government-imposed price on energy is still imposed. The shift to greener energy stays slow and market-driven, with no mandates and no source forced off early, so energy supply is not disrupted.

question"What about catastrophic tail risk?"

Present agents ought not to impose extreme, unjustified risk of death on future sentient beings (Duties to Future Generations). If credible evidence shows that risk, the harm-based greenhouse-gas limits in Pollution and Liability can be tightened in proportion to it, with the burden of proof on the rule. That still means no energy mandates and no early forced shutdowns of reliable supply.

Rationale

The IPCC attributes recent warming to human activity: it puts the human-caused rise from 1850–1900 to 2010–2019 at about 1.07°C (likely 0.8–1.3°C), out of about 1.1°C observed, with natural drivers contributing between −0.1°C and +0.1°C. It projects the scenario ranges above. FAO's 2023 Pathways towards lower emissions assessment (GLEAM) puts livestock agrifood systems — farm gate, land-use change tied to feed, and key supply-chain steps — at about 6.2 GtCO₂eq in the 2015 reference year, about 12% of anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions. That is a material share, not the largest human driver (fossil energy is larger). The 2006 Livestock's Long Shadow figure of about 18% and its livestock-versus-transport comparison are not used here; that comparison mixed a full livestock life-cycle with direct transport emissions only and was later corrected in the literature. The ~12% figure links the sentientist case against animal agriculture to the climate question as a side effect. Measured transition balances long-term wellbeing against present needs.

Adaptation and Disaster Policy

tldrIn short

I believe climate adaptation should be practical. Owners carry the risk of where they build, subsidies need opt-in funding, the 18 percent flood insurance cap should be removed, and emergency rescue stays public.

Position

Climate change is real but gradual, so adaptation should be handled as a practical matter. Taxpayer subsidies for risk, including subsidized insurance for building in high-risk areas, ought to require opt-in funding like any other subsidy. Owners should bear the risks of where and how they build, and private insurance should price those risks. I would remove the 18 percent statutory cap on annual flood-insurance premium increases.

question"What happens to people hit by a disaster?"

Emergency response, rescue, and public safety remain government functions. What changes is the long-term subsidy of rebuilding in high-risk places, which shifts costs to taxpayers who did not choose the risk.

question"Should firefighting be exempt from pollution rules?"

No. Fighting wildfire is an emergency function, and retardant should stay available because fire kills animals as well as people. Drops in waterways and protected habitat ought to stay off-limits except to save lives, and an agency that kills fish with a drop ought to answer for it like any other polluter (Pollution and Liability). The current administration's one-year exemption is graded in Current Administration.

Rationale

The National Flood Insurance Program owed the Treasury $22.525 billion after borrowing $2 billion on February 10, 2025, according to the Congressional Research Service. Under FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0, about one-third of National Flood Insurance Program policyholders already pay full-risk premiums, and 9 percent will eventually need increases of more than 300 percent, according to the Government Accountability Office. Annual increases for most policyholders are limited to 18 percent by statute; GAO estimated it would take until 2037 for 95 percent of current policies to reach full-risk premiums, leaving a $27 billion premium shortfall. Because the cap keeps premiums below the real risk for more than a decade, it should be removed. Any assistance for policyholders comes only through opt-in funding, not taxpayer subsidies. The no-subsidy rule is set out in Subsidies and Mandates.

International Climate Agreements

tldrIn short

I welcome awareness efforts. Position

I can support international efforts to raise awareness of human-induced climate change. The United States should not be beholden to any accord that goes against the freedom of its people and nation. Accords that align with reasonable U.S. conservation and regulatory laws for the public safety of sentient beings can be followed. question"Does withdrawing from accords undermine global climate efforts?"

Accords consistent with reasonable U.S. conservation and safety law can be followed. What I reject is binding commitments that override the freedom of Americans.

Rationale

Sovereignty applies to climate policy as to foreign policy. The Paris Agreement relies on nationally determined contributions, which shows that climate commitments can be set by each nation's own priorities.

Energy Policy

tldrIn short

Any shift toward cleaner energy should be slow and market-driven, with no mandates and no early forced shutdowns. I'd encourage nuclear power mainly by cutting red tape, with any public money coming only from taxpayers who choose to chip in. Renewables are welcome but not required, and energy markets ought to stay competitive without taxpayer subsidies.

Position

I accept a gradual, market-driven shift toward cleaner energy sources, with no mandates and no source forced to shut down early (Grid Reliability and Permitting). Nuclear power should be incentivized, first through deregulation such as streamlined licensing and permitting. Any public financial support for nuclear ought to be drawn only from taxpayers who opt in to fund it; no taxpayer should be compelled to subsidize nuclear or any other energy industry. Renewables may be encouraged but should not be mandated. Energy markets ought to remain competitive and consumer-oriented, without taxpayer subsidies (absent opt-in) or coercive industrial policy.

question"Does incentivizing nuclear contradict opposition to subsidies?"

No. Nuclear is incentivized first by removing the regulatory and permitting obstacles that add to its cost and delay, and any public money comes only from taxpayers who opt in, consistent with Energy & Environment. Nuclear is singled out because of its safety record and reliability. Energy policy is set out further in Energy & Environment.

Rationale

A reliable, affordable energy supply underpins economic growth, so transition should not disrupt it. Nuclear power produces large amounts of low-carbon electricity with one of the lowest death rates per unit of energy of any source. Construction cost is the largest barrier to new reactors, so streamlined licensing works alongside standardized designs and market competition rather than replacing them. Competitive markets drive innovation more efficiently than mandates.

Pollution and Liability

tldrIn short

Pollution that harms people violates their rights. I'd limit it to what public-health standards allow, make polluters pay for the damage, and count greenhouse gases as pollution above the natural baseline, regulated as harm, without energy mandates or early forced shutdowns.

Position

Pollution that harms others, or directly imposes a severe, non-consensual risk of serious harm on them, violates their negative rights. It should be regulated to a level that meets consumer and public-health standards, and polluters ought to be liable for the harm they cause to people and property. Greenhouse gases count as pollution to the extent that emissions exceed the natural baseline; the natural contribution is not attributed to anyone. Greenhouse-gas rules should be harm rules, not energy mandates: they ought to limit emissions in proportion to the harm shown, never prescribe a fuel, technology, or energy source, and set compliance periods long enough that no source is forced off early and reliable supply is not disrupted (Climate Change; Grid Reliability and Permitting). Each environmental rule should carry the burden of showing the harm it addresses.

exampleDocumented accounts

question"Doesn't pollution regulation conflict with your skepticism of regulation?"

No. Preventing serious environmental harm is one of the four domains where business regulation is legitimate. The burden-of-proof rule still applies: a pollution rule should show the harm it prevents, and rules that cannot should be removed.

question"How can anyone be liable for diffuse greenhouse-gas harm?"

Where harm cannot be traced to a specific emitter, emissions above the natural baseline are handled by the harm-based limits above rather than by individual lawsuits. Traceable harm to persons or property remains liable in court.

Rationale

Axiom 6 treats the direct imposition of severe, non-consensual risk as crossing the forfeiture threshold, and property rights protect owners against physical invasion of their land and bodies. Air pollution is the clearest documented case. EPA's peer-reviewed second prospective study estimated that the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments would prevent over 230,000 early deaths in 2020, with central benefits exceeding costs by more than 30 to one. Climate has a natural baseline, and only the human-caused share above it is counted as pollution; the IPCC finds that share is essentially all recent warming. Rules with demonstrated effects meet the burden of proof in Domestic Policy & Governance.

Regulation and Conservation

tldrIn short

Environmental rules should protect sanctuary lands and herbivore populations, and I want pollution regulated to keep people healthy.

Position

Environmental regulations should exist on the specific grounds of conserving and maintaining sanctuary lands and herbivorous animal populations. Pollution and waste ought to be regulated to a level that meets consumer and public health standards.

question"Why protect herbivores specifically rather than all wildlife?"

Herbivores can live without causing harm to other sentient beings, so the government ought to give their populations and habitats priority protection. This follows from the sentientist ethic set out in Ethical and Philosophical Framework.

question"Should the law protect animals killed by habitat loss or accidents, not just hunting?"

Yes. An animal killed by the destruction of its habitat, or by a strike the actor could foresee, is as dead as one that was targeted. Whoever causes that death ought to answer for it, as polluters do (Pollution and Liability), graded by sentience class and by intent: deliberate killing is murder. Foreseeable, avoidable killing is a lesser, non-capital liability.

Rationale

Regulation is justified where it protects sentient beings from harm. Pollution harms public health, which grounds rules like the Clean Air Act. Conservation regulation centers on the populations I value most. About 12.9% of U.S. land and inland waters is currently protected and managed mainly for biodiversity, the baseline sanctuary policy builds from.

Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat

tldrIn short

I would ban animal agriculture outright and shut it down immediately, with no wind-down. Every animal alive at the shutdown should be kept alive and given sanctuary for its natural life under the Sanctuary Plan, which also sets out what happens to the firms, the land and the workers. Federal grazing permits are revoked on day one, habitat rules protect sanctuary lands and herbivores, and lab-grown meat is the goal and the replacement. It may not meet demand at the same cost right away and scales up over time. The ban does not wait for it (Agriculture and Food Policy).

Position

Animal agriculture should be banned outright and shut down immediately. Farming sentient animals for food is a rights violation, so the ban should not be phased in, and there should be no wind-down.

noteHow this gets done: the Sanctuary Plan

There is no wind-down. The shutdown, the care and adoption of every animal alive at that moment, the conversion, divestment or liquidation of the firms, the land, the workers, enforcement, and the Sanctuary Levy that pays for it are all set out once, in the Sanctuary Plan.

Habitat protection centers on sanctuary lands and herbivore populations. No grazing permits should be issued on federal land, and existing permits should be revoked on day one. They should not be an honored commitment, and grazing should not displace protected herbivore habitat.

My view: Slaughterhouses and animal farming shouldn't exist in 2026, which follows my sentientist view. Animal agriculture should shut down immediately, with no transition. Lab-grown meat might not be able to replace the demand right away at the same cost, but it is the goal and the replacement, and it will scale up over time.

question"Doesn't this hurt ranchers?"

Yes, ranching should end. A ranch that earns most of its revenue from animals should convert fully to plant-based farming or cultivated-meat research, or be liquidated, and its owners should receive no compensation. What is left after creditors should go to the sanctuaries. Keeping every animal alive comes first. There is no retraining or job-placement program: ranchers and farm workers find new jobs or can apply for sanctuary jobs under the worker rules in the Sanctuary Plan. Public sanctuary land serves the herbivore populations it protects.

question"Animal agriculture is a huge industry. Won't ending it do serious economic damage?"

The industry is large. U.S. cash receipts from animals and animal products were a record of about $303.7 billion in 2025 and are forecast at $287.3 billion in 2026, more than half of all $540.3 billion in farm cash receipts, and the losses to producers, workers, and farm communities would be real. But economic value does not decide whether a practice violates rights. Britain did not weigh slavery's profitability against its wrong when it abolished slavery in 1833. It ended it, and the £20 million it raised, about 40% of government spending that year, went to compensating enslaved people's owners rather than to keeping the system. I draw the same line, with one difference: the industry gets no compensation, and what is left after creditors goes to the animals, in the sanctuaries. The precedent for that difference is American: Section 4 of the Fourteenth Amendment held every claim "for the loss or emancipation of any slave" "illegal and void" (Fifth Amendment question). Regulatory barriers to lab-grown meat and plant-based food are cleared so replacements scale up as animal products leave the market (Bioethics, Progress, & Technology). The full costs and funding are in Sanctuary Plan.

question"How big is the shock, and how long does it last?"

Large, but bounded, and I don't hide either part. About 558,000 people worked in animal slaughtering and processing in September 2026, and farms and aquaculture operations raising animals averaged about 271,000 payroll jobs in 2025, before counting owners and family who work their own farms. That is about 830,000 payroll jobs against roughly 160 million on U.S. nonfarm payrolls, but the loss is concentrated: USDA counts 354 farming-dependent counties, 329 of them rural, and just over half are in the Great Plains, where cattle, corn, soybeans, and wheat make up much of what is produced.

What the plan already does. Workers' unpaid wages and benefits are paid first from every liquidation. A farm family keeps its house and house lot. Landowners outside the industry are paid fair value for land the sanctuaries need. Former workers may apply for sanctuary care jobs under the worker rules, except anyone who killed animals for a living. There is no retraining program, and owners and shareholders get nothing (Sanctuary Plan).

Land. Grassland pasture and range covered about 659 million acres in 2017, 29% of U.S. land. Federal grazing permits end on day one, and that land is managed as herbivore habitat. Sanctuary land, 46 to 183 million acres across my cost cases, stays with the animals and becomes protected habitat once they no longer need it. Land a receiver sells, and pasture owned outside the industry, goes to any lawful use, including crops where the soil allows.

Food. The shortfall is in particular foods, not in calories, because the grain and soy now fed to animals are freed for people. Scarcity and higher prices for some foods should last only while supply chains, plants, and diets reorganize and replacements scale up. Nutrition is covered: the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics holds that planned plant-based diets are adequate at every stage of life, and food-safety rules ought to require B12 fortification of staple plant foods (Agriculture and Food Policy).

If this were a farming operation on the same scale with humans instead of animals, there would be no debate over shutting it down on day one. There would be no critique and no debate. It would close immediately. No one would ever ask, "What about the jobs?" or "What about the impact on the economy?" This is a human rights issue, the same as it should be a sentient rights issue. It closes immediately, without cost deciding whether it closes and with no wind-down.

question"Dairy, eggs, wool, and honey don't kill the animal. Are they banned too?"

Yes, as commercial animal agriculture. Once the food ban takes full effect, selling or possessing dairy, eggs, or honey should be banned as well, and sanctuary operators and adopters may not sell or use animal products (Sanctuary Plan). question"Prohibition failed. Won't a meat ban just create a black market?"

Some of it, yes. Alcohol Prohibition did not end drinking: Miron and Zwiebel estimate consumption fell to about 30% of its earlier level at first, then recovered to 60–70%. A meat ban will have smugglers and backyard slaughter too. Two differences matter. Prohibition banned an act between consenting adults, while every black-market slaughter kills a victim, which is why it is prosecuted as murder, graded by sentience, rather than as a vice offense. And raising an animal for slaughter takes space, feed, and time, so it is far easier to detect than a still. Even on Prohibition's own numbers, a ban cut consumption by a third or more, and each animal not killed is a life not taken.

Rationale

Killing sentient animals for food violates negative rights, so animal agriculture should be banned on the same sentientist ground as other deliberate killings outside self-defense (Animal Rights and Sentientism). The 2026 federal grazing fee is $1.69 per animal unit month (AUM) on about 18,000 Bureau of Land Management permits, while private grazing leases in 2025 ran from about $11 to $47.50 per AUM. About 12.9% of U.S. land and inland waters is protected and managed mainly for biodiversity, as cited in Regulation and Conservation. Congress can reach even home-raised animals under the Commerce Clause, as Gonzales v. Raich (2005) held for home-grown marijuana, and protecting negative rights is the government's first duty.

Sanctuary Plan

tldrIn short

I support a day-one shutdown of animal agriculture, wild-capture commercial fishing, aquaculture, commercial breeding mills, and animal testing - no wind-down. Every animal alive that day should be kept alive in sanctuary (then licensed nonprofit care) or adopted under strict vetting, with no breeding. Industry equity converts or is liquidated without shareholder compensation; workers' unpaid wages are paid first, and the proceeds fund care. Sanctuary bonds plus a flat Sanctuary Levy (never above 3%, income above $75,000 single / $150,000 joint) fund the run-down. The levy ends on the published population + repayment trigger. No compensation is owed because those firms forfeited their property protections by using their assets to violate animals' rights (Sanctuary forfeiture). On my middle case, care costs about $349 billion in the first year and $50 billion by year ten, as the animals die naturally, and the levy runs at 1.71% from 2027 to 2040; a single filer earning $150,000 pays about $1,280 a year (Costs, Levy).

Position

My plan is an immediate shutdown with no wind-down. On day one, all animal agriculture, wild-capture commercial fishing, aquaculture (including farmed crustaceans and, for consistency, farmed mollusks), commercial breeding mills and animal testing should stop. Nobody should breed, slaughter, catch or test on another sentient animal after that day. Experimentation on offenders who have forfeited their rights should continue under Animal Rights and Sentience Technology; it is not animal testing.

Keeping every animal alive comes first. No animal alive at the shutdown should be killed. Because no new animals are born, the sanctuary population and cost fall every year. Inland fish and crustacean farms should convert on site. Ocean farms should stay under the same keep-alive rule even where counts and costs are incomplete. Farmed mollusks, such as oysters, clams, mussels and abalone, should end with the rest of aquaculture for consistency, not because I claim they are sentient; I believe bivalves likely are not. The 2023 Census of Aquaculture counted 1,140 U.S. farms selling mollusks, with $575.5 million in sales, including 900 oyster farms, 373 clam farms and 30 mussel farms. My estimate of a year's throughput, not a headcount: those farms sold about 1.58 billion food-size mollusks, mostly oysters (about 553 million), hard clams (about 553 million), Manila clams (about 247 million) and mussels (about 201 million). Stock already in the water should be left unharvested and gear should be decommissioned. Numbers, gaps, and accepted risks: How many animals.

How care, adoption, state-to-nonprofit handover, firm conversion and liquidation, land, foreign firms, workers, enforcement, food-possession marks, funding, and the levy work is set out once in the subsections below — not repeated here. Shareholders and owners of animal-agriculture equity should get no compensation. Workers' unpaid wages should be paid first; a family on a liquidated farm should keep its house and house lot.

Forfeiture: The Sanctuary Levy, compelled conversion and liquidation, and taking industry property with no compensation to shareholders follow from forfeiture: animal-industry firms forfeited ordinary property protections by using their assets to violate animals' rights, as set out in Sanctuary forfeiture. The levy is time-limited and ends on the published condition. I do not treat this as a standing power to seize unrelated property.

question"Doesn't the Fifth Amendment require just compensation when the government takes property?"

Not when the law bans a harmful use. In Mugler v. Kansas (1887) the Supreme Court upheld Kansas's prohibition of alcohol without compensation to a brewer whose plant lost most of its value, holding that banning a use injurious to the public is not a taking. Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council (1992) still requires compensation for a total wipeout unless background principles of law already forbade the use. The law ought to declare, as part of the ban, that killing sentient animals for food is such a forbidden use. Courts may still order compensation for the land and buildings that pass to the sanctuaries without payment. The fair-value purchases from owners outside the industry already reduce that risk. The country has ended property in sentient beings without compensation before: Section 4 of the Fourteenth Amendment bars any government from paying "any claim for the loss or emancipation of any slave" and declares all such claims "illegal and void." Either way, the uncompensated transfers of industry assets rest on forfeiture, because those firms forfeited their property protections by using their assets to violate animals' rights, and the levy is a time-limited tax (Sanctuary forfeiture). Neither falls under ordinary eminent-domain practice.

question"A corporation is not a moral agent, and farming was legal before the ban. How can a firm forfeit anything?"

Two different things are going on, and neither is a punishment. First, no one is punished for what was legal before the ban; the ex post facto rule covers every farmer and shareholder (Ex post facto). Second, a company's assets answer for what was done with them, as they answer for its debts and torts (Corporations as Moral Entities). Shareholders lose the value of shares in a business whose use is banned, and their other property is untouched; a farm family keeps its house. That is the loss Mugler upheld for a brewery built in 1877, before Kansas adopted prohibition, worth $10,000 for brewing and no more than $2,500 after: "A prohibition simply upon the use of property for purposes that are declared, by valid legislation, to be injurious to the health, morals, or safety of the community, cannot, in any just sense, be deemed a taking or an appropriation of property for the public benefit." What forfeiture adds is why the owners have no claim on what remains: the assets were the instruments of the violation, so the proceeds go to the animals (Sanctuary forfeiture).

question"Won't the import bans break WTO rules?"

The WTO allows measures "necessary to protect public morals" under GATT Article XX(a). In EC–Seal Products (2014) the Appellate Body upheld the EU's ban on seal products on animal-welfare moral grounds while faulting how its exceptions were applied. A ban with no exceptions, applied the same to domestic and foreign products, as this one is, removes the flaw the EU's had. If a panel still ruled against it, the ban stands. The United States follows its own law (Global Institutions).

question"Is there a timeline, or only a day one?"

There is an order, and only the first step is a date. I believe the sequence should be written into the law, so no step in it can become a wind-down:

  • Day one. Animal agriculture, wild-capture commercial fishing, aquaculture, commercial breeding mills, and animal testing stop. Breeding stops, so the number of animals can only fall. Federal grazing permits and fishing and aquaculture licenses and quotas are revoked. Every animal alive that day is kept in state-run emergency sanctuary or adopted under strict vetting, and food from any animal killed after that day is banned (Enforcement).
  • First month. Animal-derived food made before the shutdown may still be sold and held. After one month, selling or possessing any food from a killed or farmed animal is banned.
  • First three years. Firms convert, divest, or are liquidated, and the proceeds arrive over these years (Liquidation, conversion and land). Bonds carry the early peak in care costs. I expect most sanctuary sites to pass from the state to licensed nonprofit operators within one to three years (Handover).
  • Until the last animal dies. The Sanctuary Levy pays for care, with unannounced welfare checks for each animal's life, and it ends on its published population and repayment trigger. Land the animals no longer need becomes protected habitat.

What is not yet timed is the build-out behind day one: the staff, feed, and sites for the emergency phase (Needs research: emergency-phase staffing and logistics). The shutdown does not wait on it.

question"Aren't the unintended consequences of an immediate ban and a predation program too risky?"

I believe the burden of proof falls on the status quo, not on ending it. Today's system is not a safe baseline. It is the largest ongoing rights violation on earth. In 2024 about 78.5 billion chickens and 1.49 billion pigs were slaughtered worldwide. Farmed fish killed for food numbered an estimated 78–171 billion in 2019, and wild fish caught an estimated 1.1–2.2 trillion a year on average over 2000–2019. Each year of delay is another year of that toll, and with fish counted it runs into the trillions.

Nature is not the gentle alternative either. Wild birds alone number about 50 billion. Because most species reproduce by having many offspring, the overwhelming majority of wild animals die shortly after they come into existence, mostly by starving or being eaten alive (Horta; Ng). Across billions of animals, that is the normal course of life in the wild, and today no one is obliged to stop it.

I would not accept a human atrocity on this scale continuing until every consequence of ending it had been modeled, and I don't accept it here. When the harm of acting is uncertain and the harm of not acting is certain and immense, uncertainty is a reason to act carefully, not a reason to wait. Acting carefully is built in:

  • Numbers only fall. Breeding stops on day one, and every animal alive that day is kept alive (Sanctuary Plan).
  • Predation is staged. Herbivore fertility control comes first, predator removal starts in pilot areas, and herbivore condition, disease, scavengers, and vegetation are monitored and reported every year (Hunting, Fishing, Pets, and Zoos).
  • The stop rule. No step may cause more suffering or more rights violations than it prevents. A step that breaks it is halted, and reversed where it can be (Wild-Animal Predation).

What I reject is the reverse burden: that those ending the killing must prove a perfect outcome in advance, while the killing itself needs no defense.

Workers

There should be no retraining or job-placement program. Workers should find new jobs, or apply for sanctuary jobs under strict rules. I would be very worried about people who killed animals for a living moving straight into caring for them, so:

  • Anyone who killed animals for a living should be barred from hands-on animal care at any sanctuary and from adopting. That covers slaughter and kill-floor workers, commercial fishing crews, fish-farm harvest staff, and lab staff who killed test animals.
  • Other former animal-agriculture workers may take care roles only after vetting at least as strict as an adopter's: a background check, a check of the animal-cruelty and animal-harm records, interviews and references, and a supervised probation period. Their work should then be checked by unannounced welfare inspections of the animals in their care, and any violation should mean removal.
  • Workers who never handled animals, such as drivers, office staff and maintenance workers, may take sanctuary jobs that involve no animal care.

Enforcement

Inspections, the import bans on animal products and seafood, and the sentience-graded penalties in Criminal Justice & Security should apply to anyone who keeps operating after the shutdown. No one should be prosecuted for farming, fishing, slaughter or testing before the shutdown; criminal penalties reach only acts after it (Animal Rights and Sentientism). A buyer who knowingly commissions or pays for the killing of an animal after the shutdown is an accessory to that killing, graded by the animal's sentience like the killer. Food from an animal killed after the shutdown should be banned from day one. Other animal-derived food made before the shutdown, including meat, fish, dairy, eggs, and honey, may still be sold and held for one month after the shutdown begins. From then on, selling or possessing any food from a killed animal or from a farmed animal should be banned, whenever it was made; that covers dairy, eggs, and honey, not only meat and fish. Each sale, possession, or eating of banned food should be one mark, and the product forfeited. Sanctuary hens' eggs stay with the hens, and nothing from a sanctuary is sold. The mark does not replace the accessory rule: a buyer who paid for a killing after the shutdown answers for that killing as well. What enforcement will cost in inspectors, customs checks, and prosecutions has no estimate yet (Needs research: inspection and customs staffing and cost).

Adoption and vetting

Farm animals could be adopted by people who would kill them for meat at home, so every adoption should be vetted first. Breeding-mill dogs, cats and other companion animals should go through the same steps. Each step should be passed before an animal leaves a sanctuary:

  1. Background check. Every adult in the household would be checked against the national records of animal-harm marks, convictions and earlier adoption violations. Any animal-harm mark or conviction would disqualify the household, and so would any adult barred from animal care under Workers.
  2. Home and facility inspection. An inspector would check the space, shelter, fencing, heating, water and access to a veterinarian against the needs of that species, before approval. The inspection would set how many animals the home may take.
  3. Signed no-slaughter commitment. The adopter would sign a commitment never to kill, sell, breed or use the animal for food or products. Euthanasia should be allowed only under the veterinary rule above.
  4. Sterilization before release. Every adopted animal would be sterilized before it leaves the sanctuary. An animal for which sterilization is medically unsafe would be placed only in a home that keeps it apart from animals it could breed with, and the inspection would check for that.
  5. ID and registry. Every adopted animal should carry a permanent ID and be entered in a national sanctuary registry, which would record its guardian and where it lives. A move or a transfer to another guardian should go through the registry and the same vetting. A death should be reported to the registry with a veterinarian's certificate.
  6. Unannounced welfare checks. Inspectors would visit without notice, first within the opening months and then at least once a year for the animal's life.
  7. Return rule. A guardian would be able to return an animal to a state sanctuary at any time, with no penalty. An animal would be removed and returned if a welfare check fails, the commitment is broken, or the guardian can no longer care for it.
  8. No sale, no stipend. Adoption should never be a purchase. No price should be paid for an adopted animal, no adopted animal should be sold, and the adopter should pay for its feed, housing and veterinary care.

This rule is for sanctuary adoption after the shutdown, when the animal is already safe. It is not a bar on paying to get an animal out of slaughter while the industry still operates (Rescue Purchases).

Killing an adopted animal would be treated under the existing murder rules in Murder, Rape, and the Mandatory Death Penalty and the cruelty rules in Animal Cruelty Law, graded by the animal's sentience like any other killing of a sentient animal. An unreported death would be investigated as a possible killing.

Handover to Nonprofit Operators

I am skeptical of state-run sanctuaries. The state should run them at first only because a rights violation this atrocious, egregious and monumental was happening nationwide, so every sanctuary starts as a place of emergency. Once the emergency phase is over, the sanctuaries would pass to private nonprofit operators while the animals are still alive. How much government stays involved would follow one test: whatever is in the best interest of the victims, the animals.

  1. Emergency phase. The state should run every sanctuary itself at first. The emergency phase should end site by site: each site should pass to a licensed operator once that operator passes accreditation, and the state should keep running any site that has none. I expect most sites to transfer within one to three years.
  2. Sanctuary license. Only nonprofits should be allowed to hold a sanctuary license. The license should set the care standards, sterilization, the adoption vetting above and unannounced inspections. A licensed operator should not be allowed to sell animals or animal products and should not breed any animal, and it should hire care staff only under the rules in Workers.
  3. Site-by-site handover. Each site would pass to a licensed operator by contract. The state should pay the operator a fixed amount per animal per year from the Sanctuary Levy. The payment should stay steady and should not be cut as donations come in, because a shortfall in donations would fall on the animals. Operators may take donations on top. An operator that fails should lose the site, back to the state or to another licensed operator.
  4. Two layers of oversight. An independent accreditation body should accredit every operator, as the Global Federation of Animal Sanctuaries does for sanctuaries today, and state inspectors should check every site as well. When animals are at risk, the state should step in.
  5. Land. Operators should get the right to use the land, never ownership, for as long as any animals remain on it. Sanctuary land should not be sold. Once the animals no longer need it, it should become protected habitat, and conservation trusts may hold it.
  6. The end. When the levy ends, the Sanctuary Endowment should pay the remaining operators until the last animal dies. After that, the sanctuaries should become ordinary private charities under ordinary animal-welfare law. An operator that carries on as a private charity once its land becomes habitat needs land of its own. If the endowment ever runs short, the general-revenue backstop in The Sanctuary Levy still applies.

Contracting out work that private organizations can do is my general rule for government (Public-Sector Size and Civil Service). The per-animal payment is payment to a contractor for a service, the care the state would otherwise provide itself, not a subsidy. It is the same money the state would spend caring for each animal, so the handover does not change the levy.

Liquidation, conversion and land

A liquidated firm should be sold off by a court-appointed receiver. The money should be paid out in this order: the receiver's costs, workers' unpaid wages and benefits, secured lenders against their collateral, taxes, and other creditors. Whatever is left, which would have gone to shareholders, should go to the sanctuaries instead. A firm that converts should keep the assets it uses in its new business and should sell the assets that only served animals, and that money should also go to the sanctuaries after the lenders secured on those assets are paid. The animals themselves should not be part of any estate; they should go to sanctuary, and neither should a farm family's house and house lot. Lenders should get no special protection: a loan the sale cannot cover is a loss, as in any failed business. A lender that earns most of its revenue from animal-agriculture loans should itself be a covered firm, and every other lender should divest the line by making no new loans to it.

The value left after creditors comes mostly from ranch and farm land, not from the meatpackers. Once the ban is in force, slaughter plants, poultry houses and brands built on animal products are worth little, and most large processors would owe more than their assets bring. The Sanctuary Levy

The Sanctuary Levy is a single flat rate on income above its own line: $75,000 for single filers and heads of household, $150,000 for married couples filing jointly. It phases in over the next $10,000 ($20,000 joint), as the flat tax does, so there is no cliff. Grandfathered Social Security benefits are not counted. Anyone at or below the line pays nothing. On IRS data for 2023 grown to 2026, that base is about $6.49 trillion a year.

Liquidation proceeds and sanctuary bonds would cover the early years, so there would be no tax spike. The levy would be held level for its whole run, which would keep the rate as low as possible. It would be recalculated every year and may only be lowered. It would never be above 3%. Once the bonds are repaid, it would fall to the rate that covers that year's care alone. It would be legally dedicated to sanctuary care, bond repayment and the closing endowment, and to nothing else.

When it ends. The end date is not fixed. The levy should end when the national sanctuary population falls below 1% of its starting animal units and the sanctuary bonds are repaid. The population is counted in animal units rather than heads, using the head counts that define a large animal feeding operation under EPA rules: 1,000 cattle, 2,500 pigs, 10,000 sheep, 55,000 turkeys, 82,000 laying hens and 125,000 meat chickens each count as 1,000 units. So a cow counts as 1, a pig 0.4, a sheep or goat 0.1, a turkey about 0.018, a laying hen about 0.012 and a meat chicken 0.008. Counting this way means a few million short-lived birds cannot keep the levy alive, and the threshold tracks what the animals actually cost to keep. The sanctuaries start with about 139 million animal units, so the threshold is about 1.4 million. On my middle case it is crossed around year 14, when the animals still alive cost about $2.5 billion a year.

The 3% cap. On the low and middle cases the cap never binds. On the high case, repaying the bonds by the 2044 threshold would need about 4.3%, so the levy stays at 3% past the threshold until the bonds are repaid, about 11 more years. The cap holds; the term stretches.

The last animals. In its final year the levy would fund a Sanctuary Endowment equal to five years of care at the threshold level. The endowment would be held in Treasury securities and would pay for the last animals until the final one dies. Anything left over would then go to the debt. If it ever runs short, the remaining care would come from general revenue, as an ordinary cost of the sanctuaries. When the levy outlasts the animals, as on the high case, it would pay their care directly and no endowment would be needed.

The bonds. On all three cases the bonds would be repaid in full by the levy. Sanctuary bonds would sit outside the debt brake's path while they are repaid on schedule (Fiscal Policy).

question"Why not let the industry wind down over ten years?"

Because every year of wind-down means more animals bred and killed. With an immediate shutdown, no new animals are born. The cost falls every year as the animals alive today live out their lives.

question"Doesn't taxing people and taking companies without compensation violate property rights?"

The levy is a time-limited tax to fund sanctuary care, and I accept that cost. Taking industry assets without compensation does not break the property-rights part of my negative-rights view: animal-industry firms forfeited those protections by using their assets to violate animals' rights. That forfeiture is stated in Property Rights and Civil Asset Forfeiture. The general rule still stands for everything else. Shareholders of firms built on a rights violation would get no compensation. What their firms are worth after creditors would go to the animals those firms held. No compensation is also the choice that spares people who did nothing wrong. Paying owners would mean charging taxpayers, most of whom never ran or profited from the industry, or shrinking what goes to the animals, which moves the cost of the wrong off the people who profited and onto people who did not; that is the trade Britain made in 1833 (Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat). The people hurt by the change through no choice of their own come first instead: workers' unpaid wages and benefits are paid before any lender, and a farm family keeps its house (Liquidation, conversion and land). Shareholders, including savers who hold these firms through funds, lose that value, as any investor in a business built on violating rights should expect.

question"Why are landowners outside the industry paid?"

A landlord who rented pasture to a feedlot, or a crop farm that kept a few cattle, did not run the industry and is not a covered firm. The sanctuaries would buy what they need from them at fair value for a practical reason, to reduce backlash, legal challenges and delay so the land is secured quickly, not because the payment is owed.

question"What stops someone adopting an animal and killing it?"

The vetting above: a background check, a home inspection, a signed no-slaughter commitment, sterilization, a registry ID, and unannounced welfare checks for the animal's whole life. No one should be able to buy an animal. Killing one is murder or cruelty under the rules I set out.

question"What happens to the workers?"

There should be no retraining or job-placement program. Workers should find new jobs, or apply for sanctuary jobs under the rules in Workers. Anyone who killed animals for a living should be barred from animal care, and other former animal-agriculture workers should be hired for care only after strict vetting.

question"Does this end experimentation on offenders?"

No. "End all testing" means all animal testing. Experimentation on offenders who have forfeited their rights should continue under Animal Rights and Sentience Technology.

Rationale

Breeding should stop on day one, so the number of animals can only fall. The cost is highest in the first year and shrinks as animals die of natural causes. In the middle case the cost per animal is about $1,800 a year for a cow, $920 for a pig and $30–35 for a chicken. The industry's own remaining value pays first, through liquidation and conversion proceeds. Bonds absorb the early peak, so the levy stays level at about 1.71% of income above the levy line on my middle case, and the 3% cap holds even on the high case. Setting the levy line at $75,000 single and $150,000 joint keeps the cost off low- and middle-income households. Ending the levy at a population threshold, with an endowment for the tail, keeps people from paying a broad tax for a few remaining animals. The middle case leaves farmed crustaceans, ocean-farm sanctuary costs, and medical-supply replacement out; those are accepted risks above, not hidden in the levy math.

Agriculture and Food Policy

tldrIn short

I want no farm subsidies without opt-in funding, and food safety held to health standards. Lab-grown meat is the goal and the replacement for animal agriculture: it may not meet demand at the same cost right away, it scales up over time, and regulation should not block it.

Position

Farming should receive no taxpayer subsidies without opt-in funding, consistent with the rule for every industry: no taxpayer money goes to farm, grazing, fishing or fish-farming businesses except through taxpayer opt-in, like any subsidy. Food safety should be regulated to consumer and public health standards, and food-safety rules ought to require vitamin B12 fortification of staple plant foods. Lab-grown meat is the goal and the replacement for animal agriculture, which should shut down immediately (Sanctuary Plan). It may not meet demand at the same cost right away; it scales up over time, and regulation does not block it from competing.

question"Won't farmers fail without subsidies?"

Farms compete like other businesses. Opt-in funding remains available for those who choose to support agriculture, and consumers are protected by safety standards rather than by subsidies.

question"What if lab-grown meat cannot meet demand when the ban starts?"

At first it may not. Cultivated meat has not yet been made at industrial scale at the price of conventional meat, and investment in it fell in 2024 (Good Food Institute – 2024 State of the Industry: Cultivated meat, seafood, and ingredients). The FDA itself says manufacturers are generally still "working on scaling up their processes to consistently produce amounts large enough to be competitively priced," and it completed the first U.S. pre-market consultation only in November 2022. The ban does not wait for it, because it rests on the animals' rights, not on a substitute being ready (Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat). Plant-based food is already sold at scale, the regulatory barriers to both are cleared, and lab-grown supply scales up over time as private innovation brings its cost down.

  • U.S. Food and Drug Administration – Human Food Made with Cultured Animal Cells
question"Won't an overnight ban leave the country short of food?"

Not of calories. Feeding crops to animals wastes most of them: worldwide, 36% of crop calories go to animal feed, and only 12% of those feed calories reach people as meat, milk, and eggs. Ending animal agriculture frees that grain, soy, and land for people. The change will not be seamless. Supply chains, processing plants, and diets built around animal products will not reorganize overnight, and some foods will be scarce or expensive before they adjust. Prices of meat substitutes and some plant proteins will rise at first, and households will feel it. The ban still does not wait, because it rests on the animals' rights, and the government ought to clear every regulatory barrier to plant-based and cultivated food so supply follows quickly.

  • Cassidy et al., Environmental Research Letters – Redefining agricultural yields: from tonnes to people nourished per hectare (2013)
question"Can everyone, including children and pregnant women, eat healthily without animal products?"

Yes, with planning. The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics holds that appropriately planned vegetarian diets, including vegan diets, are healthful and nutritionally adequate at every stage of life, including pregnancy, lactation, infancy, childhood, and older age. Critics point out that vitamin B12 has to come from fortified foods or supplements, and that iron, calcium, iodine, omega-3s, and vitamin D need attention, most of all for infants, pregnant women, and the elderly. That is why food-safety rules ought to require B12 fortification of staple plant foods, so no one has to plan it alone.

Commercial Fishing and Aquaculture

tldrIn short

Commercial fishing and fish farming of sentient animals should be banned outright and shut down immediately, with no wind-down, under the Sanctuary Plan. Farmed fish alive at the shutdown are kept alive and given sanctuary. Farmed crustaceans are kept alive the same way. Farmed mollusks end with the rest of aquaculture for consistency, not as a sentience claim. Licenses and quotas are revoked on day one. Firms that earn most of their revenue from fishing or aquaculture convert fully to cultivated seafood or plant-based alternatives, or are liquidated, with no compensation to shareholders. The ban covers shellfish, bycatch, and seafood imports. In my view the best available science decides which fish are sentient, cultivated seafood is the replacement, and fish and the oceans fall within the future reordering of nature against predation.

Position

Commercial fishing and aquaculture of sentient animals should be banned outright and shut down immediately. Catching or farming sentient animals for food is a rights violation, so the ban should not be phased in, and there should be no wind-down.

Fish should follow the same sentience gradient as the murder rules in Criminal Justice & Security: killing higher-sentience fish should be on the capital track, and killing lower-sentience fish should be punished and should count as a mark against the offender.

noteHow this gets done: the Sanctuary Plan

There is no wind-down. Like land-based animal farming in Agriculture, Grazing, and Habitat, wild-capture commercial fishing and aquaculture should shut down immediately. No fishing operations, vessels, fish farms, or hatcheries should operate after day one, and existing fishing licenses, quotas, and aquaculture licenses should be revoked. They are not an honored commitment, because catching or farming sentient animals is a rights violation. Every farmed fish alive at the shutdown is kept alive and given sanctuary, with no breeding or stocking. Farmed crustaceans are kept alive the same way. Farmed mollusks end too, for consistency, not as a sentience claim: those already in the water are left unharvested, and the farms are decommissioned. Every domestic company, vessel owner, fish farm, supplier or lender that earns most of its revenue from fishing or aquaculture converts fully to cultivated-seafood research or plant-based alternatives, or is liquidated. Others divest only that line. Shareholders get no compensation. After workers and creditors are paid, the proceeds go to the sanctuaries. Foreign firms may not operate in the United States or do business with US-held companies. There is no retraining or job-placement program. Workers find new jobs or can apply for sanctuary jobs under the worker rules in the Sanctuary Plan. Inspections, the seafood import ban, bycatch bans, and the sentience-graded penalties in Criminal Justice & Security apply to anyone who keeps operating. The full plan is in Sanctuary Plan.

Which fish are sentient follows the best available science. Crabs, lobsters, crayfish, and other decapod crustaceans sit in the high class, and shrimp are placed species by species, like fish (sentience classes). Bivalves, such as oysters and mussels, are also covered by the ban for consistency, not as a sentience claim; I believe they are likely not sentient, so they sit in no class (Sentience Determination). Bycatch ends with the fishing ban. Non-fishing marine activity, such as shipping, dredging, and offshore energy, should use the least-harmful method that works, such as speed limits and routing around whales, under the same rule as on land (Sentience Determination). Failing to use it should be punished, and unavoidable incidental deaths carry no criminal liability. Wild oysters and mussels keep filtering water when no one harvests them, so the ban costs those waters nothing; only the farms end. Imports of wild-caught or farmed seafood should be banned. Subsidies follow the opt-in rule in Agriculture and Food Policy. The long-term replacement is cultivated seafood produced through private innovation. In the bioengineering future of predation abolishment and the reordering of nature against predation and suffering, fish and the oceans are classified within that framework.

question"Are fish even sentient?"

Sentience is determined by the best available scientific consensus, as for any being (Ethical and Philosophical Framework). Species that the best available science identifies as sentient are protected.

Rationale

Animal agriculture and aquaculture should be abolished because they kill sentient beings that hold negative rights; farmed and wild-caught sentient fish fall under the same rule. Because every catch is a killing of a sentient being, the ban is the position. Subsidies follow the opt-in rule set out in Subsidies and Mandates. I treat predation as a rights violation (Wild-Animal Predation), and the oceans are included in the future reordering of nature against predation and suffering. The scale is significant: in 2024, U.S. commercial fishermen landed 7.7 billion pounds of seafood valued at $4.9 billion, and U.S. aquaculture produced an estimated 561 million pounds of farmed seafood valued at $1.3 billion.
